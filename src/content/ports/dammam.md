## Direct answer

King Abdulaziz Port in Dammam is Saudi Arabia's Gulf-facing container gateway and, on 2025 volumes, its largest container port. Two SGP terminals offer a combined 3.8 million TEU of annual capacity, 16 m of draft at Terminal 2 and up to seven daily trains to [Riyadh Dry Port](/ports/riyadh-dry-port/). Expect 18–26 days from China, then 3–7 days of clearance with a complete SABER file. The constraint is not water depth. It is the Hormuz approach, the rail slot and the product certificate.

Use Dammam for Riyadh, the Eastern Province and the Gulf corridor. Do not use it as a substitute for [Jeddah](/ports/jeddah/) when your customer is in Makkah, Madinah or the Red Sea hinterland. The two ports look interchangeable on a Saudi map and behave nothing alike in a disrupted week. *(Editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

**7 October 2026 — SGP launched a dedicated terminal operating system at King Abdulaziz Port.** The system gives planners a single view of cargo movements, equipment allocation and delay risk. The direction matters more than the detail: SGP is investing in visibility at the same time as it adds quay. *(Reported fact)*

**August 2026 — Saudi ports handled 750,634 TEU, up 9.5% year on year.** Transshipment rose 14.7% to 189,407 TEU, and vessel calls rose 13.16% to 1,118. The headline tonnage fell on liquid bulk, not containers. For Dammam, the signal is that feeder connectivity is improving even while the broader cargo picture looks soft. *(Reported fact, Mawani data via Arab News)*

**April 2026 — SAR launched five new logistics corridors and extended free storage at Riyadh Dry Port from 7 to 30 days.** The corridors link eastern ports with western ports, northern borders and regional markets. The storage extension changes the economics of inland clearance: a container can now sit at Riyadh for a month without storage charges, which makes the rail option more forgiving for importers with slow customs files. *(Reported fact)*

**April 2026 — SGP took over the Riyadh Dry Port Ecosystem expansion.** The agreement with SAR targets 1.5 million containers a year by 2030, more than double the current handling capacity. SGP already operated the dry port; the new concession adds a modernisation programme, new equipment and a unified digital platform. For Dammam shippers, the same operator now controls the sea terminal, the rail link and the inland node. That reduces handovers on paper. It also concentrates operational risk in one company. *(Reported fact)*

**2025 — Dammam became Saudi Arabia's largest container port.** Chinese trade press, citing Saudi port data, put Dammam at about 2.17 million TEU in 2025, up 9.7%, ahead of Jeddah. SGP's own network, including Jubail, handled more than 4.5 million TEU in 2025, and the Dammam terminals passed 15 million TEU cumulative since 2015. The ranking is a milestone, not a permanent status: Jeddah's Red Sea recovery could reverse it in 2027. *(Reported fact)*

**November 2025 — the T2 expansion opened and the Dammam Integrated Logistics Zone broke ground.** The berth expansion added 225 m to 700 m of quay, enabling two mega vessels to berth simultaneously. The logistics zone covers 1 million m² and represents an investment of up to SAR 1.3 billion (about US$346 million). Both are capacity bets on the Eastern Province, not on current demand. *(Reported fact)*

**2026 — the Hormuz factor moved from risk to routing assumption.** Jeddah absorbed Gulf-bound cargo when the strait was constrained, and [Sohar](/ports/sohar/) marketed itself as the only deepwater box gate outside Hormuz. Dammam, inside the strait, could not make that claim. Its schedule became a function of convoy windows, war-risk pricing and carrier appetite rather than berth productivity. That is the single most important thing to understand about Dammam in 2026. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Why it matters: the Gulf-facing gate

Dammam does a different job from Jeddah. Jeddah is a Red Sea port serving western Saudi and the holy cities, with a land bridge to Riyadh. Dammam is a Gulf port serving the Eastern Province, Riyadh and the industrial corridor that runs from Jubail to Ras Al Khair. It is also the closest Saudi container gateway to Bahrain, Qatar and Kuwait by road, although transit cargo needs a Saudi bond and a clear onward plan.

The hinterland is the point. The Eastern Province produces a large share of Saudi Arabia's oil, petrochemicals and industrial output, and its import demand is weighted toward machinery, project cargo, chemicals, food and consumer goods. Riyadh, about 400 km inland, is the largest consumption market in the country and the base for a growing e-commerce and distribution sector. A port that can reach Riyadh by rail in half a day has a structural advantage over one that requires a 900 km truck move from the Red Sea.

That advantage has a ceiling. Dammam sits inside the Strait of Hormuz. When the strait is open, it is the cheapest and fastest route to Riyadh. When it is constrained, the same geography becomes a liability: war-risk premiums rise, carriers skip calls, and cargo that would have discharged at Dammam is routed through Jeddah or Sohar and moved overland. The port's 2026 story is therefore not a simple growth story. It is a story about a gateway whose value depends on a chokepoint it does not control.

The 3.8 million TEU headline is a statement about what Dammam can handle, not what it is handling. In a disrupted year, capacity is useful only if the ships can reach it.

## Two terminals, one operator

SGP operates both container terminals under a long-term concession. That is unusual in the Gulf, where competing operators often run adjacent terminals, and it changes how you should think about terminal choice.

**Terminal 1** is the larger site: 138 ha, 1,680 m of berth, 14 m of draft, about 2.08 million TEU of capacity, 11 quay cranes and 35 rubber-tyred gantries. It handles the bulk of the port's gateway volumes and has the deeper hinterland connections by road. Its limitation is draft: 14 m is enough for most Gulf services but not for the largest fully laden callers.

**Terminal 2** is smaller at 64 ha but deeper at 16 m, with 925 m of berth, nine quay cranes and 30 RTGs. The 2025 expansion added 225 m to the existing 700 m of quay, which allows two mega vessels to berth at the same time. That is a berth-window improvement, not a landside improvement. If your container is on a service that calls T2, you get deeper water and a newer yard; if it calls T1, you get scale and a shorter road leg to some industrial customers.

Both terminals share the same customs regime, the same Fasah system and the same road network. The differences that matter are draft, berth window, free time and the specific carrier's proforma. A quote that says "Dammam" without a terminal is a range, not an offer.

The TOS launch in October 2026 is worth watching for a different reason. A single operating system across both terminals should make equipment allocation and delay prediction more consistent. If it works, the gap between T1 and T2 narrows. If it does not, the terminal with the newer system becomes the safer choice for time-sensitive cargo. *(Editorial judgment)*

## The rail link is the real advantage

Dammam's most underrated asset is not a crane. It is the on-dock rail connection to Riyadh Dry Port, operated with Saudi Arabia Railways. SGP advertises up to seven daily services between the port and the dry port. The journey takes about 4–6 hours, depending on pathing and loading.

That changes the maths for a Riyadh importer. A truck move from Dammam to Riyadh is about 400 km and typically takes 4–5 hours on the highway, but it is subject to driver availability, axle-weight limits, checkpoint delays and the cost of a return leg. Rail is less flexible but more predictable for full containers, and it can be cheaper per FEU when volumes are steady. The catch is that rail requires a slot, a bond for inland movement and a terminal that can release the box to the rail operator. Without those three, the train is not an option.

The 30-day free storage at Riyadh Dry Port, introduced in April 2026, makes the rail route more forgiving. A container can arrive at Riyadh, sit in the bonded yard while the importer assembles documents, and still avoid storage charges for a month. That is a meaningful buffer for SABER delays, particularly for regulated products that need a product certificate before a shipment certificate can issue.

But free storage is not free clearance. Customs duty, VAT, inspection and demurrage at the sea terminal continue to run on their own clocks. The 30-day window is a buffer against inland storage, not against the cost of leaving a container at Dammam. If the box is not cleared at the sea port, the carrier's free time expires and the daily charges start before the rail move ever happens.

The rail option also has a capacity limit. Seven daily services is a lot for a single corridor, but it is still a fixed number. In a peak week, the slot you want may not exist, and the fallback is a truck. That is why experienced importers book the rail slot at the same time as the ocean booking, not after discharge.

## Clearance at Dammam: the sequence that decides your week

Dammam clearance follows the Saudi national sequence. The port is not the variable; the documents are.

1. **Pre-arrival file.** Invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, insurance and SABER documents are lodged through Fasah before the vessel arrives. The invoice and packing list must agree line by line. A mismatch is the most common reason a green-channel shipment turns yellow.

2. **SABER validation.** For regulated products, a product certificate (PC) must exist before a shipment certificate (SC) can issue. The PC is the long pole. It cannot be created at the port, and it cannot be retrofitted to a container that is already on the water. If you are shipping electronics, machinery, toys, cosmetics or food-contact products, start the PC process before the booking.

3. **Duty and VAT assessment.** Duty is assessed on CIF value at the tariff line; VAT is 15% on the duty-inclusive value. A valuation query stops the clock. So does a classification dispute. The importer of record carries the liability, which is why aggressive DDP valuation eventually damages the customer relationship.

4. **Inspection.** Green channel releases on documents. Yellow and red channels require scanner or physical inspection. Food, cosmetics, chemicals and some electronics attract lab testing, which adds days and a sampling cost.

5. **Payment and delivery order.** Destination charges are settled, usually through the carrier or its agent. No payment, no gate pass. This is the step where a cheap ocean rate can be undone by a long list of destination charges.

6. **Rail or truck release.** A released container still needs a rail slot or a truck appointment. At Dammam, the gate and the rail ramp are separate queues. A container can be customs-clear and still sit because the rail operator has no slot.

The [FCL guide](/guides/fcl/) covers the container-level decisions; the [Saudi Arabia hub](/countries/saudi-arabia/) covers the national compliance picture. The port page is about the handoffs that happen after the vessel berths.

## How to act: six decisions before you book

**1. Decide whether Dammam is the right gate at all.** If your consignee is in Riyadh or the Eastern Province, Dammam is the default. If the consignee is in Jeddah, Makkah, Madinah or the southern Red Sea, use [Jeddah](/ports/jeddah/). The land bridge is a contingency, not a cheap alternative.

**2. Price the strait, not just the port.** Ask the carrier for the war-risk and congestion surcharge separately, and ask what happens if the strait closes mid-voyage. A Dammam quote that assumes an open Hormuz is not a quote for November 2026.

**3. Choose the terminal by draft and service, not by habit.** If your carrier calls T2, you get 16 m and a newer berth. If it calls T1, you get scale. Confirm the terminal, the free days and the storage tariff in writing.

**4. Start SABER before the container sails.** The product certificate is the long pole. A shipment certificate issued after arrival is a week late even if the file is perfect. For regulated categories, use a Saudi-registered importer of record who already holds the PC.

**5. Book the rail slot with the ocean booking.** The Dammam–Riyadh train is the port's best feature and its most finite one. If you need the box in Riyadh in a specific week, reserve the slot before discharge. If you cannot get one, plan a truck and price the difference.

**6. Treat the 30-day free storage as a buffer, not a plan.** It buys time for documents. It does not buy time for a missing certificate, a valuation query or a carrier that will not release the box. Clear the sea terminal first; the inland window is a second buffer.

## Cost and transit drivers

Dammam costs have four layers. The percentages below are editorial ranges for dense, low-value containerised cargo, not a rate card.

**Ocean and risk.** Base freight plus bunker adjustment plus war-risk and congestion surcharges. In a normal week, the ocean leg is the largest single line, often 50–65% of a door-to-door cost for low-value goods. In a disrupted week, surcharges can add a double-digit percentage and change the ranking of routes. The driver is not distance; it is insurance and schedule reliability.

**Destination port charges.** Terminal handling, documentation, release, storage and demurrage. For a container that clears within free time, this is typically 10–20% of the door-to-door cost. For a container that misses free time, storage can exceed the ocean freight on low-value cargo. Dammam's terminal free time is typically 5–7 days; the carrier's free time is a separate contract.

**Inland movement.** Rail to Riyadh is usually cheaper per FEU than truck for full containers, but it requires a slot and a bond. Truck is faster door-to-door and more flexible for multiple delivery points. For a single Riyadh warehouse, rail plus a short truck leg is often the lower-cost option; for a Jeddah or Dammam city delivery, truck wins.

**Clearance and compliance.** Customs brokerage, SABER certificates, inspection, lab testing and translation. This layer is small in a clean file and large in a broken one. A missing PC can add more cost than the ocean freight saved by shopping around.

**Transit ranges.** China–Dammam is 18–26 days direct and 22–30 days via transshipment *(industry claim)*. Berth to gate is 3–7 days with a complete file and 7–14 days with a SABER gap. Dammam–Riyadh rail is 4–6 hours; truck is 4–5 hours. A realistic China–Riyadh door-to-door range is 24–35 days, with the wide end driven by clearance rather than sailing.

## Risk and compliance

**Hormuz exposure.** Dammam is inside the strait. War-risk premiums, convoy windows and carrier omissions are not tail risks in 2026; they are ordinary planning variables. A Dammam routing needs a tested alternative via Jeddah or Sohar, not a assumption that the strait stays open.

**SABER timing.** A product certificate cannot be created at the port. If you ship a regulated product without a PC, the container will sit while you assemble one. That is a compliance delay, not a freight delay, and it is the most expensive kind.

**Valuation and classification.** Duty is assessed on CIF, and VAT is 15% on the duty-inclusive value. Reclassification can raise both retroactively. The importer of record carries the liability. Do not let a forwarder's DDP quote hide a valuation position you cannot defend.

**Dangerous goods.** Lithium batteries, chemicals and aerosols require carrier acceptance, UN38.3 documentation and terminal DG rules. Dammam is stricter than many Asian ports on paperwork, and DG cargo is a poor fit for a tight clearance window.

**Overweight and axle limits.** Saudi road limits are enforced. An overweight container can be denied a gate pass or require a permit, and the rail option has its own weight limits. Check the gross weight before booking, not at the weighbridge.

**Transit cargo.** If the container is moving onward to Bahrain, Qatar or Kuwait, the bill of lading needs a transit clause, the notify party and payer must be Saudi-based, and the bond must be in place before discharge. The rules tightened in 2026; confirm the current requirement in writing.

**Concentration risk.** SGP operates both container terminals and the Riyadh Dry Port Ecosystem. That is convenient for coordination and uncomfortable for contingency. If the operator has a system outage or a labour disruption, both the sea and inland nodes are affected. Keep a truck fallback.

## Our read

**Dammam is the right default for Riyadh and the Eastern Province, and the wrong default for western Saudi.** The rail link is the differentiator. A port that can put a box in Riyadh in half a day, with 30 days of free storage at the inland end, is structurally cheaper than a Red Sea routing for the centre of the country.

**The 3.8 million TEU headline is capacity, not demand.** The T2 expansion and the logistics zone are bets on 2027–2030, not evidence of a 2026 boom. Read the monthly Mawani numbers, not the press release.

**The strait is the swing factor.** Dammam's productivity is good enough that the port is rarely the bottleneck. The bottleneck is getting the ship there. In a disrupted month, the best Dammam strategy is a dual-gate strategy: book Dammam for the base case, keep a Jeddah or Sohar fallback warm, and price the land bridge before you need it.

**Do not use Dammam as a cheap Jeddah.** The land bridge adds cost and days, and it is justified only when Hormuz risk or Jeddah congestion is worse. For a Makkah or Madinah consignee, Jeddah wins on every dimension except a temporary disruption.

**The rail slot is the new free time.** In 2026, the scarce resource is not yard space; it is a confirmed rail allocation and a clean SABER file. Importers who book both before the vessel sails are the ones who avoid the storage invoice. *(Editorial judgment)*

## FAQ

**How many container terminals does Dammam have?**
Two, both operated by Saudi Global Ports: Terminal 1 (14 m draft, about 2.08m TEU) and Terminal 2 (16 m draft, about 1.77m TEU). Combined capacity is about 3.8m TEU after the 2025 T2 expansion.

**How long does clearance take at Dammam?**
With a complete file, 3–7 days from discharge to gate. With a SABER gap, a valuation query or a lab test, 7–14 days. Plan for the longer end and treat a fast release as upside.

**Is Dammam congested in November 2026?**
The port has berth capacity and a second deep-water terminal; the constraint is usually the strait, the rail slot or the document file rather than the quay. Check the [update feed](/updates/) before a peak booking.

**Can I rail cargo from Dammam to Riyadh?**
Yes. SGP and SAR run up to seven daily services between the port and Riyadh Dry Port, about 4–6 hours. You need a rail slot, an inland bond and a terminal release. The same operator runs the sea terminal and the dry port, which simplifies coordination but concentrates risk.

**How long is free storage at Riyadh Dry Port?**
Thirty days for various goods and transit containers, extended from seven days in April 2026. The free window applies at the dry port; terminal free time at Dammam and carrier demurrage are separate.

**Does Dammam handle lithium batteries and dangerous goods?**
Yes, subject to carrier acceptance, UN38.3 documentation and terminal DG rules. Expect a stricter document check and a slower inspection. DG cargo is a poor fit for a tight clearance window.

**Is Dammam better than Jeddah for Riyadh?**
For most Riyadh cargo, yes. Dammam is about 400 km from Riyadh with a direct rail link; Jeddah is about 950 km with a land bridge that depends on rail slots and truck capacity. The exception is a period when Hormuz is closed and Dammam is unreachable; then Jeddah is the fallback.
