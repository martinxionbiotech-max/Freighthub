## Direct answer

Salalah is the container port that kept working when the Gulf did not. It sits on Oman's Arabian Sea coast, about 15 km south of Salalah city in Dhofar, roughly 500 km southwest of the Strait of Hormuz and well north of Bab el-Mandeb. A vessel calling Salalah does not need to transit either chokepoint, and the port is almost on the main Asia–Europe lane rather than at the end of a detour. That geography turned a competent regional terminal into the western Indian Ocean's relief valve in 2026.

The operating numbers are large. The container terminal has a 2.4 km quay, seven berths with drafts up to 18 m and a design capacity of 6.5 million TEU after a US$300 million upgrade completed in early 2025. It handled 4.325 million TEU in 2025, then 2.03 million TEU of transshipment in the first half of 2026 alone. August 2026 set a monthly record at 491,114 TEU; September beat it at 511,209 TEU. The port ranked third globally in the World Bank and S&P Global 2025 Container Port Performance Index. ([Oman hub](/countries/oman/) has the national picture; this page is about using the terminal.)

The number that matters for a shipper is smaller. In the first half of 2026, Salalah handled 77,148 import TEU against 2.03 million TEU of transshipment. The port is a carrier network node first and an Omani gateway second. Use it for relay cargo, for Dhofar, and as a contingency outside Hormuz. Do not use it for a Muscat consumer launch and expect a short truck leg: the road is about 1,000 km. *(Editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

**2 November 2026 — the concession was ended, not renewed.** Salalah Port Services confirmed that Oman's Ministry of Transport, Communications and Information Technology had formally notified it that the existing concession for operating facilities at Salalah will not be extended beyond its contractual expiry on 1 November 2028. The ministry described the decision as "final and unconditional". No successor operator or transition process has been announced. *(Reported fact)*

**October 2026 — APM Terminals said it wants to stay.** APM Terminals, which holds 30% of SPS and has operated the container terminal since it opened in 1998, told WorldCargo News that it plans to remain a central party to the future concession and operational management of the terminal. That is a statement of intent, not a contract. The Omani government has not said how the next operator will be chosen. *(Reported fact)*

**September 2026 — a second consecutive monthly record.** Salalah handled 511,209 TEU in September, 4.1% above August's 491,114 TEU. The two months together passed one million TEU, equivalent to about 23% of the port's entire 2025 volume. The port attributed the surge to network changes caused by the Hormuz closure and the Red Sea disruption. *(Reported fact)*

**August 2026 — the first-half picture was already exceptional.** Transshipment reached 2,033,734 TEU in January–June, with 77,148 import TEU and 106,586 restow TEU. The port's full-year 2025 volume was 4.325 million TEU, itself a record and about 34% above 2024. *(Reported fact)*

**2025 — the US$300 million upgrade was completed.** The project lifted design capacity from 4.5 million to 6.5 million TEU, added yard and berth infrastructure and brought new container-handling equipment. It is the reason Salalah could absorb the 2026 surge without the congestion that would otherwise have followed. *(Reported fact)*

**October 2026 — Salalah Free Zone's project pipeline reached US$3.3 billion.** The zone reported 106 active industrial tenants and service providers, with projects across petrochemicals, food processing, minerals and logistics. The free zone is a long-term industrial play; it is not the reason the container terminal is full. *(Reported fact)*

**2026 — the "last port standing" narrative hardened.** When Hormuz closed at the end of February and Houthi attacks resumed at Bab el-Mandeb, Salalah was the only major transshipment hub in the region outside both risk zones. Carriers rerouted, volumes jumped, and the port's efficiency ranking became a commercial asset rather than a plaque. *(Reported fact plus editorial judgment)*

## Why it matters: the port outside both chokepoints

The Strait of Hormuz is the Gulf's single point of failure. Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia's eastern ports and the UAE's Gulf coast all depend on it. The Bab el-Mandeb is the Red Sea's. In 2026 both were disrupted at once, which is why the phrase "outside Hormuz" became a freight selling point.

Salalah is outside both. It sits on the Arabian Sea, roughly 500 km southwest of Hormuz, with the Indian Ocean in front of it and the Dhofar mountains behind it. A container ship on the Asia–Europe lane passes close by; the deviation is measured in hours, not days. From Salalah, a relay vessel can reach the Indian subcontinent, East Africa, the Red Sea and the Gulf without retracing its route through a war zone.

That is a genuine strategic asset. It is also a narrow one. Salalah's efficiency is built for transshipment: large vessels discharge, smaller vessels lift, and the boxes continue to another port. The terminal's productivity numbers are excellent precisely because the boxes do not have to clear customs, enter a domestic market or travel far inland. A gateway port has a harder job.

The gateway job is small. Dhofar is a sparsely populated governorate; Salalah city is its commercial centre, and the surrounding economy is built on fishing, agriculture, tourism, logistics and the free zone. The entire country's container import market is a fraction of the port's throughput. In the first half of 2026, imports were 77,148 TEU against 2.03 million TEU of transshipment. That ratio is the single most important fact about Salalah.

For a Chinese exporter, the practical consequence is simple. If your cargo is destined for Muscat or the northern Omani market, Salalah is usually the wrong discharge port. Sohar is 200 km from Muscat and about 170 km from Dubai; Salalah is about 1,000 km from Muscat. A Salalah discharge for a Muscat delivery adds a long truck leg, a second handling operation and several days. If your cargo is destined for Dhofar, Salalah is the natural gateway. If your cargo is transshipment, Salalah is the hub.

## Inside Salalah: 2.4 km of quay and a 6.5 million TEU design

Salalah's container terminal is a purpose-built relay facility. The quay runs 2.4 km, with seven berths and drafts up to 18 m. That is deep enough for the largest container ships afloat, and the berth lineup is designed to work several of them at once. The terminal has the yard, cranes and reach-stackers to move boxes from deep-sea vessel to feeder without touching the Omani customs system.

The US$300 million upgrade is the reason the terminal could scale. It lifted design capacity from 4.5 million to 6.5 million TEU, expanded the yard and brought new equipment. The physical footprint did not change much; the productivity did. Salalah's ranking — third globally in the 2025 Container Port Performance Index — reflects vessel time in port, not just quay length. For a carrier, that is the number that matters: a faster turnaround releases the ship for the next leg.

The terminal is multi-purpose. Salalah also handles general cargo, bulk, liquid bulk and cruise vessels, with a general cargo terminal and liquid infrastructure alongside the box terminal. The bulk side matters for Omani exports — limestone, gypsum, cement clinker and other minerals move through the port — and for project cargo that cannot be containerised. A shipper who thinks of Salalah only as a box hub misses half the port.

The operator is Salalah Port Services, a listed Omani company. APM Terminals holds 30%; the Omani government holds 20%, government pension funds 23% and other shareholders 27%. APM Terminals has managed the container terminal since 1998 under a build-operate-transfer concession. That structure gave Salalah a global operator, a listed local vehicle and a long-term mandate. It also means the port's commercial logic has been shaped by a carrier-affiliated operator for nearly three decades.

The weakness is concentration. One container terminal, one operator, one channel. There is no second box facility at Salalah to absorb a crane failure, a yard blockage or a labour dispute. The port's efficiency is a competitive advantage in normal times and a single point of failure in bad ones. That is not a reason to avoid Salalah; it is a reason to know what you are buying.

## The transshipment machine and its narrow gateway

Salalah's business is relay. A mainline vessel from Asia discharges at Salalah; a feeder takes the boxes to Jeddah, Djibouti, Karachi, Nhava Sheva, Colombo, Muscat or the Gulf. The port earns handling revenue on both moves. The Omani economy earns almost nothing on the box itself, because the box is not entering Oman.

This model has three advantages. First, it is high-volume and high-productivity: the terminal can handle large ships quickly, which is what carriers pay for. Second, it is resilient to Omani demand shocks: a recession in Muscat does not empty the Salalah yard. Third, it is strategically valuable: a relay hub outside Hormuz is an insurance policy for the whole region.

It has three vulnerabilities. First, it depends on carrier network decisions. If Maersk, MSC or CMA CGM redesigns a rotation, Salalah's volume can move within a season. The 2026 surge was created by a network crisis; it can be reversed by a network normalisation. Second, it depends on feeder connectivity. A relay hub with no feeder to the destination is a parking lot. Third, it depends on the operator. A concession transition can freeze investment and unsettle carriers even if the physical terminal keeps working.

The gateway business is small but real. Omani importers in Dhofar use Salalah for consumer goods, food, building materials and project cargo. The free zone generates some container traffic of its own. The port also handles Omani exports, especially minerals and agricultural products. But the gateway is a rounding error against the relay volume, and it is not growing fast enough to change the ratio.

For a Chinese seller, the distinction is contract-level, not semantic. A transshipment booking moves under a through bill of lading; the box does not enter Oman, and Omani duty and VAT do not apply at Salalah. An import booking requires a Bayan declaration, an Omani importer of record, duty and VAT, and conformity evidence for regulated products. If a forwarder quotes "Salalah" without saying which one, the quote is incomplete.

## The 2028 concession cliff

The most important date in Salalah's future is 1 November 2028. That is when the current concession expires, and Oman has decided not to extend it. The ministry's notification was explicit: the decision is final and unconditional. The government has not named a successor, published a tender timetable or explained how the transition will work.

The risks are predictable. A terminal operator facing handover has less incentive to invest in the final two years. Carriers dislike uncertainty and may shift rotations to a competing hub — Colombo, Jebel Ali, Duqm, or a new facility. Staff may leave. Systems may change. The terminal can remain physically open while becoming commercially uncertain. That is the scenario that matters to a shipper, not the legal form of the concession.

There is a counter-case. Salalah is too valuable to strand. The Omani government has a strong interest in keeping the hub working, and APM Terminals has said it wants to remain central to the future concession. A managed transition, with the operator continuing under a new contract or a joint venture, is more likely than a chaotic handover. Oman has also shown, at Sohar and Duqm, that it can run port transitions without shutting the gate.

Our judgment: the 2028 transition is more likely orderly than chaotic, but "more likely" is not a guarantee. The practical response is not to avoid Salalah. It is to avoid building a 2029–2031 supply chain that assumes the current service pattern will survive unchanged. Ask the carrier what its post-2028 plan is. Ask the terminal what capex it has committed for 2027. Ask your forwarder what contingency it has if the relay moves. If the answers are vague, that is the answer. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Salalah Free Zone: the landside hedge

Salalah Free Zone was established in 2006 by royal decree. It offers 100% foreign ownership, no customs duties on imports and exports, no minimum capital requirement, a 30-year exemption from taxes on profits and dividends, no personal income tax and free repatriation of capital and profits. It also offers one-stop-shop licensing and ready infrastructure.

The zone has 106 active industrial tenants and service providers, with a project pipeline reported at US$3.3 billion in October 2026. The sectors are what you would expect from Dhofar: petrochemicals, minerals, food processing, logistics and light manufacturing. Salalah Methanol is one of the anchor industrial investors; the port's liquid infrastructure exists partly to serve that cluster.

For a Chinese manufacturer, the free zone is a way to add value before the box moves. Assembly, packaging, labelling, light processing and quality control can be done in the zone, and the finished goods can be exported to the GCC, Africa, the Indian subcontinent or further afield. The zone's US and Singapore free trade agreements are a genuine advantage for the right product, but the origin rules are strict: a warehouse or a repacking operation does not automatically confer origin. The value added has to be real.

The free zone is not a substitute for the Omani market. It is a production and logistics platform with a small domestic hinterland. A Chinese company that sets up in Salalah to sell to Oman will be disappointed by the addressable population. A company that sets up to serve East Africa, the Indian Ocean or the US under an FTA has a more credible case. The difference is the onward logistics, not the tax holiday.

## How to act: seven decisions before you book

**1. Decide whether Salalah is a relay or a destination.** If the cargo is transshipment, the port is a hub and the through B/L governs. If the cargo is for Oman, the port is a gateway and the 1,000 km road to Muscat is part of your cost. These are different products, and a quote that does not distinguish them is not a quote.

**2. Name the terminal and the berth.** "Salalah" can mean the container terminal, the general cargo terminal or the liquid berths. They have different drafts, equipment and tariffs. For project cargo, ask for the berth and the heavy-lift capacity, not just the port name.

**3. Get the carrier's post-2028 plan.** The concession ends on 1 November 2028. If your routing depends on a specific Salalah relay, ask the carrier whether it will keep the service under a new operator. A vague answer is a risk you should price.

**4. Put the importer of record and Bayan access in place before the first booking.** Bayan is a company system: the Omani importer needs a commercial registration, a PKI token and an authorised user, and the declaration runs on 12-digit HS codes. A Chinese seller quoting DDP cannot be the importer of record without an Omani entity.

**5. Open the conformity file at the sample stage.** Regulated products need a CoC or a type-examination certificate under the Omani Conformity Scheme, valid for one year. Electricals and appliances are the centre of gravity. A certificate that arrives after the vessel sails is a warehousing cost.

**6. Keep free zone and domestic inventories in separate accounts.** Goods entering Salalah Free Zone are not duty-paid in the same way as goods entering Oman. When they leave the zone into Oman, duty and VAT apply; when they are re-exported, the paperwork has to prove it. A commingled stock is a customs dispute.

**7. Build a weather and congestion buffer.** The southwest monsoon can bring swell and rain between June and September, and a volume surge can tighten yard space and berth windows. For project cargo and reefer shipments, add days rather than assuming a perfect turnaround.

## Cost and transit drivers

**The ocean leg is short; the land leg is long.** Salalah is on the Asia–Europe lane, so direct calls from China are commonly 14–19 days port to port *(industry claim)*. A relay through Colombo, Singapore or Port Klang adds 3–7 days. The road to Muscat is about 1,000 km, which is a day and a half of driving plus a border-free domestic haul. For a Muscat delivery, Sohar is usually cheaper door to door even if the ocean rate to Salalah looks lower.

**Transshipment tariffs are not gateway tariffs.** A relay box is handled twice at Salalah but does not clear customs, and the terminal's transshipment tariff reflects that. An import box needs a declaration, a customs inspection, duty and VAT, and often a conformity check. Comparing a transshipment rate with a gateway rate is comparing two different services.

**The port's efficiency is a cost advantage, not a price guarantee.** Fast berth turnaround lowers the carrier's vessel cost, which supports competitive ocean rates. It does not automatically lower the terminal handling charge or the inland haulage. Ask for the THC, the storage free time and the road rate separately.

**War-risk insurance is cheaper than a Gulf transit, not free.** Salalah avoids the Hormuz leg, which is the most expensive part of a Gulf war-risk quote. But the port is still in a region at war, and the Red Sea route has its own attacks. Hull and cargo premiums reflect the whole voyage, not just the port.

**Duty and VAT are simple; the financing is not.** Five percent duty on CIF and 5% VAT on the duty-paid value give an effective rate of about 5.25%. A registered importer recovers the VAT later, so the cost is cash flow rather than a permanent charge. On a high-value box, that cash sits for months.

**The free zone changes the cost base, not the market size.** A 30-year tax holiday and duty-free imports are real advantages for a production or re-export operation. They do not create Omani consumers. Model the onward logistics before you model the tax saving.

## Risk and compliance: where Salalah shipments break

**A port statistic read as a market size.** The 511,209 TEU monthly record describes boxes handled, not boxes consumed in Oman. Most of the volume is relay cargo. A brand that builds inventory against the port number will overstock a small market. Fix: ask for the gateway-versus-transshipment split before you set a sales forecast.

**The "Salalah is in Oman, so it is an Omani port" shortcut.** Salalah is in Oman, but most of its boxes are not. A transshipment booking does not create an Omani import; an import booking does. Fix: decide the customs status of the cargo before you book, and name the importer of record.

**The 2028 handover that nobody priced.** A 2029 budget that assumes the current Salalah service pattern is a bet on a concession transition that has not been designed yet. Fix: ask the carrier and the terminal for their post-2028 plans, and build a contingency through Sohar or Jebel Ali.

**The certificate that missed the boat.** A regulated appliance lands without a CoC because the importer assumed it could be arranged after arrival. Fix: make the certificate a booking prerequisite and put its expiry date in the launch calendar.

**The free zone exit with a thin paper trail.** Cargo leaves the zone for a third country, but the file cannot prove re-export. The fallback is duty on the full value. Fix: keep the entry declaration, the re-export declaration and the transport document together.

**The monsoon schedule.** June to September can bring swell and rain to Dhofar. Project cargo and reefer shipments are the most exposed. Fix: add weather days and confirm the berth's operating limits for your vessel.

**The single-terminal concentration.** One container terminal, one operator, one channel. A crane failure or a labour dispute has no alternative at Salalah. Fix: keep a contingency routing and price it before you need it.

## Our read: use the hub, don't mistake it for a market

**Do** treat Salalah as the western Indian Ocean's best relay hub outside Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb. For a carrier or a forwarder designing a contingency, it is the most credible alternative to a Gulf hub when the strait is closed. The efficiency ranking, the 6.5 million TEU capacity and the 2026 performance are real.

**Do** use Salalah for Dhofar, for Omani exports and for free zone cargo. The port is the natural gateway for southern Oman, and the free zone is a credible production and logistics platform for the right investor. The mistake is to use it for Muscat.

**Do** put the 2028 concession on your risk register. It is the biggest governance change in Omani ports this decade. A managed transition is likely; a cost-free transition is not. Contract the risk rather than assuming it away.

**Avoid** extrapolating the 2026 record into a trend. The surge was created by a chokepoint crisis, and chokepoint crises reverse. Salalah's long-run volume depends on carrier networks, not on Omani demand. A 2027 forecast that assumes 500,000 TEU every month is a forecast of a war that may not continue.

**Avoid** treating Salalah Free Zone as a tax haven with a port attached. The incentives are real, but the zone is an industrial platform with a small domestic market. The business case has to include the onward logistics, the origin rules and the customer, not just the tax holiday.

**Avoid** quoting a Salalah rate without the land leg. The ocean rate is the easy part. The 1,000 km road to Muscat, the customs entry, the conformity file and the VAT cash flow are what decide the landed cost. A quote that stops at the port gate is not a delivered price.

## FAQ

**Is Salalah Port outside the Strait of Hormuz?**
Yes. Salalah is on Oman's Arabian Sea coast, about 500 km southwest of Hormuz and well north of Bab el-Mandeb. A vessel calling Salalah does not transit either chokepoint.

**How long does sea freight from China to Salalah take?**
Commonly 14–19 days direct on the Asia–Europe lane, port to port, and 20–28 days with a relay. Add clearance and the road leg. The ranges are industry claims, not a sailing schedule.

**Is Salalah a transshipment port?**
Yes, predominantly. About 90–95% of container volume is transshipment. In the first half of 2026, imports were 77,148 TEU against 2.03 million TEU of transshipment.

**Can I use Salalah as a gateway to Muscat?**
You can, but it is usually the wrong choice. The road to Muscat is about 1,000 km. Sohar is about 200 km from Muscat and is the better gateway for the northern market.

**What is Salalah's container capacity?**
About 6.5 million TEU a year after the US$300 million upgrade completed in early 2025. The previous design capacity was 4.5 million TEU.

**Who operates Salalah's container terminal?**
Salalah Port Services, a listed Omani company in which APM Terminals holds 30%. The Omani government holds 20%, government pension funds 23% and other shareholders 27%.

**What happens to the concession in 2028?**
It expires on 1 November 2028. Oman's Ministry of Transport has notified SPS that it will not be extended, and described the decision as final and unconditional. No successor operator has been announced. APM Terminals says it plans to remain central to the future concession.

**What are the import duties and taxes in Oman?**
Five percent duty on CIF for most goods, and 5% VAT on the duty-paid value, for an effective combined rate of about 5.25%. Excise applies to tobacco, alcohol, energy drinks, carbonated drinks and pork.

**Do I need a certificate of conformity for Salalah imports?**
Yes, for regulated products. The Omani Conformity Scheme requires a CoC or a type-examination certificate, valid for one year. Electricals and appliances are the core scope.

**Is Salalah Free Zone tax-free?**
It offers a 30-year exemption from taxes on profits and dividends, 100% foreign ownership, no customs duties on imports and exports, and no personal income tax. The incentives are real, but the zone is an industrial platform, not a consumer market.
