## Direct answer

Shuaiba is Kuwait's industrial port, not a second consumer gateway. It has 20 berths and 4,068 metres of quay, but only three container berths, no general warehouse and no shore cranes. Cargo is worked direct: ship to truck, truck to ship, with the quay as the only buffer. In 2024 the port handled 282,870 TEU, 23.7 million tonnes of general cargo and 2.7 million tonnes of clinker across 2,908 vessel calls. ([Kuwait hub](/countries/kuwait/) has the national picture; this page is about using the terminal.)

That combination makes Shuaiba excellent for factory inputs, project cargo, cement, chemicals and heavy equipment, and wrong for a retailer's seasonal stock. The port's reputation for "no congestion" was built on general cargo; in 2026 the aggregate average waiting time reported across ship types was 5.4 days, and the March drone strike on a US military site about a mile from the cargo piers repriced the security risk. Book Shuaiba when the consignee has a factory, a crane and a truck. Book [Shuwaikh](/ports/shuwaikh/) when the consignee has a shop. *(Editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

**15 December 2025 — AD Ports signed an MoU with the Kuwait Ports Authority.** The agreement covers the exploration, development and operation of container facilities at Shuaiba. AD Ports is to prepare technical, environmental and financial feasibility studies. It is an MoU, not a concession, and it does not change who works your vessel this quarter. *(Reported fact)*

**15 April 2026 — the KPA floated a Shuaiba port infrastructure tender.** MEED reported the tender in April; by 23 July, firms had submitted bids. This is the physical upgrade track — quays, yards, services — running in parallel with the commercial track. Construction at a working industrial port means disruption before capacity. *(Reported fact)*

**1 March 2026 — a drone struck a US military site at Port Shuaiba.** Six US Army Reserve soldiers were killed at a tactical operations centre about a mile from the civilian cargo piers. The site sat between oil storage tanks, a refinery complex and a power plant. The civilian port is a separate facility and kept operating, but the attack changed how insurers, carriers and crews price a Kuwait call. *(Reported fact)*

**27 March 2026 — Shuwaikh was hit.** A hostile drone attack damaged facilities at Kuwait's main commercial port. No casualties were reported, and the port kept working. Two attacks in four weeks on Kuwait's port system is now the baseline that war-risk underwriters use, not a one-off. *(Reported fact)*

**September 2026 — Iran warned it would strike tankers at Kuwaiti and Bahraini ports.** Iranian media carried a warning telling crews to evacuate vessels. Shuaiba's oil pier and the adjacent industrial jetties are tanker infrastructure, so the warning matters even to container and breakbulk operators: a tanker incident can close the harbour entrance, suspend pilotage and stop truck gates. *(Reported fact)*

**October–November 2026 — the KPA appointed KPMG to lead a strategy to 2035.** The mandate covers the three commercial ports, including Shuaiba. A strategy is not a dredger. The useful signal is that the KPA is preparing a new investment case, which usually precedes a new tariff schedule and a new concession round. *(Reported fact)*

**Through 2026 — waiting times rose.** ShipNext's port data puts the average waiting time across ship types at 5.4 days in 2026, with wide variation by vessel class. Shuaiba has historically worked general cargo with one to two days of berthing delay. The aggregate number is worse because the mix includes tankers and bulkers waiting for berths and because war-risk routing bunched arrivals. Treat 5.4 days as a planning ceiling, not a promise. *(Industry data)*

## Why it matters: a direct-delivery port has no shock absorber

Most ports sell storage. Shuaiba sells throughput. The port describes itself as a direct-delivery port: berth allocation follows first come, first served, subject to document readiness by the cargo receiver. There is no general warehouse to park a problem in. The 318,000 m² yard belongs to the container terminal, not to general cargo.

The port's customers are the industrial tenants behind the fence: petrochemical plants, a power station, cement works and fish-processing plants, not a consumer distribution market. The old Shuaiba refinery has closed; the industrial complex around it has not. That is why the cargo mix is heavy, dirty and valuable to the factory next door, and why a retailer's carton of seasonal goods is a poor fit for the berth.

That single design choice drives everything a shipper experiences.

**The truck is part of the port.** If the consignee's truck is not at the gate when the cargo is discharged, the cargo sits on the quay or on the ship. Quay rent and demurrage begin immediately. A forwarder who quotes "port to door" without naming the haulier has quoted half a service.

**The crane is part of the port.** Shuaiba has no shore cranes. General cargo and project lifts are worked with mobile cranes hired locally, with local operators and local rigging. That is workable, and it is not instant. A 60-tonne lift that would be routine at a container terminal becomes a crane hire, a lift plan and a permit at Shuaiba.

**The berth is not guaranteed.** Berths 15, 16 and 17 are container berths and get priority. Berth 18 is dedicated to military cargo. General cargo can use 15 and 18 only when no container or military vessel needs them, and a general cargo vessel can be shifted to the anchorage if one arrives. The shifting cost sits on the principal's account. That clause is the most expensive sentence in the port's handbook for a breakbulk operator.

**The channel is not the constraint.** At 10–14 metres at most berths and a listed maximum of 15.8 metres, Shuaiba takes more draft than Shuwaikh's 8.5-metre channel. The constraint is landside: trucks, cranes, clearance and storage. This is the opposite of Shuwaikh, where the tide sets the schedule. At Shuaiba, the consignee sets it.

## The berth map: three for boxes, one for the military

Shuaiba's 20 berths are not interchangeable, and the differences are not cosmetic.

| Berth group | What it handles | What it means for a booking |
|---|---|---|
| 15, 16, 17 | Container terminal | Priority for box ships; the only practical container window |
| 18 | Military cargo | Dedicated; civilian use only when free |
| 15 and 18 | General cargo when no container or military vessel is due | You can be shifted, at your cost |
| Other berths | Industrial, bulk, liquid and support berths | Berth-specific draft, equipment and approvals apply |
| 200 m nominal length | Longer vessels accepted at a few berths | Extra berthing dues and more delay |

The practical consequence is that Shuaiba is two ports in one. The container terminal is a small, priority-protected box operation. The rest is an industrial quay where the berth, the crane and the truck are all arranged around a factory's production schedule. A forwarder who treats the port as a generic "Kuwait port" will misprice both.

## What the AD Ports MoU does and does not change

The AD Ports agreement is the most important commercial signal at Shuaiba since the 1980s, and it is also the most over-read.

**What it is.** A non-binding MoU, signed in Kuwait City on 15 December 2025, to explore the development and operation of container operations at Shuaiba. AD Ports is preparing feasibility studies covering technical, environmental and financial questions. The KPA remains the landlord, the regulator and the incumbent operator.

**What it is not.** A concession award. A tariff change. A terminal handover. A guarantee that AD Ports will operate the terminal. The feasibility work has to finish, the terms have to be agreed, and the infrastructure tender has to produce a quay and a yard that a new operator can use.

**Why it still matters.** Kuwait's container system is thin. Shuwaikh handled 613,093 TEU in its latest reported year; Shuaiba handled 282,870 TEU in 2024. Together they are smaller than many single terminals in the region. A credible international operator with box experience could lift productivity, but the port's physical limits — three container berths, a compact basin and a 50 km road leg — do not disappear with a management contract.

**What to do with the signal.** Do not build a 2026 or 2027 routing on the assumption of a new Shuaiba container terminal. Do watch for three milestones: the feasibility study being accepted, a concession or joint-venture announcement, and the first berth taken out of service for reconstruction. The first two are announcements; the third is the one that changes your schedule.

## How to act: six decisions before you book

**1. Decide whether Shuaiba is the right door.** If the cargo is going to a factory in Ahmadi or Shuaiba, a refinery contractor, a cement plant, a steel fabricator or a project site in the south, Shuaiba is usually the better port. If the cargo is going to a retailer, a distributor or an e-commerce warehouse in Kuwait City, Shuwaikh is usually cheaper door to door even when the ocean rate looks worse. The 50 km road leg and the crane hire are real money. *(Editorial judgment)*

**2. Contract the truck before the vessel sails.** Direct delivery rewards preparation. Name the haulier, the plate, the driver, the gate pass and the receiving hours. Confirm that the consignee can take the cargo on the discharge day, not "around then". If the consignee cannot, book a container yard outside the port and budget the extra move. Do not assume the port will store it.

**3. Book the crane with the cargo, not after it arrives.** There are no shore cranes. For breakbulk, heavy lifts and out-of-gauge cargo, get a mobile crane quote with capacity, radius, rigging and operator included. Confirm the lift plan and the port's approval before the vessel arrives. A crane that is "available" on the day is not a plan.

**4. Confirm the berth priority in writing.** If you are general cargo booked at 15 or 18, ask the agent what happens if a container or military vessel arrives. Get the shifting-cost allocation in the booking, not in a dispute later. If the cargo is time-critical, price the risk of a shift into the freight, not into optimism.

**5. Clear the industrial file before production.** KUCAS applies to regulated products: a Technical Evaluation Report at product level, a Technical Inspection Report at shipment level. Chemicals and industrial inputs may also need Environmental Public Authority or Public Authority for Industry approvals. Consumer goods that are not related to the industrial area can require prior KPA approval to berth at Shuaiba at all. Confirm the approval route before the factory starts, not before the vessel sails.

**6. Price the relay as the base case until a direct call is confirmed.** Kuwait's direct China calls are fewer than the UAE's. Many boxes discharge at Jebel Ali or Dammam and feed into Shuwaikh or Shuaiba. That is not a defect; it is the network. But the quote must name the hub, the feeder, the cut-off and the second set of destination charges. A "Kuwait rate" without a connection is an ocean rate, not a delivered rate.

## Cost and transit drivers

**The ocean leg is a range, not a number.** China–Kuwait direct calls commonly price above China–Jebel Ali because the vessel is smaller and the rotation is thinner. Relayed cargo can look cheaper per ocean mile and more expensive per delivered box once the feeder, the hub handling and the second documentation set are added. The only useful comparison is door to door, with the relay broken out. *(Editorial judgment)*

**War risk is a line item now.** By mid-2026, hull war-risk cover for a Gulf transit was being quoted at 3.5–7.5% of value, on industry estimates that Singapore's transport ministry cited, with carrier surcharges of US$1,500–4,000 per TEU on top. Those are ranges, not quotes, and they move with the security picture. A low-value, dense cargo such as clinker or cement absorbs the same per-box surcharge as a container of electronics, which is why bulk shippers plan around inventory and vessel timing rather than around rate recovery. *(Industry claim)*

**The port cost is driven by time, not by the tariff.** Shuaiba's published tariffs are not the issue. The issue is that a direct-delivery port has no free storage to absorb a missed truck. Quay rent, demurrage, crane standby and a shifted berth are the four costs that appear when the landside plan fails. A shipment that clears in two days can be cheaper at a higher headline rate than a shipment that clears in six days at a lower one.

**The inland leg is short but not trivial.** About 50 km to Kuwait City is an hour on a good day and longer in traffic or at a checkpoint. For Iraq-bound cargo, the Abdali crossing is a separate road leg, a separate customs regime and a separate contract. Do not let a Kuwaiti forwarder sell an "Iraq door" without naming the border broker and the Iraqi haulier.

**Cargo type decides the economics.** Clinker and cement are low-value, high-weight and freight-sensitive: a small change in the per-tonne rate changes the landed cost. Project cargo is the opposite: the freight is a small share of the project cost, and the schedule and the lift are the expensive parts. Container overflow sits in between and is usually the most price-sensitive to the road leg.

**Transit ranges.** China to Kuwait is commonly 20–28 days direct and 26–36 days relayed, port to port, before clearance and trucking. Add two to five days for KUCAS inspection if the product is regulated, and add the truck and crane days at Shuaiba. The ranges are industry claims, not a sailing schedule.

## Clearance at Shuaiba: KUCAS, EPA and the industrial file

Kuwait's import regime is simpler than most GCC markets on tax and more demanding on product conformity.

**Duty and VAT.** Duty is 5% ad valorem on CIF for most goods. Kuwait has no VAT; the 2026–2030 fiscal plan omitted it. That makes DDP arithmetic unusually clean: duty on the CIF value, no VAT recovery problem, no input-credit chain. It does not make Kuwait cheap. It makes it predictable.

**KUCAS.** The Kuwait Conformity Assurance Scheme applies to regulated products, with a Technical Evaluation Report (TER) at product level and a Technical Inspection Report (TIR) at shipment level. The TER is not a shipment certificate. A supplier who sends a TER and expects it to clear a container will lose a week. The regulated list is broad and includes many electrical and consumer products.

**Industrial approvals.** Shuaiba's reason for existing is the industrial area. Chemicals, solvents, industrial gases and some raw materials may need Environmental Public Authority approval; industrial inputs may need Public Authority for Industry involvement. The port itself can require prior approval for commercial cargo that is not related to the industrial area. These are not KUCAS items; they are separate files with separate timelines.

**The military berth.** Berth 18 is dedicated to military cargo. Civilian cargo can use it only when it is free. In a year with an active Iran conflict, military movements can take priority at short notice. A berth plan that assumes 18 is available is not a plan.

**Iraq transit.** Cargo destined for Iraq can move through Kuwait, but the Kuwaiti clearance is only the first customs regime. The Abdali crossing, the Iraqi declaration and the road haulier are separate. The documents that work for a Kuwaiti import do not automatically work for an Iraqi transit.

## Risk and compliance: the failure modes we see

**The truck that was "arranged".** A consignee says the truck is booked. The vessel discharges on a Thursday; the truck arrives on Sunday. The container sits on the quay for three days and the demurrage exceeds the ocean freight saving. Fix: name the haulier and the gate slot in the booking, and have a backup haulier.

**The crane that was not in the quote.** A breakbulk quote excludes crane hire because the forwarder assumes the port provides it. Shuaiba does not. The crane is hired locally, the lift plan is separate, and the vessel waits. Fix: make crane hire a line item with capacity and radius.

**The general cargo berth that became a container berth.** A breakbulk vessel is shifted to the anchorage when a container ship arrives. The shifting cost is on the principal's account and the schedule slips. Fix: confirm priority and cost allocation in writing.

**The TER that was treated as a TIR.** A regulated product arrives with a Technical Evaluation Report but no Technical Inspection Report. Customs holds the shipment. Fix: treat TER and TIR as two separate checkpoints, and book the inspection before the vessel arrives.

**The consumer cargo that needed approval.** A forwarder books consumer goods to Shuaiba because the rate is better than Shuwaikh. The port requires prior approval for commercial cargo not related to the industrial area, and the box is redirected. Fix: match the port to the cargo, not the rate.

**The war-risk exclusion.** A carrier's quote excludes war risk, or the insurer excludes Kuwait after an incident. The rate looks competitive until the surcharge is added. Fix: ask for the war-risk clause and the surcharge basis in writing, and stress-test the quote against a two-week closure.

**The 2024 statistic used as a 2026 fact.** The KPA's published Shuaiba figures are for 2024. They are the best official baseline available, and they are not a description of this month. Fix: use them for structure and scale, and use current port data for waiting times.

## Our read: an industrial port with a thin margin for error

**Do** use Shuaiba for what it was built for: factory inputs, clinker and cement, steel, chemicals, project cargo and heavy lifts. The port's berths, its industrial hinterland and its road access to southern Kuwait are a genuine advantage. A factory in Ahmadi does not need Shuwaikh.

**Do** treat the landside as the critical path. At Shuaiba, the ocean leg is often the easy part. The truck, the crane, the KUCAS inspection and the port approval are where launches fail. A good forwarder at Shuaiba is a landside operator, not a rate broker.

**Do** watch the AD Ports and KPA tracks separately. The MoU is about container operations; the tender is about infrastructure. A new operator without a rebuilt yard is a management change. A rebuilt yard without a new operator is concrete. The investable moment is when both land.

**Avoid** using Shuaiba as a consumer container gateway. Three container berths, a 50 km road leg and no general storage are structural disadvantages for retail distribution. Shuwaikh's two container berths are closer to the market and better connected to the city's trucking base. The right answer for consumer goods is usually Shuwaikh, even when Shuaiba quotes lower.

**Avoid** planning around a "no congestion" reputation. The port's general cargo operation has historically been quick. In 2026, the aggregate wait is 5.4 days, the security picture is volatile and the military berth can take priority. Price a buffer.

**Avoid** treating the March attack as a commercial closure. The civilian port kept working. The lasting effects are insurance, carrier appetite and crew willingness, not a physical shutdown. Plan for the commercial consequence, not the headline.

## FAQ

**Is Shuaiba a container port?**
Yes, but a small one. It has three container berths (15, 16 and 17) and handled 282,870 TEU in 2024. Shuwaikh is the larger container gateway with 613,093 TEU in its latest reported year.

**What is the draft at Shuaiba?**
Most berths work at 10–14 metres. The port's listed maximum draft is 15.8 metres, which applies only to the deepest berth and should be confirmed for the specific vessel and berth. It is deeper than Shuwaikh's 8.5-metre channel.

**Can I store cargo at Shuaiba?**
There is a 318,000 m² container yard, but no general warehouse. Shuaiba is a direct-delivery port: cargo is expected to move from ship to truck. If you need storage, arrange it outside the port before the vessel arrives.

**Do I need to hire a crane?**
Yes, for breakbulk and project cargo. Shuaiba has no shore cranes. Mobile cranes are hired locally, with a separate lift plan and operator. Confirm capacity and radius before booking.

**How far is Shuaiba from Kuwait City?**
About 50 km south, in Ahmadi Governorate. It is an hour by truck on a good day, longer in traffic or at checkpoints.

**Do I need KUCAS for Shuaiba?**
For regulated products, yes. KUCAS requires a Technical Evaluation Report at product level and a Technical Inspection Report at shipment level. Industrial chemicals may also need environmental or industry approvals.

**Is Shuaiba safe after the March 2026 attack?**
The civilian port kept operating after the drone strike on the US military site about a mile from the cargo piers. The practical risks are war-risk insurance, carrier appetite and sudden security restrictions, not a closed port. Check the current carrier and insurer position before booking.

**Can I use Shuaiba for Iraq-bound cargo?**
Yes, as a corridor. Kuwaiti clearance is only the first regime; the Abdali crossing, the Iraqi declaration and the road haulier are separate contracts. Name the border broker.
