## Direct answer

Shuwaikh is a tide port with a city attached. The channel is about 8 km long and maintained at 8.5 m; the port takes vessels drawing 7.5 m at zero tide and up to 9.5 m at high tide. That two-metre window, not the berth count, decides when your vessel arrives, when it works and when your container leaves the gate.

Twenty-one berths sounds generous until you count the container ones: two. The rest are general cargo, which is why a single crane outage can back up the whole box operation. Book Shuwaikh when the consignee is in Kuwait City and the cargo fits a standard box; route [Shuaiba](/ports/shuaiba/) for industrial, project and overflow cargo. *(Editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

**27 March 2026 — the port was attacked.** The Kuwait Ports Authority reported an overnight hostile drone attack on Shuwaikh: material damage to facilities, no casualties, emergency procedures activated. The port kept working, but the event changed how insurers and carriers price Kuwait. A drone strike on Port Shuaiba earlier in March killed US service members at a military installation about a mile from the civilian cargo piers. *(Reported fact)*

**FY2026–27 — a 17-project tender programme.** The KPA published its annual plan covering upgrades across the three commercial ports, alongside expansion works including the Shuwaikh berths development and a semi-automated container terminal on reclaimed land. Multi-year construction at a working city port means disruption before capacity. *(Reported fact)*

**July–September 2026 — Mubarak Al-Kabeer advanced.** Kuwait and China moved the US$4.1 billion Boubyan Island project forward: a first-phase container terminal of about 1,200 m and a long-run target above 8 million TEU. The financing is real; the timeline is not. *(Reported fact)*

**Late October 2026 — congestion moderate and rising.** Shuwaikh's public performance figures are published on a seven-day delay, and the trend into the fourth quarter was upward. That is the normal seasonal pattern in a port with two container berths — which means buffer days, not zero buffer. *(Reported fact)*

## Why it matters: a two-metre tide window sets the national schedule

Most ports publish a draft. Shuwaikh publishes a range, because the water moves. At zero tide the limit is 7.5 m; at high tide it is 9.5 m. The channel is 8 km of dredged approach inside Kuwait Bay, and a vessel that arrives outside its window either waits at the anchorage or sails light.

The consequences compound. **Arrivals bunch**, because ships time their approach to the tide, so a delayed vessel does not simply arrive late — it arrives in the next window, competing with ships already scheduled there. **Sailings bunch too**, and a vessel that finishes cargo after the tide turns waits half a day or more. **Bigger ships pay more**, because a 9.5 m vessel can only enter on the tide; that is why so much Kuwait-bound cargo arrives on a feeder from [Jebel Ali](/ports/jebel-ali/) or [Dammam](/ports/dammam/) rather than on a direct China call. And **pilotage has a hole** from roughly 0600–0900, so a vessel ready at 05:30 may still wait for the shift change.

*(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

The practical translation: Shuwaikh's schedule reliability is structurally lower than a deep-water port's, even when the terminal is working well. Build two to four days of arrival variance into any promise that depends on a Shuwaikh call, and more in the fourth quarter. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Twenty-one berths, two container berths

The berth count misleads in the other direction. Of 21 berths, two are container berths. The rest handle general cargo, project cargo, livestock, vehicles and the miscellaneous traffic of a port serving a city of roughly three million people.

Three effects follow. **Outages hit directly:** at a terminal with eight box berths, losing one is a 12% capacity cut; at Shuwaikh, losing one of two is a 50% cut to the container operation. **Yard competition:** a project cargo shipment that needs open storage can displace containers, and vice versa, because backup land is limited. **Reefer capacity is finite:** Kuwait imports most of its [food](/sectors/food/), and a reefer booking without a confirmed plug is a booking without a plan. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

The useful comparison is Shuaiba: about 20 berths at 10–14 m, four of them container berths, designed around petrochemicals, cement and project cargo. It lacks Shuwaikh's consumer-market proximity, and when Shuwaikh is full it absorbs the overflow — at the cost of a longer truck leg and a different clearance queue. *(Reported fact)*

## The city-port problem: trucks, not cranes

Shuwaikh sits inside the urban fabric. That is its commercial advantage — warehouse districts and the consumer market are minutes away — and its operational weakness. The gate opens onto roads shared with city traffic, and the empty-container return competes with commuters.

Academic work on the Shuwaikh port system has identified exactly this: road congestion around the port delays empty-container returns, and that delay feeds back into the terminal's ability to release import boxes. The port is not short of quay; it is short of road capacity at the hours that matter. *(Reported fact)*

For an importer: book the truck before the vessel arrives, plan the empty return as part of the delivery rather than after it, avoid the first and last hours of the working day, and keep the warehouse inside the Shuwaikh–Shuaiba corridor. The further inland the consignee, the more the last mile dominates landed cost. *(Editorial judgment)*

## How to act: five decisions before you book

**1. Confirm the vessel's arrival draft.** Not nominal capacity — the draft on the day. A 9.5 m vessel is a tide-dependent call; a 7.5 m vessel is not. If the carrier cannot state the arrival draft, the schedule is a guess. *(Industry claim)*

**2. Ask which berth, not just which port.** Two container berths means berth allocation is the schedule. A booking that says "Shuwaikh" without a berth number is a booking that can wait at anchor. *(Industry claim)*

**3. Price direct against relay.** Direct China–Shuwaikh is commonly 20–28 days; relayed via [Jebel Ali](/ports/jebel-ali/), Dammam or [Hamad](/ports/hamad/) is 26–36. The relay is cheaper on some lanes and worse on schedule. Check whether the feeder has a confirmed tide slot. If you are comparing container products, start with [FCL](/guides/fcl/) rather than assuming a groupage service will hold the booking. *(Industry claim)*

**4. Confirm the reefer plug and the free time.** Reefers are plug-limited, and free time at a two-berth terminal is short. Demurrage is the cost you control least — get free days in writing. *(Editorial judgment)*

**5. Start KUCAS before the vessel sails.** The technical evaluation report covers the product; the technical inspection report covers the shipment. Neither is issued at the gate. See the [certification guide](/guides/certification/). *(Industry claim)*

**Transit reality.** A direct call is commonly 20–28 days port-to-port; a relay adds 6–8 days, and clearance adds two to five working days on a clean file. Door-to-door to a Kuwait City warehouse is realistically 26–34 days direct and 33–42 days relayed, before a congestion event. The tide window can add another day at either end, and it does not appear on a rate sheet. *(Industry claim + editorial judgment)*

## Clearance: KUCAS, the TER and the TIR

Kuwait's conformity regime stops more cargo than duty does. KUCAS requires a technical evaluation report for the product and a technical inspection report for the shipment. The TER is the product's registration; the TIR is the shipment's certificate. A supplier with a valid TER but no TIR has half the file.

The sequence: classify the HS line and check whether the product is regulated; register the product and manufacturer for the TER; book the shipment inspection for the TIR; declare with Kuwait Customs and pay 5% duty on CIF; truck to the consignee, return the empty, close the file. *(Industry claim)*

The common failures are a mismatched consignee, a TER that covers a different model, an inspection booked after arrival, and a valuation query on a declared value below the customs benchmark. Each adds days, and at a two-berth port those days are expensive because the yard has nowhere to put the box. *(Industry claim)*

## Risk and compliance

**Security.** Shuwaikh was struck by drones on 27 March 2026, with material damage and no casualties. Shuaiba was struck earlier the same month, with fatalities at an adjacent military installation. The pattern is regional, not port-specific, and it is not resolved. War-risk cover should name Shuwaikh, Shuaiba and the inland leg separately. *(Reported fact)*

**Single-channel dependency.** Kuwait has no maritime route around Hormuz; unlike [Oman](/countries/oman/), it cannot offer a Gulf-of-Oman alternative. When the strait is disrupted, Kuwait-bound cargo waits, relays, or diverts by land through Saudi Arabia — which brings its own transit regime. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

**Draft misdeclaration.** A carrier that accepts a booking without checking the arrival draft can create a demurrage event before the ship reaches the buoy. Put the draft limit in the booking confirmation, not in a side email. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Construction disruption.** The KPA expansion will take years and will be built around a working port. Expect berth closures, yard reconfiguration and changed gate hours. Ask for the current berth plan, not last year's. *(Reported fact)*

**Sanctions and re-export.** Cargo moving onward to Iraq through Kuwait's road network carries end-use and documentation obligations. It is a legitimate market, not a shortcut around screening. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Our read

**Shuwaikh is a precision instrument, not a volume gateway.** It is right when the consignee is in Kuwait City, the cargo is consumer or commercial goods in standard boxes, and you can live with tide-dependent scheduling. It is wrong for project cargo, heavy lifts, [machinery](/sectors/machinery/) that needs a deep berth on demand, and anything booked as [LCL](/guides/lcl/) without a confirmed CFS slot. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Book the tide, not the rate.** The difference between a 7.5 m and a 9.5 m arrival is a day or more, and it never appears in the freight quote. Ask for the arrival draft and the berth number before comparing prices. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Do not wait for Mubarak Al-Kabeer.** The project is real and the financing is real, but a first-phase terminal on Boubyan Island will not carry commercial container traffic at scale this decade. Plan on Shuwaikh and Shuaiba. *(Editorial judgment)*

**What would change our mind.** A confirmed 14 m channel at Shuwaikh, a second container terminal in operation, or a sustained Hormuz reopening that restores direct weekly calls. None is a 2027 assumption. *(Editorial judgment)*

## FAQ

**Is Shuwaikh Port open?**
Yes. It has operated since the March 2026 drone attack, which caused material damage but no casualties. Expect schedule variance, not closure. *(Reported fact)*

**What is the maximum draft at Shuwaikh?**
7.5 m at zero tide and up to 9.5 m at high tide, in a channel maintained at 8.5 m. The tide, not the berth, sets the limit. *(Reported fact)*

**How many container berths does Shuwaikh have?**
Two, out of 21. That concentration is why a single berth outage matters more here than at a dedicated container terminal. *(Reported fact)*

**How long does China–Kuwait transit take?**
Commonly 20–28 days direct and 26–36 days with transshipment, plus two to five working days for clearance and a day for the tide window at either end. For time-critical cargo, compare [sea versus air](/compare/sea-vs-air/) before switching modes. *(Industry claim)*

**Do I need KUCAS for every shipment?**
No — only for regulated categories. But the TER is product-level and the TIR is shipment-level, so a compliant supplier still needs the inspection booked. *(Industry claim)*

**Will Mubarak Al-Kabeer replace Shuwaikh?**
Not this decade. Phase one is a 1,200 m container terminal on Boubyan Island with a target above 8 million TEU. It will complement the existing ports before it replaces them. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*
