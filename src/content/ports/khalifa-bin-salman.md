## Direct answer

Khalifa Bin Salman Port is Bahrain's only general commercial port, and that sentence explains almost everything about it. The terminal sits on 110 hectares of reclaimed land at Hidd, about 13 km southeast of Manama. It handles all of the country's containerised cargo, most of its general cargo and its vehicle imports. There is no second container terminal to divert to, no competing operator to discipline tariffs and no alternative channel if the approach is closed.

The physical specification is mid-sized, not mega-hub. The port has an 1,800 m quay, including a 900 m container quay, a maximum depth of 15 m, four post-Panamax ship-to-shore cranes with 18-row outreach, 12 rubber-tyred gantries, 840 reefer plugs and 63,500 m² of warehousing. Initial capacity is 1 million TEU, with potential for 3 million. The largest vessel it can handle is about 14,000 TEU and 360 m length overall. APM Terminals Bahrain, 64% owned by APM Terminals and listed on the Bahrain Bourse, is the exclusive operator. ([Bahrain hub](/countries/bahrain/) has the national picture; this page is about using the terminal.)

2026 was the year the single-gate model was stress-tested. The Strait of Hormuz closed at the end of February, the port shut in March and part of April, and monthly import containers fell from 19,562 TEU in January to 3,779 TEU in May — an 80% collapse. General cargo stopped entirely in March. The recovery began in May and June, with general cargo reaching a two-year high of about 112,600 tonnes in June, but container imports were still only 9,036 TEU in October, roughly half the January level. The port did not lose its capacity. It lost access to the sea, and the network that feeds it. *(Reported fact plus editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

**October 2026 — car shipments resumed.** Khalifa Bin Salman Port recorded 705 vehicles in October, the first registered car shipments after six consecutive months without any. The port's vehicle trade is a useful indicator because it is discretionary, import-heavy and dependent on RoRo services. A restart is a sign that carriers are calling again, not just that Bahraini dealers are restocking. *(Reported fact)*

**October 2026 — container imports reached 9,036 TEU.** That was the strongest month since January, but still about half the pre-crisis level. The monthly path — 8,024 TEU in August, 8,185 in September, 9,036 in October — is a slow climb, not a recovery to trend. *(Reported fact)*

**August 2026 — the Q2 results quantified the damage.** APM Terminals Bahrain reported revenue of BHD 14.3 million, down 25.4% year on year, operating profit of BHD 1.6 million, down 68.2%, and total profit down 71.7%. Container terminal revenue fell 25.4%, general cargo 9.8% and marine services 42.7%. The company attributed the decline to the Hormuz closure and the disruption to regional shipping, partly offset by cost cuts. *(Reported fact)*

**June 2026 — general cargo hit a two-year high.** The port handled about 112,600 tonnes of general cargo in June, after about 105,500 tonnes in May. The two-month total of roughly 218,200 tonnes was about 198% above the same two months of 2025, when the port recorded 73,200 tonnes. June alone was nearly four times June 2025's 29,000 tonnes. *(Reported fact)*

**May 2026 — the BHD 100 threshold took effect.** Bahrain extended duty and 10% VAT to imported shipments valued at BHD 100 or more. The change targeted low-value parcels and personal imports. For e-commerce sellers, it removed an unusually cheap entry point into the GCC. *(Reported fact)*

**March–April 2026 — the port stopped.** Cargo movement came to a complete halt in March and was disrupted through part of April after Iranian attacks on Bahrain and other Gulf states. APM Terminals Bahrain declared force majeure at KBSP in early March. The port reopened, but the carrier network took longer to return than the gate did. *(Reported fact)*

**November 2026 — the next test is the Q3 report.** APM Terminals Bahrain's board was scheduled to approve third-quarter results on 11 November. The Q3 numbers will show whether the June–October recovery reached the income statement or whether fixed costs continued to outrun revenue. That is the most useful public signal on the port's commercial health. *(Reported fact)*

## Why it matters: one port, one channel, one operator

Bahrain is the GCC's smallest market with its most concentrated freight system. One port handles essentially all containerised cargo. One company operates it under an exclusive concession. One causeway connects the island to the market that actually matters, Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province. That structure is efficient in normal times and fragile in a crisis.

The fragility is not theoretical. When the Strait of Hormuz closed, Bahrain did not merely lose a sailing. It lost the feeder connection that made its relay work, the RoRo services that supply its car market, and the general cargo vessels that stock its warehouses. A country with one port and no land border of its own discovered that its access to the sea was a single point of failure.

The economics of a fixed-cost terminal made it worse. Cranes, quay, yard and labour cost roughly the same whether the terminal moves 20,000 TEU a month or 4,000. When volume halves, unit costs rise. The operator can recover the gap through tariffs, defer investment or absorb it. APM Terminals Bahrain did some of all three: revenue fell 25.4% in Q2, operating profit fell 68.2%, and the company cut discretionary spending. A shipper should expect the consequences to show up in terminal handling charges and service levels, not just in the operator's accounts.

The domestic market cannot fill the gap. Bahrain has about 1.6 million people. Even at 2025 volumes, KBSP was running at roughly a third of its nameplate capacity. The port's commercial logic depends on serving Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province as much as on serving Bahrain. That is not a new insight, but 2026 made it existential.

## Inside KBSP: four cranes and a 900-metre box quay

Khalifa Bin Salman Port opened on 1 April 2009, replacing Mina Salman in Manama. The new port was built on reclaimed land at Hidd, with a layout designed for containers, general cargo, RoRo and cruise traffic. It is a multi-purpose port, not a dedicated box terminal, and that shapes its operations.

The container terminal has a 900 m quay within the 1,800 m total, four post-Panamax ship-to-shore cranes with 61 m outreach and 18-row span, 12 RTGs and 840 reefer plugs. Maximum depth is 15 m, maximum vessel length 360 m and maximum practical vessel size about 14,000 TEU. Published productivity figures from 2021 were 75 berth moves per hour and 37 crane moves per hour. Those are competent mid-sized terminal numbers, not mega-hub numbers.

Four cranes are the binding constraint. A large vessel's discharge is paced by the equipment, not by the berth length. If one crane is down for maintenance, the terminal loses a quarter of its ship-to-shore capacity. If a vessel arrives with a heavy discharge and a tight window, the terminal has little slack. That is why KBSP is a relay market rather than a direct-call market: the number of mainline services that can be worked efficiently is limited.

The terminal is also the country's general cargo and vehicle gateway. It handles breakbulk, project cargo, steel, cement, food and RoRo. The 63,500 m² of warehousing and the 840 reefer plugs support distribution into Bahrain and, for some cargo, re-export to Saudi Arabia. The Bahrain Logistics Zone sits at the port, with 24/7 customs presence, 20-year renewable land leases and pre-built warehouses.

The operator's structure is unusual for the region. APM Terminals Bahrain is a public company: 64% APM Terminals, 16% Yusuf Bin Ahmed Kanoo and 20% public shareholders. It listed on the Bahrain Bourse in December 2018. That gives the port a published quarterly accounts, a share price and a visible incentive to defend its concession. It also means the port's commercial decisions are legible in a way that state-owned terminals often are not.

The weakness is the same as the strength. One operator, one terminal, one channel. There is no second berth to divert to when a crane breaks down, no competing terminal to discipline tariffs, and no alternative port if the channel is closed. Oman has three container ports; the UAE has several; Bahrain has one.

## The causeway is the product

Ask a Bahraini forwarder what they actually sell and the honest answer is often "access to Saudi Arabia". The King Fahd Causeway carries trucks from Bahrain to Al Khobar and Dammam in under two hours on a good day; from Dammam, Riyadh is roughly 450 km by road. The causeway is about 25 km long and is the island's only fixed link to the mainland.

For a Chinese exporter, this creates three distinct businesses that are often confused.

**1. Bahrain import.** Goods are cleared in Bahrain, duty and VAT are paid, and the goods are sold in Bahrain. This is a 1.6 million-person market with a 15.5% effective tax wedge. It is small, rich and price-sensitive in the way small rich markets are.

**2. Bahrain distribution to Saudi Arabia.** Goods are imported into Bahrain, stored in a logistics zone or warehouse, and then exported or moved to Saudi Arabia under Saudi import procedures. The advantage is speed and storage cost; the disadvantage is that the goods must still clear Saudi customs and meet Saudi conformity requirements, including SABER. Bahrain does not confer Saudi market access.

**3. Transit.** Goods move under bond from KBSP to Saudi Arabia without entering Bahrain's domestic market. This requires a transit declaration and a guarantee. It is operationally cleaner than importing and re-exporting, but it is a customs procedure, not a distribution strategy.

The distinction matters because a Chinese seller who is told "we can distribute to Saudi from Bahrain" needs to ask which of the three is being proposed. The first is a small market. The second is a bonded warehouse play with Saudi compliance attached. The third is a transit route. None of them is a shortcut into Saudi Arabia.

There is also a hard constraint that predates 2026: the GCC is not a customs union in the way the EU is. Goods cleared in Bahrain are not cleared in Saudi Arabia. Duty paid in Bahrain does not discharge Saudi duty. A truck crossing the causeway is an export from Bahrain and an import into Saudi Arabia, with two declarations and two regimes. Any quote that treats the causeway as an internal border is wrong.

The King Hamad Causeway, a planned second link combining road and rail, remains a development project rather than an operating asset. It is a 2030s story, not a 2026 routing option. When it opens, it will add capacity and a rail option; it will not create a customs union.

## The 2026 shutdown and the shape of the recovery

The timeline matters because it shows what a port closure does to a small, import-dependent economy.

**End of February 2026.** The Strait of Hormuz closed. Carrier suspensions followed. Bahrain, which depends on the strait for essentially all of its seaborne trade, lost access to its mainline and feeder network.

**March 2026.** Cargo movement at KBSP came to a complete halt. APM Terminals Bahrain declared force majeure. Monthly import containers fell to 6,147 TEU, from 19,562 in January.

**April 2026.** The port was disrupted through part of the month. Imports were 6,288 TEU. The numbers were slightly above March because some cargo was cleared from inventory and some feeders resumed, not because the network had normalised.

**May 2026.** Imports bottomed at 3,779 TEU. General cargo began to recover, reaching about 105,500 tonnes. The BHD 100 duty and VAT threshold took effect.

**June 2026.** General cargo reached about 112,600 tonnes, a two-year high. The port handled roughly four times June 2025's volume. Container imports had not yet recovered to the same degree.

**August–October 2026.** Imports climbed from 8,024 to 8,185 to 9,036 TEU. The recovery was real but incomplete: October was still about half of January and well below the 2025 monthly average of roughly 17,000 TEU.

**October 2026.** Car shipments resumed with 705 vehicles after six months without registered vehicles. That is a leading indicator for RoRo services and dealer demand, not for container volumes.

The shape of the recovery is instructive. General cargo rebounded first because it is project cargo, construction materials and stocked goods that can move on any available vessel. Container imports recovered more slowly because they depend on scheduled liner services and feeder connections, which are network decisions made outside Bahrain. Cars recovered last because they need dedicated RoRo calls and a dealer network willing to take inventory.

The lesson for a shipper is that a port reopening is not the same as a supply chain reopening. The gate can be open while the services are still thin. A booking that assumes a pre-crisis sailing frequency will be disappointed.

## Bahrain Logistics Zone: the re-export play

The Bahrain Logistics Zone sits at KBSP and is the country's main bonded logistics platform. It offers 100% foreign ownership, no corporate or personal income tax, no inheritance tax, full repatriation of capital and profits, and 24/7 customs presence. Land leases run for 20 years and are renewable; the published rate is BHD 4.14 per square metre per year, inclusive of utilities and services. Pre-built warehouses are available from private developers.

The zone's proposition is re-export and value-added logistics. A company can import goods duty-free into the zone, store or process them, and then re-export them to the GCC, the wider region or further afield. For a Chinese manufacturer, that can be a way to hold inventory closer to Saudi Arabia without paying Bahraini duty and VAT on every box.

The limits are important. The zone is not a Saudi market-access shortcut: goods leaving the zone for Saudi Arabia still face Saudi customs and conformity. It is not a manufacturing free-for-all: the benefits are designed for logistics and light processing, and the origin rules for the US and Singapore FTAs are strict. A warehouse that merely repacks goods does not automatically confer origin.

The zone is also small relative to the port. It cannot absorb the container volumes that a Saudi distribution operation would generate at scale. It is a useful node for a regional inventory strategy, not a substitute for a Saudi warehouse.

## How to act: seven decisions before you book

**1. Decide whether Bahrain is the destination or the hinterland.** If you are selling into Bahrain, size the market honestly: 1.6 million people, high income, a 15.5% effective tax wedge. If you are using Bahrain to reach Saudi Arabia, price the Saudi clearance and conformity, not just the Bahrain leg.

**2. Price the causeway and the Saudi entry before the ocean leg.** The road from KBSP to Al Khobar or Dammam is short; the Saudi import is not. SABER, Saudi duty, Saudi VAT and a Saudi importer of record are separate costs. A Bahraini forwarder who quotes "delivery to Dammam" without them is quoting a truck, not a delivery.

**3. Name the vessel and the relay.** China–KBSP is commonly 20–26 days direct and 25–35 days relayed *(industry claim)*. Direct calls are limited. If the routing is a relay through Jebel Ali, Sohar or Colombo, the connection time is the swing factor. Ask for the feeder and the cut-off, not just the ocean rate.

**4. Register for VAT before you import.** Ten percent VAT is recoverable for a registered business, but it is paid at the border. An unregistered importer absorbs it as a cost. The BHD 100 threshold means even small consignments now carry the charge.

**5. Use the Bahrain Logistics Zone only with a real onward plan.** The zone works for bonded inventory and re-export. It does not create Saudi market access, and it does not suit a high-volume Saudi distribution operation. Model the onward customs and transport before you sign a lease.

**6. Build a contingency through Sohar or Jebel Ali plus road.** Bahrain has one port. If KBSP closes, the alternative is a sea–road combination through Oman or the UAE, with a Saudi or Bahraini land border at the end. Price that route before you need it.

**7. Contract the closure risk.** Force majeure, port closure, carrier omission and diversion are not hypothetical in the Gulf. Ask what happens to your booking, your storage charges and your delivery window if the port shuts for a month. A contract that is silent on closure is a contract that allocates the risk to you.

## Cost and transit drivers

**The ocean leg is short; the relay is the variable.** China–KBSP is commonly 20–26 days direct and 25–35 days relayed *(industry claim)*. A relay through Jebel Ali, Sohar or Colombo adds a connection, a feeder and a second documentation set. The direct rate is usually higher per box; the relay is cheaper on the ocean leg and more expensive on the connection.

**The causeway is cheap per kilometre, expensive per declaration.** The road from KBSP to Al Khobar or Dammam is under two hours on a good day. The Saudi entry adds a declaration, duty, VAT and conformity. Price the border as a fixed cost per shipment, not as a line in the road rate.

**Terminal handling charges are under pressure.** A terminal running at a third of capacity has high unit fixed costs. The operator can recover some of that through tariffs, defer investment or absorb it. Expect the first and watch the second. Ask for the THC, storage free time and demurrage separately.

**Duty and VAT are the biggest line item.** Five percent duty on CIF and 10% VAT on the duty-paid value give an effective rate of about 15.5%. That is the second-highest entry cost in the GCC after Saudi Arabia. For a low-margin consumer product, it can decide whether the market is viable.

**War-risk insurance is priced for the Gulf, not for Bahrain alone.** KBSP is inside the Strait of Hormuz, so a call carries the Gulf war-risk premium even when the port itself is calm. The 2026 closure showed that the premium can move faster than the freight rate.

**Air freight is a niche.** Bahrain International Airport has belly capacity on scheduled carriers and some freighter service. It is useful for launch stock, spares and high-value electronics. It is not a substitute for ocean freight at scale.

**The currency is pegged.** The Bahraini dinar is pegged at about 0.376 to the US dollar, so FX risk is low. The documentation discipline is not: a mismatch between the invoice, the packing list and the declaration is a customs delay, not a rounding error.

## Risk and compliance: where KBSP shipments break

**The single-port trap.** One terminal, one operator, one channel. A crane failure, a labour dispute or a channel incident has no alternative at KBSP. Fix: keep a contingency routing via Sohar or Jebel Ali plus road, and price it before you need it.

**The force-majeure shock.** The port closed for weeks in 2026. A booking that assumed a normal sailing frequency was stranded. Fix: contract the closure risk, keep buffer stock for fast-moving lines and ask the forwarder what its diversion plan is.

**The "Bahrain is the gateway to Saudi" shortcut.** Bahrain does not confer Saudi market access. Goods crossing the causeway face Saudi customs, Saudi duty and SABER. Fix: name the Saudi importer and the conformity route before the first booking.

**The VAT threshold surprise.** Since May 2026, shipments of BHD 100 or more attract duty and 10% VAT. Small e-commerce consignments that used to slip through now carry the charge. Fix: reprice low-value SKUs and register for VAT if you are selling at volume.

**The conformity gap.** Regulated products need GSO-aligned conformity evidence, and the importer is responsible for it. A certificate that arrives after the vessel sails is a warehousing cost. Fix: make conformity a booking prerequisite and keep the file with the commercial invoice.

**The free zone origin illusion.** A warehouse in the Bahrain Logistics Zone does not automatically confer Bahraini origin or US FTA access. The origin rules require substantial transformation. Fix: get a written origin assessment before you build a US-bound supply chain on a Bahrain address.

**The data confusion.** Import TEU, total container throughput and vessel calls are different measures. A headline that says "97,000 containers in five months" is not comparable with a monthly import series of 9,000 TEU. Fix: use the import series for demand and the vessel count for operations.

**The investment deferral.** KBSP has 1 million TEU of initial capacity and potential for 3 million. That potential is only realised if someone funds the cranes and yard. A sustained volume shock makes the case for expansion harder. Fix: ask the operator about its capex plan, not just its tariff.

## Our read: a Saudi hinterland play with a small domestic market

**Do** treat Bahrain as a distribution platform for Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, not as a standalone market of 1.6 million people. The causeway is the product. The port is the enabler. The Saudi clearance is the cost.

**Do** use KBSP for Bahraini imports, for bonded inventory in the Bahrain Logistics Zone and for project cargo that needs a small, responsive port. The terminal is competent, the operator is transparent and the customs process is workable.

**Do** price the 2026 risk into your 2027 plan. A port that closed for weeks can close again. A carrier network that omitted Bahrain can omit it again. The contingency is part of the landed cost, not an insurance afterthought.

**Avoid** assuming that a Bahrain address gives you Saudi market access. It does not. The GCC is not a customs union in practice, and SABER is a real gate. A Chinese seller that builds a Saudi strategy on a Bahrain warehouse will discover this at the first delivery.

**Avoid** reading one good month as a recovery. October's 9,036 TEU was better than May's 3,779, but it was still about half of January and well below the 2025 run rate. The port is recovering; the network is not restored.

**Avoid** treating APM Terminals Bahrain's cost cuts as a sign of strength. They protected the profit line in a bad quarter. They do not create capacity, and they do not guarantee service levels when volume returns. Watch the Q3 results and the capex plan, not the press release.

## FAQ

**Is Khalifa Bin Salman Port the only port in Bahrain?**
It is the only general commercial port and handles essentially all containerised cargo. There are specialised industrial jetties, but no second container terminal.

**How long does sea freight from China to Bahrain take?**
Commonly 20–26 days direct, port to port, and 25–35 days with a relay through Jebel Ali, Sohar or Colombo. Add clearance and the road leg. The ranges are industry claims, not a sailing schedule.

**What happened at KBSP in 2026?**
The port closed in March and part of April after the Strait of Hormuz closure and attacks on Bahrain. APM Terminals Bahrain declared force majeure. Monthly import containers fell from 19,562 TEU in January to 3,779 TEU in May, then recovered to 9,036 TEU in October.

**What is KBSP's capacity?**
One million TEU initial, with potential for three million. The terminal has a 900 m container quay, four post-Panamax cranes, 15 m maximum depth and a practical vessel ceiling of about 14,000 TEU.

**Who operates KBSP?**
APM Terminals Bahrain, 64% owned by APM Terminals, 16% by Yusuf Bin Ahmed Kanoo and 20% by public shareholders. It has been listed on the Bahrain Bourse since December 2018 and holds an exclusive concession.

**What are the import duties and taxes in Bahrain?**
Five percent duty on CIF for most goods and 10% VAT on the duty-paid value, for an effective combined rate of about 15.5%. From May 2026, shipments of BHD 100 or more attract duty and VAT.

**Can I use Bahrain as a gateway to Saudi Arabia?**
Yes, but it is not a customs shortcut. Goods crossing the King Fahd Causeway face Saudi customs, Saudi duty and Saudi conformity requirements, including SABER. Bahrain does not confer Saudi market access.

**What is the Bahrain Logistics Zone?**
A bonded logistics zone at KBSP with 100% foreign ownership, no corporate or personal income tax, 24/7 customs and 20-year renewable land leases at about BHD 4.14 per square metre per year. It is designed for re-export and value-added logistics, not for mass-market manufacturing.

**Is the King Hamad Causeway open?**
No. It is a planned second Saudi–Bahrain link combining road and rail. It remains a development project, not a current routing option.

**How does KBSP compare with Sohar or Jebel Ali?**
KBSP is smaller, shallower and more concentrated. It is a competent gateway for Bahrain and a distribution platform for Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province. Sohar and Jebel Ali have more capacity, more services and more routing options.
