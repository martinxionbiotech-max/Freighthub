## Direct answer

Use FCL when you have roughly 15–20 cubic metres or more, when the cargo is fragile or high-value, or when you need a predictable transit. Use LCL for smaller shipments, mixed supplier orders and test volumes. The crossover is not fixed: destination charges can make LCL more expensive than FCL at 12 cbm, and a low ocean rate can make FCL attractive at 10 cbm.

## Side-by-side

| Factor | FCL | LCL |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing | Per container | Per cbm or tonne, with a minimum |
| Minimum volume | A container | Often 1 cbm, with a minimum charge |
| Handling | Loaded once, sealed | Consolidated and deconsolidated |
| Transit | Usually faster | Usually slower |
| Damage risk | Lower | Higher |
| Free time | Container free time | CFS storage terms |
| Dangerous goods | Carrier approval per container | Co-loader approval, harder |
| Best for | 15–20+ cbm, fragile, high-value | Under 15 cbm, mixed orders, tests |

## The volume crossover

The simple rule is 15 cbm. The real rule depends on four variables:

1. **Ocean freight** per container versus per cbm.
2. **Destination charges** — LCL has a CFS handling charge, a documentation fee and sometimes a minimum.
3. **Inland delivery** — a 20-foot container needs a truck that can take it; LCL cargo can be delivered in a smaller vehicle.
4. **Time** — LCL adds consolidation and deconsolidation days.

A worked example, using planning numbers:

- A 20-foot container holds about 28–33 cbm of usable space.
- A 40-foot high-cube holds about 65–76 cbm.
- LCL is priced per cbm, with a minimum charge of 1–2 cbm.
- Destination LCL charges can be USD 100–300 per cbm, depending on the port.

At 10 cbm, the LCL freight may be lower, but the destination charges can be similar to a 20-foot container's. At 15 cbm, the gap narrows. At 20 cbm, FCL usually wins on total cost and clearly wins on handling.

The exact crossover should be calculated per lane, not assumed. Ask the forwarder for both quotes with the full destination charges.

## Handling and damage

LCL cargo is handled more often:

- At the origin CFS, when it is loaded into the container.
- At the destination CFS, when it is unloaded.
- At the warehouse, when it is sorted.

Each handling is a chance for damage, loss or mis-shipment. For fragile furniture, electronics, machinery or anything with a finished surface, FCL is usually worth the premium even at lower volumes.

FCL also gives the shipper control over the loading plan. The container is packed once, sealed, and opened at the destination. That reduces the risk of a co-loader putting heavy cargo on top of fragile cargo.

## Transit

LCL is slower for three reasons:

- The cargo must wait for the consolidation cut-off.
- The container may be held until it is full.
- The destination CFS must deconsolidate before the cargo is available.

A typical LCL shipment adds three to seven days to the FCL transit, and sometimes more in peak season. For time-sensitive cargo, that matters.

## Destination charges

This is where many LCL quotes mislead. The ocean freight per cbm looks cheap, but the destination charges are per shipment and can be high:

- CFS handling.
- Documentation and delivery order.
- Customs brokerage.
- Storage after free time.
- Delivery to the consignee.

A small LCL shipment can pay the same destination charges as a 20-foot container. That is why the crossover is often lower than the freight-only comparison suggests.

## Dangerous goods

LCL and DG are a difficult combination. The co-loader must accept the class, the container must be segregated, and the CFS must be licensed. Many consolidators refuse DG entirely, and the ones that accept it charge a premium.

For batteries, chemicals and other DG cargo, FCL is usually the practical option even at low volume, because the shipper controls the container and the carrier approval.

## When FCL wins

- Volume above roughly 15–20 cbm.
- Fragile, high-value or finished cargo.
- Dangerous goods.
- Reefer cargo.
- A delivery date that cannot absorb LCL consolidation time.
- A loading plan that needs control.
- Multiple suppliers whose cargo must be consolidated at origin.

## When LCL wins

- Volume below roughly 10–12 cbm.
- A test order or a new supplier.
- Mixed SKUs from multiple suppliers.
- A consignee without space for a full container.
- A destination with cheap CFS handling and frequent consolidations.
- Cargo that is not time-sensitive or fragile.

## The hybrid: buyer's consolidation

A buyer's consolidation (also called a co-load or a multi-supplier FCL) combines cargo from several suppliers into one container. It gives the buyer FCL economics and control without filling the container from one factory.

The model works when:

- The suppliers are in the same region.
- The cargo can be delivered to a consolidation warehouse.
- The buyer has a forwarder who can manage the consolidation.
- The volume is enough to justify a container.

It is more work than LCL and less work than managing several FCLs. For a buyer with a growing order book, it is often the best of both.

## Cost comparison checklist

When comparing FCL and LCL, include:

1. Ocean freight.
2. Origin CFS or container loading.
3. Destination CFS or container terminal.
4. Documentation and delivery order.
5. Customs brokerage.
6. Storage and free time.
7. Inland delivery.
8. Insurance.
9. The cost of a day of delay.

The last item is often omitted. If the LCL adds five days and the cargo is launch stock, the delay may cost more than the freight difference.

## Our read

FCL is the better product for most cargo above 15 cbm, and the gap widens for fragile, high-value or DG cargo. LCL is a useful tool for small volumes, but it is not a cheap version of FCL; it is a different service with more handling and less control.

The mistake to avoid is comparing the ocean rate only. An LCL quote that looks 30% cheaper can be more expensive once the destination charges and the delay are included.

## FAQ

**At what volume is FCL cheaper than LCL?**
Usually around 15–20 cbm, but it depends on the lane and the destination charges. Get both quotes with the full cost.

**Is LCL slower?**
Yes, typically by three to seven days because of consolidation and deconsolidation.

**Can I ship dangerous goods LCL?**
Sometimes, but many consolidators refuse DG. FCL is usually the practical option.

**Can I ship reefer cargo LCL?**
Rarely. Reefer containers are usually FCL, because the temperature control is container-specific.

**How much does a 20-foot container hold?**
About 28–33 cbm of usable volume, depending on the cargo and the loading plan.

**How much does a 40-foot high-cube hold?**
About 65–76 cbm of usable volume, depending on the cargo and the loading plan.

**Is FCL always faster?**
Usually, but a direct LCL service can beat a slow FCL rotation. Check the routing, not just the mode.
