## Direct answer

Chemicals are the least forgiving cargo in the GCC. The shipment needs a UN classification, a GHS-compliant safety data sheet, the correct UN packaging and, in most countries, an import permit that is separate from customs clearance. A mis-declared chemical is not a paperwork problem; it is a port safety incident, a fine and a possible refusal of the whole consignment. Start with the SDS and the HS code, not the freight rate.

## Classification: the first decision

A chemical shipment is classified on three axes:

**UN number and proper shipping name.** The UN number identifies the substance or the generic group. The proper shipping name is the legal name used on the declaration, not the marketing name.

**Hazard class and packing group.** Class 3 (flammable liquids), Class 8 (corrosives) and Class 6.1 (toxic) are the most common in China–GCC trade. Packing group I, II or III reflects the degree of danger.

**HS code.** The tariff code determines duty and the import permit regime. A chemical can be dutiable at 5% and still require an environment permit that takes weeks.

The SDS usually contains the UN number and the class. If it does not, the SDS is incomplete, and the shipment is not ready.

## The safety data sheet

A GHS-compliant SDS has 16 sections:

1. Identification
2. Hazard identification
3. Composition
4. First-aid measures
5. Fire-fighting measures
6. Accidental release measures
7. Handling and storage
8. Exposure controls
9. Physical and chemical properties
10. Stability and reactivity
11. Toxicological information
12. Ecological information
13. Disposal
14. Transport information
15. Regulatory information
16. Other information

For the GCC, three sections matter most:

**Section 14 — transport information.** The UN number, class, packing group, proper shipping name and packing instruction. If this section is missing or inconsistent, the carrier will reject the booking.

**Section 2 — hazard identification.** The GHS pictograms, signal words and hazard statements. The label on the drum must match.

**Section 15 — regulatory information.** The destination's requirements. This is often the weakest section in a Chinese SDS, because the supplier is not familiar with GCC rules.

Arabic labelling is required in most GCC markets for consumer chemicals and often for industrial chemicals. The SDS itself may be accepted in English, but the label and the emergency information should be in Arabic and English.

## Import permits: the part that takes time

Customs clearance is only one approval. Chemicals typically need additional permits:

**Saudi Arabia.** SASO standards, the Ministry of Environment, Water and Agriculture for certain substances, the Civil Defence for storage, and SFDA for cosmetics, drugs and food-contact chemicals. The importer must hold the permit; the exporter cannot obtain it on the importer's behalf.

**UAE.** The emirate's environment authority and civil defence regulate hazardous materials. Dubai Municipality's environment department issues permits for certain chemicals. The port and the storage facility add their own approvals.

**Qatar.** The Ministry of Environment and Climate Change and the Ministry of Public Health regulate chemicals and hazardous waste. Hamad Port requires DG acceptance.

**Kuwait.** The Environment Public Authority and the Public Authority for Industry regulate chemicals. KUCAS applies to some products.

**Oman.** The Environment Authority and the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion regulate imports and storage.

**Bahrain.** The Supreme Council for Environment and the Ministry of Industry and Commerce regulate chemicals and hazardous materials.

The permit timeline is often longer than the shipping time. A first-time importer should start the permit process before the production order, not after the vessel sails.

## Packaging and transport

**UN specification packaging.** Drums, jerry cans, IBCs and combination packaging must be UN-certified for the substance and the packing group. A standard steel drum is not automatically a UN drum.

**ISO tanks.** For bulk liquids, an ISO tank is the standard. The tank needs a valid certificate, a cleaning certificate and the correct markings. Tank cleaning between products is a cost and a contamination risk.

**Segregation.** Some chemicals cannot be stowed together. The IMDG Code sets the segregation rules, and the carrier's planner applies them. A mixed container of incompatible chemicals is a serious risk.

**Temperature control.** Some chemicals need a reefer or a ventilated container. The SDS section 7 and 9 gives the storage temperature range.

**Samples.** A 1-litre sample is still a DG shipment. The same classification, packaging and documentation apply, often with a small-quantity exemption that does not eliminate the paperwork.

## Sea versus air

Sea is the default for chemicals. Air is used for small samples, time-sensitive catalysts and high-value specialty chemicals, subject to IATA rules and carrier approval.

Sea advantages:

- Lower cost per tonne.
- Larger volumes, including ISO tanks.
- More carriers accept the common classes.

Sea constraints:

- Port acceptance for DG is limited.
- Segregation and stowage take time.
- Some ports refuse certain classes.

Air advantages:

- Speed for samples and urgent products.
- Smaller quantities.

Air constraints:

- Quantity limits per package and per aircraft.
- Many classes are forbidden on passenger aircraft.
- The documentation burden is high relative to the shipment size.

## Storage: the hidden constraint

A chemical importer needs a licensed warehouse. The licence covers:

- The classes that can be stored.
- The maximum quantity.
- The separation distances.
- The fire-fighting equipment.
- The emergency response plan.

In the GCC, hazmat storage is scarce and expensive. A normal 3PL will not accept Class 3 or Class 8 cargo. A free-zone warehouse may have a DG licence, but the licence is specific to the zone and the class.

The practical consequence: **do not book the vessel until the warehouse is confirmed**. A container of chemicals that arrives without a licensed receiver can sit at the port, incurring storage and demurrage, while the importer finds a facility.

## Cost drivers

| Driver | Effect | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| DG surcharge | Fixed fee per shipment or container | Whether the quote includes it |
| Packaging | UN drums, IBCs, ISO tanks cost more | The packing instruction |
| Tank cleaning | Adds cost per ISO tank | The previous product |
| Permits | Add weeks and a fee | Start early |
| Storage | Hazmat storage is scarce | Licensed facility |
| Insurance | Higher premium for DG | Cover for pollution and fire |
| Port choice | Not every port accepts every class | DG acceptance |
| Volume | Less-than-container loads are harder | Consolidation options |

A chemical shipment can cost 30–60% more than a general cargo shipment once packaging, DG surcharges, permits and storage are included. The exact premium depends on the class and the volume.

## Risks and failure modes

**Mis-declaration.** The most serious. A chemical declared as general cargo can be refused, fined or involved in a fire.

**Incomplete SDS.** The carrier or the port rejects the booking because section 14 is missing or inconsistent.

**Missing permit.** The cargo arrives and cannot be cleared because the environment or health permit was not obtained.

**Wrong packaging.** A non-UN drum is refused at the gate.

**Incompatible stowage.** Two chemicals are packed together and react.

**No licensed warehouse.** The container sits at the port while the importer finds storage.

**Arabic labelling.** The label is in English only, and the shipment is held for re-labelling.

## How to act: a chemical shipment checklist

1. **Get the SDS** and check sections 2, 7, 9, 14 and 15.
2. **Classify the substance** — UN number, class, packing group, HS code.
3. **Confirm the import permit** for the destination and the importer.
4. **Confirm the packaging** — UN specification, ISO tank or IBC.
5. **Confirm the carrier and port** accept the class.
6. **Confirm the warehouse** is licensed for the class and quantity.
7. **Prepare the DG declaration** and the Arabic label.
8. **Book with a buffer** for permit and inspection delays.

## Our read

Chemicals are a specialist trade, and the GCC is not a market for generalist forwarders. The shippers who succeed have a fixed set of products, a repeatable SDS file, a licensed warehouse and a carrier relationship that accepts their class. The ones who fail treat a chemical as a product with a freight rate.

The regulatory burden is highest in Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and lower but still real in the smaller markets. If a product can be permitted in Saudi Arabia, the other GCC markets are usually manageable, with national additions.

The strategic advice is simple: **do not improvise**. A chemical shipment that is mis-declared or under-permitted is not a cost problem; it is a safety and legal problem.

## FAQ

**Do I need an SDS to ship chemicals?**
Yes. A GHS-compliant SDS is required for the carrier, the port and the importer. Section 14 must contain the transport information.

**What is a UN specification package?**
Packaging that has been tested and certified to the UN standard for the substance and packing group. A standard drum is not automatically UN-certified.

**Can I ship chemicals by air?**
Some classes and quantities, subject to IATA rules and carrier approval. Many chemicals are not economic or permitted by air.

**Do chemicals need an import permit in the GCC?**
Usually yes, in addition to customs clearance. The permit is issued by an environment, health or industry authority, and the importer must hold it.

**Can I store chemicals in a normal warehouse?**
No. Hazmat storage requires a licence that covers the class, the quantity and the fire-fighting arrangements.

**What is the most common rejection reason?**
An incomplete or inconsistent SDS, followed by a missing import permit and non-UN packaging.

**Can I ship samples?**
Yes, but a sample is still a DG shipment. The classification, packaging and documentation apply, even for small quantities.
