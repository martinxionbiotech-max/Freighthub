## Direct answer

Machinery is a project, not a parcel. The freight cost is driven by dimensions, weight, lifting points and road permits, not by a per-container rate. Most machines move on flat racks, open tops or break bulk, with a crating specification that protects against sea air and handling. The biggest risks are damage in transit, a missing spare part and a certification or voltage mismatch that stops commissioning.

## Why machinery is different

A machine is defined by its dimensions, its weight, its centre of gravity and its lifting points. Those four facts determine the equipment, the route, the crane and the cost. A forwarder who quotes a machine without them is guessing.

The second difference is the **installation date**. A production line that arrives late can delay a factory opening, a commissioning milestone or a contractual handover. The freight is a small share of the project cost, but a delay can be a large share of the penalty.

The third difference is the **documentation**. A machine needs a manual, a wiring diagram, a spare-parts list and, often, a certificate of origin and a conformity certificate. The customs broker needs the invoice and the HS code; the engineer needs the rest.

## Equipment: what fits what

**Standard container.** Suitable for small machines that fit through the door and can be loaded by forklift. The machine must be braced to prevent movement.

**Flat rack.** Suitable for machines that are too wide or too tall for a container but can be lifted onto the platform. The cargo is lashed to the rack and covered with a tarpaulin. Flat racks are available in 20-foot and 40-foot lengths.

**Open top.** Suitable for machines that are too tall for a standard container but fit within the width. The top is covered with a tarpaulin, and the cargo is loaded by crane.

**Break bulk.** Suitable for very large or very heavy items that cannot be containerised. The cargo is lifted directly onto the vessel, with a stowage plan and lashing designed by the carrier.

**Heavy lift.** For items above the capacity of a standard crane or a standard vessel. A heavy-lift ship or a specialized carrier is required, and the port must have the lifting capacity.

The equipment choice is a function of the machine's dimensions, weight and lifting points, and the port's crane capacity. A machine that can be built and shipped in modules is often cheaper to move than a single large unit.

## Crating and protection

Sea transport exposes a machine to salt air, condensation, vibration and handling. The crating specification should include:

- **A timber crate or a steel frame** sized to the machine, with skids for forklift access.
- **ISPM 15 heat-treated wood**, stamped with the IPPC mark.
- **Moisture barrier** — a VCI bag or a desiccant, especially for electronics and precision surfaces.
- **Shock indication** — a shock label or a data logger for sensitive equipment.
- **Lashing points** — designed into the crate, not added at the port.
- **Markings** — weight, centre of gravity, lifting points, "this side up", the project reference.

The most common damage is not a dropped crate. It is condensation inside a sealed crate, or a machine that shifted because the lashing was attached to the crate rather than the machine.

## Voltage, frequency and manuals

The GCC uses 220–240V at 50Hz. China also uses 220V at 50Hz, so a Chinese machine is often compatible, but not always:

- **Motor speed.** A 60Hz motor running on 50Hz runs about 17% slower, and a 50Hz motor running on 60Hz runs faster and may overheat.
- **Control systems.** PLCs, drives and sensors may be voltage-specific.
- **Plugs and sockets.** The GCC uses type G (UK-style) in many buildings, with type A/B in some older installations. Confirm the plug and the socket.
- **Manuals.** Arabic and English are expected for many projects. A Chinese-only manual can delay commissioning.

The fix is to specify the voltage, frequency and plug type in the purchase order, and to include the manuals and wiring diagrams in the shipment.

## Certification and pre-shipment inspection

Machinery is regulated at the product level and, for some projects, at the contract level.

**Product conformity.** Some machines fall under SASO technical regulations, especially those with electrical components, pressure vessels, lifting equipment or safety devices. SABER may apply. The UAE's ESMA/ECAS may apply to electrical machinery.

**Pre-shipment inspection.** Some government, utility and oil-and-gas contracts require a pre-shipment inspection certificate from an approved inspection body. The inspection covers the specification, the quantity, the packaging and sometimes the performance. Confirm the requirement before the machine is crated.

**Dual-use export controls.** Some machine tools, lasers, sensors and precision equipment are subject to Chinese export controls or destination import controls. Confirm the classification before the order.

**Pressure and lifting equipment.** Pressure vessels, boilers, cranes and lifting gear may need a design review or a certificate from a recognised body. The requirements vary by country.

## Ports and routes

**Jebel Ali** is the region's main project-cargo hub, with heavy-lift capacity, a large forwarder pool and good road links to the UAE and Saudi Arabia.

**Dammam** serves the Eastern Province and Riyadh, with heavy-lift capacity and a rail link to Riyadh Dry Port.

**Khalifa Port** in Abu Dhabi has deep water and project-cargo facilities, and it serves KEZAD industrial projects.

**Sohar and Duqm** in Oman are alternatives for projects that want to avoid Hormuz or use Oman's industrial zones. Duqm has a dry dock and large industrial land.

**Jeddah** handles project cargo for western Saudi, but the port is urban and the heavy-lift capacity is more constrained than Jebel Ali or Dammam.

The route decision should consider the crane capacity at the port, the road permits for the inland leg, the bridge clearances and the project site access.

| Insurance | Higher value and risk | All-risk, project cargo |
| Inspection | Adds time and cost | Contract requirement |
| Installation | Separate from freight | Engineer availability |

A project-cargo shipment can cost several times a container shipment of the same value, because the equipment, the crane, the permits and the insurance are all specialised. The exact cost depends on the dimensions and the route.

## Risks and failure modes

**Damage in transit.** Condensation, shifting or handling. The claim requires a survey and photos.

**Missing spare part.** A machine arrives without the part needed to commission it.

**Voltage mismatch.** The machine is built for the wrong frequency or plug type.

**Certification gap.** The machine is regulated but not certified.

**Road permit delay.** An oversize load cannot move without a permit, and the permit takes time.

**Site access.** The machine fits the road but not the site gate or the foundation.

**Customs valuation.** A high-value machine with a related-party invoice attracts scrutiny.

## How to act: a machinery shipment checklist

1. **Measure and weigh the machine** — dimensions, weight, centre of gravity, lifting points.
2. **Choose the equipment** — flat rack, open top, break bulk or heavy lift.
3. **Design the crating** — ISPM 15, VCI, shock indicators, lashing points.
4. **Confirm the voltage, frequency and plug type.**
5. **Confirm the certification and inspection requirements.**
6. **Survey the route** — port crane, road permits, site access.
7. **Book the heavy lift early** — capacity is limited.
8. **Ship the manuals and spare parts** with the machine.
9. **Insure the project cargo** — all-risk, including installation.

## Installation and commissioning

The freight scope usually ends at the site, but the project does not. Installation and commissioning require the manual, the wiring diagram, the spare-parts list and, often, a technician from the manufacturer.

Three freight implications:

- **The technician's visa and travel** are separate from the cargo and should be planned with the same lead time.
- **The commissioning spares** should travel with the machine, not in a later shipment. A missing sensor can delay a start-up by weeks.
- **The installation tools** may be classified separately from the machine and need their own declaration.

A machine that arrives on time but cannot be commissioned is a machine that is late. The logistics plan should include the installation sequence, not just the delivery date.

## Our read

Machinery is the category where a cheap forwarder is most expensive. The machine is often the most valuable thing in the container, the installation date is contractual, and the cost of a delay or a damage claim dwarfs the freight saving.

The shippers who succeed treat the move as a project: a route survey, a crating specification, a lifting plan, a permit plan and a spare-parts list. The ones who fail book a flat rack and hope.

For the GCC, Jebel Ali and Dammam are the safest defaults for containerised and flat-rack machinery. For very large projects, Duqm and Sohar are worth evaluating, especially if the project is in Oman or the client wants a Hormuz-free route.

## FAQ

**Can a machine fit in a standard container?**
Only if it fits through the door and can be loaded by forklift. Most industrial machines need a flat rack, an open top or break bulk.

**What is ISPM 15 and why does it matter?**
It is the international standard for wood packaging. Solid wood crates and pallets must be heat-treated or fumigated and stamped, or the destination can refuse or treat them.

**Do I need a pre-shipment inspection?**
Some government, utility and oil-and-gas contracts require one. Confirm before crating, because the inspection may require access to the machine.

**What voltage does the GCC use?**
220–240V at 50Hz. Confirm the machine's rating and the plug type before shipment.

**How long does project cargo take?**
18–35 days port to port, plus heavy-lift booking, permits and inland delivery. The range is wide because the equipment and the route vary.

**Who arranges the crane at the destination?**
Usually the consignee or the project contractor, but the forwarder should confirm the port crane capacity and the site crane plan.

**Can I ship a machine with a battery?**
Yes, subject to the battery rules. A machine with an embedded lithium battery may need a DG declaration.
