## Direct answer

Lithium batteries are Class 9 dangerous goods, and the GCC is stricter in practice than the rulebook suggests. Air shipments need UN38.3 test summaries, a state-of-charge limit (typically 30% for lithium-ion) and explicit carrier approval. Sea shipments follow the IMDG Code with DG declarations, UN packaging and port acceptance. Many carriers and forwarders refuse battery cargo unless the file is perfect. Budget extra time, extra cost and a real chance of rejection.

## The four UN numbers you need to know

**UN3480 — lithium-ion batteries shipped alone.** The strictest common case. Air shipment is cargo aircraft only, with a state-of-charge limit and specific packing instructions. Many forwarders will not handle it unless the shipper is known.

**UN3481 — lithium-ion batteries contained in equipment, or packed with equipment.** The common case for laptops, phones, power tools and e-scooters. The rules depend on whether the battery is inside the device or packed alongside it.

**UN3090 — lithium-metal batteries shipped alone.** Used in watches, sensors and some medical devices. Air transport is heavily restricted, and many carriers refuse it entirely.

**UN3091 — lithium-metal batteries contained in equipment, or packed with equipment.** The common case for small devices with coin cells.

The UN number is not a shipping detail. It determines the packing instruction, the label, the documentation and whether the cargo can fly at all.

## UN38.3: the test that unlocks everything

UN38.3 is the United Nations test series for lithium batteries. It covers:

- Altitude simulation.
- Thermal test.
- Vibration.
- Shock.
- External short circuit.
- Impact/crush.
- Overcharge.
- Forced discharge.

The manufacturer or the battery supplier must provide a UN38.3 test summary. The summary is not the full test report; it is a one-page document that identifies the cell or battery, the tests and the result. Customs and carriers ask for it.

Three practical points:

**The summary must match the battery.** A summary for a 5,000 mAh pouch cell does not cover a 20,000 mAh pack. The model, the capacity and the chemistry must match.

**The summary must be current.** If the cell supplier changes the chemistry or the design, the test may need to be repeated.

**The summary is not a certificate of conformity.** It is a transport document. The product still needs SASO, ESMA, KUCAS or QGOSM certification for the destination market.

## Air freight: the rules that reject cargo

Air transport is governed by the IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations, which follow the ICAO technical instructions. The key constraints for lithium batteries:

- **State of charge.** Lithium-ion cells and batteries shipped alone are typically limited to 30% state of charge. The limit reduces the risk of thermal runaway.
- **Cargo aircraft only.** UN3480 is generally forbidden on passenger aircraft. It must move on cargo aircraft, which limits capacity and routing.
- **Quantity limits.** The packing instruction sets maximum quantities per package and per consignment.
- **Packaging.** UN specification packaging, or the specific packaging allowed by the packing instruction, with the correct lithium battery mark.
- **Documentation.** A shipper's declaration for dangerous goods, plus the UN38.3 summary and the air waybill notation.
- **Carrier approval.** The airline must accept the DG class and the specific commodity. A forwarder's booking is not the airline's acceptance.

The most common air rejection is not a rule violation. It is an incomplete file: a missing UN38.3 summary, a state of charge declaration that does not match the battery, or a label that is the wrong size or in the wrong place.

## Sea freight: more capacity, more paperwork

Sea transport follows the International Maritime Dangerous Goods (IMDG) Code. The constraints are different from air:

- **Class 9 classification** with the correct UN number and packing group.
- **DG declaration** from the shipper, with the correct technical name.
- **UN specification packaging** for the battery type and quantity.
- **Marking and labelling** — the Class 9 label, the lithium battery mark, the UN number.
- **Stowage and segregation** — away from heat sources, with the correct container type.
- **Port and terminal acceptance** — not every terminal accepts Class 9, and not every carrier does.
- **Vessel approval** — the carrier must accept the booking and the stowage plan.

Sea is usually the only economic option for large volumes, but it is not a loophole. A container of batteries that is mis-declared as general cargo is a serious safety and legal risk, and the carrier can refuse it at the gate.

## Damaged, defective and recalled batteries

Damaged, defective or recalled batteries are a separate category. They are generally forbidden for air transport and heavily restricted for sea. If they must move, they require a specific approval, special packaging and a carrier that explicitly accepts them.

The practical advice: do not ship damaged batteries. Discharge, dismantle or dispose of them locally, and document the disposal. A cheap disposal is better than a rejected shipment and a safety incident.

## GCC import and certification

Transport compliance and product compliance are separate.

**Saudi Arabia.** SASO technical regulations cover batteries and battery-powered products. SABER PCoC/SCoC applies to the product. The Saudi Standards body also regulates the safety of electrical products. For large or industrial batteries, additional requirements may apply.

**UAE.** ESMA/ECAS covers regulated electrical products, including some battery-powered goods. Dubai Municipality and the emirate customs authorities regulate DG storage and transport.

**Kuwait, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain.** KUCAS, QGOSM and the national schemes apply. DG import permits may be required in addition to product conformity.

The common mistake is to treat the UN38.3 summary as the product certificate. It is not. The product needs the destination's conformity certificate, and the shipment needs the transport documents.

## Packaging and warehousing

Battery packaging has three jobs: prevent short circuit, prevent damage, and contain a failure.

- **Terminal protection.** Cover the terminals with the original cap or tape.
- **Inner packaging.** Prevent movement and contact between cells.
- **Outer packaging.** UN specification for the quantity and type.
- **Labels.** Class 9, lithium battery mark, UN number, orientation arrows.
- **Documentation pouch.** The DG declaration and the UN38.3 summary.

Warehousing is the other half. A battery warehouse needs:

- Fire detection and suppression suitable for lithium fires.
- Separation from combustible goods.
- Temperature control.
- Trained staff.
- A register of quantities and UN numbers.

In the GCC, DG storage is licensed. A normal 3PL cannot store batteries without the approval, and a free-zone warehouse may have different rules from a mainland warehouse.

| Insurance | Higher premium for DG | Cover for fire and contamination |
| Warehousing | DG storage is scarcer and costlier | Licensed facility |
| Compliance | UN38.3 and product certification | Current documents |

A battery shipment can cost 20–50% more than a comparable general cargo shipment once the DG surcharge, packaging and insurance are included. The exact premium depends on the carrier, the route and the quantity.

## Risks and failure modes

**Mis-declaration.** The most serious. A battery shipment declared as general cargo can be rejected, fined or, in the worst case, involved in a fire.

**Expired or mismatched UN38.3.** The summary does not cover the battery in the box.

**State of charge.** The batteries arrive above the air limit, and the airline refuses them.

**Carrier rejection.** The forwarder booked a carrier that does not accept Class 9.

**Warehouse refusal.** The 3PL is not licensed for DG storage.

**Product certification gap.** The battery is transport-compliant but not market-compliant.

**Returns.** A returned battery is a DG shipment in reverse, and many ecommerce returns processes are not set up for it.

## How to act: a battery shipment checklist

1. **Identify the UN number** for the exact configuration.
2. **Obtain the UN38.3 summary** and confirm it matches the cell or battery.
3. **Confirm the state of charge** and the air eligibility.
4. **Confirm the carrier accepts Class 9** on the route.
5. **Pack to the correct instruction** with the correct labels.
6. **Prepare the DG declaration** and the UN38.3 file.
7. **Confirm the destination product certificate** — SASO, ESMA, KUCAS, QGOSM.
8. **Confirm the warehouse** is licensed for DG storage.
9. **Plan the returns** process before the first sale.

## Our read

Batteries are the category where cheap freight quotes are most dangerous. The forwarder who quotes a general-cargo rate for a battery shipment is either mis-declaring it or planning to. Both outcomes are bad.

The shippers who succeed are the ones who build a repeatable DG file: a UN38.3 summary per cell, a packing instruction per configuration, a carrier approval per route and a licensed warehouse per market. It is expensive up front and cheap per shipment.

For ecommerce sellers, the battery category is a trap. Power banks, e-scooters and replacement batteries are restricted by many marketplaces and carriers, and the returns process is a DG problem. Confirm the marketplace's category rules before the inventory leaves China.

## FAQ

**Can I ship lithium batteries by air?**
Yes, subject to UN38.3, state-of-charge limits, packing instructions, documentation and carrier approval. Batteries shipped alone are cargo aircraft only; some lithium-metal batteries are effectively refused.

**What is UN38.3?**
The UN test series for lithium batteries, covering altitude, thermal, vibration, shock, short circuit, impact, overcharge and forced discharge. A test summary is required for transport.

**What is the 30% state of charge rule?**
Lithium-ion cells and batteries shipped alone by air are typically limited to 30% state of charge to reduce fire risk.

**Can I ship batteries by sea?**
Yes, under the IMDG Code, with a DG declaration, UN packaging, labels and carrier/port acceptance.

**Do batteries need SABER?**
Battery-powered products covered by SASO technical regulations need SABER certification. The UN38.3 summary is a transport document, not a product certificate.

**Can I ship a damaged battery?**
Generally no by air, and only with specific approval by sea. Do not ship damaged or recalled batteries as general cargo.

**Why is my battery shipment so expensive?**
DG surcharges, UN packaging, insurance and limited carrier capacity add cost. The premium is highest for air and for small parcels.
