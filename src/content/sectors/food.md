## Direct answer

Food is the GCC's most inspection-heavy import category. Registration, Arabic labelling, shelf life and, for meat and dairy, halal and veterinary certificates are checked before customs release. A reefer container is only part of the cold chain; the port, the inspection yard and the truck are also temperature nodes. Plan registration before production, book reefer equipment early, and treat the remaining shelf life at arrival as a hard constraint.

## Why food is different

Three features make food the most operationally demanding category.

**It is inspected, not just cleared.** Customs checks the declaration; the food authority checks the product. The two processes are separate, and the food authority can reject a shipment that customs has already released.

**It has a clock.** Every food product has a shelf life, and the remaining shelf life at import is a regulatory and commercial constraint. A two-week delay can turn a sellable container into a write-off.

**It is temperature-sensitive.** A reefer container is a system, not a guarantee. The cold chain includes the pre-cooling at origin, the container, the vessel, the port, the inspection and the truck. A break at any node can spoil the cargo.

## The regulators and what they require

**Saudi Arabia — SFDA.** The Saudi Food and Drug Authority regulates food, beverages, cosmetics, drugs and medical devices. Food imports need product registration, Arabic labelling and, for some categories, a health certificate and halal evidence. The importer must be registered with SFDA and hold the relevant activity licence.

**UAE — Dubai Municipality and ADAFSA.** Dubai Municipality's food safety department registers food products and premises in Dubai. Abu Dhabi Agriculture and Food Safety Authority (ADAFSA) regulates Abu Dhabi. The other emirates have their own arrangements. The UAE does not have a single federal food registration for all emirates.

**Qatar — Ministry of Public Health.** Food safety and registration sit with the Ministry. Arabic labelling and shelf-life rules apply.

**Kuwait — PAFN.** The Public Authority for Food and Nutrition registers food products and inspects imports. Arabic labelling and shelf-life rules apply.

**Oman and Bahrain.** Food safety is administered by the health and municipal authorities, with national labelling and shelf-life requirements.

The common thread: **registration is product-specific and importer-specific**. A registration held by one importer does not transfer automatically to another, and a product registered in one emirate is not automatically registered in another.

## Labelling: the most common rejection

GCC food labelling typically requires:

- Product name in Arabic and English.
- Ingredients list in Arabic (English may be added).
- Net content in metric units.
- Production date and expiry date.
- Storage conditions.
- Country of origin.
- Name and address of the manufacturer and the importer.
- Allergen information.
- Nutritional information, where required.
- Halal mark, where applicable.
- Batch or lot number.

The label must be applied before arrival. A sticker applied at the port is not acceptable for most categories, and a label that covers the original label can be rejected.

The most common failures:

- Arabic translation errors or missing Arabic.
- Expiry date format that the inspector cannot read.
- Production and expiry dates that do not match the shelf-life claim.
- Importer name that does not match the registration.
- Allergen information missing.
- Nutrition table in the wrong format.

## Shelf life: the hard constraint

GCC regulators typically require a minimum remaining shelf life at the time of import — often a proportion of the total shelf life, and sometimes a fixed minimum for short-life products. The exact rule varies by country and category.

The practical implication is that **transit time is a product decision**. A product with a 12-month shelf life and a 30-day transit has more room than a product with a 6-month shelf life and the same transit. A slow or delayed shipment can arrive below the threshold and be rejected, even though the product is safe.

The mitigation:

- Choose a shelf life that accommodates the transit plus the distribution margin.
- Book the fastest reliable service for short-life products.
- Avoid transhipment for chilled or short-life cargo.
- Track the vessel and pre-alert the importer if the ETA slips.

## Halal and the certificate chain

Halal is required for meat, poultry and some processed foods in Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and it is a commercial expectation across the GCC. The certificate chain usually includes:

- A halal certificate from a recognised certification body.
- A slaughterhouse approval, for meat and poultry.
- A health certificate from the exporting country's authority.
- A veterinary certificate, for animal products.
- The halal mark on the label.

The certificate must match the product, the plant and the shipment. A certificate for a different plant or a different product line is not valid. For meat, the slaughter method and the certifying body's recognition by the destination authority are critical.

## The cold chain

A reefer container is designed to hold temperature, not to pull it down. The cargo must be pre-cooled before loading.

The cold chain nodes:

1. **Production and cold storage** at origin.
2. **Pre-cooling and loading** into the reefer.
3. **Inland transport** to the Chinese port.
4. **Port storage** and vessel loading.
5. **Ocean transit** with the reefer set point.
6. **Discharge and port storage** at destination.
7. **Inspection** — the container may be opened.
8. **Inland delivery** to the warehouse.

Each node has a failure mode: a warm loading dock, a reefer that is not plugged in, a port that cannot supply power, an inspection that takes too long, a truck without a genset.

The controls:

- A temperature data logger in the container.
- A pre-trip inspection of the reefer.
- A written set point and tolerance.
- A port with reefer plugs and monitoring.
- A truck with a genset for the delivery.
- An insurance policy that covers spoilage.

## Customs and inspection

Food clearance involves both customs and the food authority. The sequence is usually:

1. Customs declaration and duty/VAT assessment.
2. Food authority document review.
3. Inspection and sampling, if selected.
4. Release or rejection.

Sampling can add days. A laboratory result may be required before release, and the container stays at the port while it is processed. For chilled cargo, that is a spoilage risk.

The importer should pre-alert the food authority, have the registration and label file ready, and book a berth with reefer capacity.

## Risks and failure modes

**Registration gap.** The product is not registered, and the shipment cannot be cleared.

**Label rejection.** Arabic or allergen information is missing or wrong.

**Shelf-life rejection.** The remaining shelf life at import is below the threshold.

**Cold-chain break.** A reefer failure, a port power outage or an unplugged container spoils the cargo.

**Halal certificate mismatch.** The certificate does not cover the plant or the product.

**Inspection sampling delay.** The laboratory result takes longer than the shelf life allows.

**Rejection and destruction.** The importer bears the cost of return or destruction.

## How to act: a food shipment checklist

1. **Register the product** with the destination authority before production.
2. **Approve the Arabic label** with the importer and the authority.
3. **Confirm the shelf life** at arrival meets the destination rule.
4. **Confirm the halal and health certificates** for the product and plant.
5. **Book the reefer** early and confirm the set point.
6. **Pre-cool the cargo** before loading.
7. **Use a temperature logger** and keep the record.
8. **Pre-alert the importer and the food authority.**
9. **Insure the cargo** for spoilage.

## Our read

Food is a market for specialists. The registration, labelling and cold-chain work is disproportionate to the first shipment, and a single rejection can wipe out the margin on a season.

The shippers who succeed build a product file once and reuse it: registration, label artwork, halal certificate, health certificate, shelf-life calculation and a reefer booking template. The ones who fail treat food as a commodity with a freight rate.

Saudi Arabia is the largest market and the strictest. The UAE is the easiest entry point, but the emirate-level registration means "UAE approval" is not a single thing. For a first launch, pick one emirate and one product, get the file right, then expand.

## FAQ

**Do I need SFDA registration to import food into Saudi Arabia?**
Yes. Food products need registration with SFDA, and the importer must hold the relevant licence. Arabic labelling and shelf-life rules apply.

**Is a UAE food registration valid in all emirates?**
Not automatically. Dubai Municipality, ADAFSA and the other emirate authorities have separate registration and inspection regimes.

**What is the minimum shelf life for GCC imports?**
It varies by country and category. A common requirement is a proportion of the total shelf life remaining at import, sometimes half. Confirm the specific rule before shipping.

**Do I need halal certification?**
For meat, poultry and some processed foods, yes. The certificate must be from a recognised body and match the plant and product.

**Can I ship chilled food by sea?**
Yes, in a reefer container with a controlled set point, but the transit time and the shelf life must be compatible. Air is used for short-life and high-value products.

**What happens if the food is rejected?**
The importer must re-export, destroy or, in some cases, re-label the cargo. The cost is borne by the importer or the seller, depending on the contract.

**Why is my reefer container not cold?**
The cargo may not have been pre-cooled, the reefer may not have been plugged in, or the set point may be wrong. A temperature logger shows where the break occurred.
