## Direct answer

Furniture is a volume business, not a weight business. The freight cost is driven by cubic metres, container utilisation and damage, not by the ocean rate per tonne. Use FCL for regular programmes and LCL for mixed or small orders, but check the destination charges before assuming LCL is cheaper. The biggest risks are moisture, corner damage and a loading plan that wastes 20% of the container.

## Why furniture pricing is different

A container of furniture can weigh less than a container of paper but fill the same space. Ocean carriers price per container, so the freight is fixed; the variable is how much product fits in the box.

That makes **loading efficiency** the most important cost lever. A 40HQ has about 76 cubic metres of internal volume, but usable volume is lower once you account for pallets, packing, irregular shapes and the door opening. A well-packed container can carry 60–68 cbm of product; a poorly packed one may carry 45–50 cbm. The difference is a 20–30% swing in freight per unit.

The second lever is **damage**. Furniture claims are common because the product is bulky, fragile and often finished. A damaged container can turn a profitable order into a loss.

## LCL versus FCL

**LCL** is priced per cubic metre or per tonne, with a minimum charge. It suits:

- Mixed orders from multiple suppliers.
- Small test orders.
- Products with a short season.
- Buyers without a full container.

**FCL** is priced per container. It suits:

- Regular programmes.
- A single supplier with a full container.
- High-value or fragile products where less handling is better.
- Products that fill a 40HQ or 45HQ.

The crossover is usually around 12–18 cbm, but it depends on the destination charges. LCL adds a consolidation and deconsolidation step, which adds cost, time and damage risk. For furniture, the destination charges can be a large share of the total, so a 15 cbm LCL shipment can cost more than a 20-foot FCL.

## Packaging: the part that decides the claim rate

Furniture packaging has four jobs: protect the surface, protect the corners, protect against moisture and keep the load stable.

**Corners.** Use corner protectors and edge boards. A corner crush is the most common damage claim.

**Surfaces.** Use foam, bubble wrap or a soft blanket, then a carton or a shrink wrap. Do not put tape directly on a finished surface.

**Moisture.** Use desiccant, a container liner or moisture-barrier bags. A container crossing from a cold Chinese winter to a hot Gulf summer can condense water on the cargo. Wood and upholstery are especially vulnerable.

**Compression.** Do not stack heavy items on soft furniture. Use a loading plan that distributes weight and fills voids.

**Wood packaging.** Pallets, crates and dunnage made of solid wood must meet ISPM 15: heat-treated or fumigated, and stamped with the IPPC mark. Plywood and processed wood are exempt in most cases, but the rule depends on the destination.

## Certification and standards

Furniture is less regulated than electronics or food, but it is not unregulated.

**Saudi Arabia.** SASO technical regulations cover some furniture categories, especially children's furniture, upholstered furniture and items with electrical components. Formaldehyde emission from wood panels is a growing area of scrutiny. SABER may apply to regulated categories.

**UAE.** ESMA and the emirate authorities regulate some furniture categories, including children's furniture and products with electrical parts. Fire safety of upholstery may be required for commercial projects.

**Kuwait, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain.** GSO standards and national requirements apply. Project furniture often has a specification that goes beyond the minimum.

The practical advice: confirm the category before production. A general "furniture" certificate may not cover a child's bed, a sofa with a motorised recliner or a desk with a built-in USB charger.

## Container loading

A good loading plan considers:

- **Product dimensions** and whether items nest or stack.
- **Weight distribution** — heavy items on the floor, centred over the axles.
- **Door security** — a barrier at the door to prevent shift.
- **Ventilation** — furniture needs air circulation to avoid condensation.
- **Unloading sequence** — the first item out should be at the door.
- **Documentation** — a packing list that matches the load.

For flat-pack furniture, the cartons are rectangular and load well. For assembled furniture, the shapes are irregular and the usable volume falls. A 3D loading plan is worth the cost for a mixed container.

## Risks and failure modes

**Moisture and mould.** The most common quality claim. A container without a liner or desiccant can condense water.

**Corner damage.** A loading plan without corner protection and void fill.

**Container shift.** Poor weight distribution or a missing door barrier.

**ISPM 15 non-compliance.** An unstamped pallet is refused or treated at the destination.

**Certification gap.** A regulated category, such as children's furniture, is shipped without the certificate.

**Unloading access.** A sofa that does not fit the lift or the stairwell. Measure the destination, not just the container.

**Project delay.** A hotel or residential project needs the furniture on a date; a late container can delay handover.

## How to act: a furniture shipment checklist

1. **Confirm the category and certification** — children's, upholstered, electrical.
2. **Measure the product and the destination** — doorways, lifts, stairwells.
3. **Design the packaging** — corners, surface, moisture, compression.
4. **Confirm ISPM 15** for wood packaging.
5. **Build a loading plan** — weight distribution and door barrier.
6. **Choose FCL or LCL** — compare total landed cost, not freight.
7. **Insure the cargo** — damage and general average.
8. **Book the delivery** — truck size, access and unloading.

## Our read

Furniture is a category where the factory's packaging and the forwarder's loading plan matter more than the ocean rate. A supplier who invests in corner protection, moisture control and a 3D loading plan can beat a competitor who saves 5% on freight and pays 15% in damage claims.

For the GCC, the UAE is the distribution hub and Saudi Arabia is the volume market. Project furniture — hotels, offices, residential compounds — is a separate business with specification, certification and delivery-date risk. Treat it as a project, not a container.

## FAQ

**Is LCL or FCL cheaper for furniture?**
It depends on volume and destination charges. Under 12–15 cbm, LCL is often cheaper on freight; above 18–20 cbm, FCL usually wins. Compare the total landed cost.

**How much furniture fits in a 40HQ?**
A 40HQ has about 76 cbm of internal volume, but usable product volume is typically 55–68 cbm depending on packaging and loading. A 20-foot container holds roughly half.

**Do I need ISPM 15?**
Yes, for solid wood packaging such as pallets, crates and dunnage. The wood must be heat-treated or fumigated and stamped with the IPPC mark.

**How do I prevent mould on furniture?**
Use a container liner or desiccant, avoid wet pallets, and do not load during rain. Wood and upholstery are vulnerable to condensation.

**Does furniture need SABER?**
Some categories do, especially children's furniture, upholstered furniture and items with electrical components. Confirm the SASO technical regulation for the specific product.

**Can I ship assembled furniture?**
Yes, but the usable container volume falls and the damage risk rises. Flat-pack is more efficient where the design allows it.

**Who pays for damage?**
It depends on the Incoterm and the insurance. Under CIF or DAP, the seller usually insures; under FOB, the buyer does. The claim requires photos, the packing list and the survey report.
