## Direct answer

GCC ecommerce is a last-mile business with a freight problem attached. Amazon.ae, Amazon.sa and noon dominate, and most serious sellers use FBA or a local 3PL rather than cross-border parcels. The import model matters: a B2B2C shipment to a fulfilment centre clears duty and VAT once, while a direct-to-consumer parcel clears per order. Certification, Arabic labelling and returns are the constraints that decide whether a SKU survives.

## The three import models

**Direct-to-consumer parcels.** The seller ships individual orders from China. The parcel clears as a low-value consignment, and the last mile is handled by a postal or courier partner. This model has the lowest upfront cost and the worst unit economics: per-parcel clearance, high shipping cost, slow delivery and a returns process that crosses borders.

**B2B2C to a fulfilment centre.** The seller ships a bulk consignment to Amazon FBA, noon or a 3PL in the GCC. The shipment clears once, duty and VAT are paid on the bulk value, and the marketplace handles the last mile. This is the standard model for serious sellers.

**Free-zone fulfilment.** The seller holds stock in a UAE free zone and ships to customers across the GCC. The free zone defers duty and VAT until the goods leave for the mainland, and it can support cross-border returns. It suits regional sellers with multiple markets.

The B2B2C model usually wins on unit economics, but it requires a customs importer, a VAT registration and a product file. A seller who is not ready for that should start with a 3PL rather than FBA.

## VAT and customs

**UAE.** Import VAT is 5% on CIF plus duty. A VAT-registered business can recover it. Sales VAT is 5%, and Amazon or noon may collect it on the seller's behalf depending on the model.

**Saudi Arabia.** Import VAT is 15% on CIF plus duty. A VAT-registered business can recover it. Sales VAT is 15%. The seller needs a Saudi VAT registration if it is selling into Saudi Arabia and holding stock there.

**Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, Bahrain.** VAT is 0% in Qatar and Kuwait, 5% in Oman and 10% in Bahrain. Each market has its own customs and marketplace landscape.

The practical point: **import VAT is a cash-flow cost, not a permanent cost**, if the seller is registered and can recover it. A seller who is not registered absorbs the VAT in the margin.

## Customs thresholds and formal entry

Low-value consignments often have a simplified clearance procedure, but "simplified" does not mean "free". Duty and VAT may still apply, and the courier may charge a clearance fee.

Above the threshold, a formal entry is required. That means:

- A customs declaration with an HS code.
- A commercial invoice and packing list.
- A certificate of origin, where required.
- A conformity certificate for regulated products.
- An importer of record with a local registration.

For FBA and 3PL shipments, the seller or the 3PL acts as the importer. The seller must provide the product file and the invoice, and the 3PL must be licensed to import the category.

## Product certification and restricted categories

Marketplaces enforce category rules, and the GCC authorities enforce product rules. The two are not the same.

**Saudi Arabia.** SABER PCoC/SCoC for regulated products. SFDA for food, cosmetics and medical devices. CITC for connected devices. SASO for toys, electricals and many consumer goods.

**UAE.** ESMA/ECAS for regulated products. Dubai Municipality for food and cosmetics. TDRA for telecoms.

**Restricted categories.** Batteries, power banks, e-scooters, cosmetics, food, supplements, medical devices, laser products and some toys are restricted or require approval. A marketplace may delist a product that is not compliant, and a customs authority may seize it.

The most common ecommerce compliance failure is a product that is legal in China and the EU but not registered in the GCC. The second is a label that is in English only, or a product that lacks the Arabic warning.

## Labelling and packaging

Marketplace labelling:

- **FNSKU or marketplace barcode** on each unit.
- **Carton labels** with the shipment ID and quantity.
- **Poly bag** with a suffocation warning, where required.
- **Expiry date** for food, cosmetics and some consumables.
- **Arabic label** for regulated products and consumer goods.

The packaging must survive the last mile. A poly bag that tears, a box that crushes or a product that leaks creates a return and a negative review. Test the packaging with a drop test before the first shipment.

## Last mile and returns

The GCC last mile is fragmented. Amazon and noon have their own networks; other sellers use Aramex, Emirates Post, Saudi Post or a regional courier. Delivery times vary from same-day in Dubai to several days in secondary Saudi cities.

Cash on delivery is still significant in Saudi Arabia, which adds a reconciliation and fraud risk. Prepaid orders are cheaper to serve.

Returns are the hidden cost:

- **Return rate.** 5–15% is normal for fashion and electronics; lower for consumables.
- **Restocking.** The marketplace or 3PL charges a fee.
- **Inspection.** A returned item must be checked before resale.
- **Disposal.** Unsellable returns are destroyed or liquidated.
- **Cross-border returns.** Sending a return to China is usually uneconomic.

The returns process should be designed before the first sale, not after the first return.

## Unit economics: the model that matters

A simple unit economics model:

1. **Landed cost** — product + freight + duty + VAT (if not recovered) + certification.
2. **Marketplace fee** — referral fee, usually a percentage of the sale price.
3. **Fulfilment fee** — FBA or 3PL pick-and-pack.
4. **Last-mile fee** — included in FBA, separate for 3PL.
5. **Storage fee** — monthly, plus long-term storage.
6. **Returns reserve** — 5–15% of revenue.
7. **Marketing** — advertising cost per sale.
8. **Payment and FX** — card fees and currency conversion.

If the contribution margin after those lines is below 20%, the SKU is fragile. A freight increase or a returns spike can turn it negative.

## Risks and failure modes

**Certification gap.** A product is listed but not registered, and the shipment is held or delisted.

**VAT registration gap.** The seller holds stock and sells without a VAT registration, and the marketplace or the authority flags it.

**Importer-of-record gap.** The 3PL cannot import the category, and the shipment is refused.

**Inventory mismatch.** The seller overstocks a slow SKU and pays long-term storage.

**Returns spiral.** A sizing or quality issue creates a return rate that erases the margin.

**Counterfeit or IP claim.** A marketplace suspends the listing or the account.

**Packaging failure.** A product arrives damaged, and the review score falls.

## How to act: a launch checklist

1. **Choose the model** — direct parcel, B2B2C or free zone.
2. **Confirm the product certification** for each market.
3. **Register for VAT** if holding stock.
4. **Appoint an importer** — the 3PL, the marketplace or the seller's local entity.
5. **Design the label and packaging** — FNSKU, Arabic, drop test.
6. **Build the unit economics** — landed cost, fees, returns, marketing.
7. **Plan the inventory** — 6–8 weeks before Ramadan and White Friday.
8. **Set up returns** — inspection, restocking, disposal.

## Displaying VAT and duty in the price

GCC marketplaces display prices inclusive of VAT, and the buyer expects the price at checkout to be the price paid. That creates a pricing decision for the seller: absorb the import VAT and duty into the price, or pass them through as a separate line.

For a VAT-registered seller holding stock, the import VAT is recoverable, so the true cost is the duty plus the cash-flow timing. For an unregistered seller or a direct-to-consumer parcel, the VAT is a real cost.

The display rules matter. A price that excludes VAT or duty can be misleading, and a marketplace may remove a listing that does not show the final price. The seller should model the inclusive price, the marketplace fee, the fulfilment fee and the returns reserve before setting the retail price.

A second issue is currency. Amazon.ae prices in dirhams and Amazon.sa in riyals. The seller's costs are in dollars or yuan. A currency swing can change the margin without any change in the freight rate.

## Our read

GCC ecommerce rewards sellers who treat it as a retail business with a logistics cost, not as a freight arbitrage. The winners have a narrow catalogue, a certified product, a local importer and a returns process. The losers ship a container of unregistered SKUs to a 3PL and discover the category rules after the listing goes live.

The UAE is the test market: small, dense, high-income and easy to serve from Dubai. Saudi Arabia is the scale market: larger, more regulated and more expensive to serve outside the main cities. A seller who can make the unit economics work in Riyadh and Jeddah can usually make them work across the GCC.

## FAQ

**Can I ship directly from China to GCC customers?**
Yes, but the per-parcel cost and the returns process usually make it uneconomic at scale. Most sellers use FBA or a 3PL.

**Do I need a VAT registration to sell on Amazon UAE or Saudi?**
If you hold stock and sell in the market, yes. The marketplace may collect sales VAT, but import VAT and the seller's own registration are separate.

**What is the customs threshold for ecommerce parcels?**
It varies by country and changes. Low-value consignments may use a simplified procedure, but duty and VAT can still apply. Confirm with the courier or broker.

**Can I sell batteries or power banks?**
Only with the right DG documentation, carrier approval and marketplace category approval. Many marketplaces restrict or prohibit them.

**How much does FBA cost?**
Referral fees, fulfilment fees, storage fees and optional advertising. The total varies by category and size. Build the unit economics before launch.

**What is the return rate in the GCC?**
5–15% is normal, depending on the category. Fashion and electronics are higher; consumables are lower.

**Can I use a UAE free zone for Saudi sales?**
Yes, but Saudi duty, VAT and SABER apply when the goods enter Saudi Arabia. The free zone defers UAE duty, not Saudi duty.
