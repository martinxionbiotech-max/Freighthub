## The thesis

The Red Sea story is usually told as a binary: the route is open or closed, rates are high or low, the crisis is over or it is not. The reality for a Gulf shipper is messier. A partial return to Suez improves some things, leaves others unchanged, and creates a new set of decisions about how much to rely on the improvement.

The most important thing it does not change is the Gulf's dependence on the Strait of Hormuz. A container bound for Jebel Ali, Dammam, Hamad or Shuwaikh does not need Suez. It needs Hormuz. The Red Sea and Suez are upstream of the Gulf in the network, not on the final approach.

## What changes

**Schedule reliability.** A shorter Asia–Europe voyage absorbs less vessel capacity and fewer containers. When more services return to Suez, the global equipment pool rotates faster, and the Gulf gets some of that benefit through feeder connections.

**Transit on some strings.** Services that do transit Suez are faster. A proforma that was 40 days on the Cape route can return to 30–35 days on Suez, depending on the port rotation.

**Rate sentiment.** A return to Suez is bearish for rates. More capacity, more confidence and more competition tend to soften the market. That does not mean rates fall immediately; it means the direction of travel changes.

**War-risk pricing.** If the perceived risk falls, insurance and surcharges should follow. They usually lag the news, and they can spike again quickly.

## What does not change

**The Hormuz exposure.** The Gulf ports are inside the strait. A Suez return does not create an alternative approach. The only real bypasses are the UAE east coast (Khor Fakkan, Fujairah) and Oman (Sohar, Salalah), and both have landside constraints.

**The customs regimes.** SABER, ECAS, KUCAS, QGOSM, Fasah, Al Nadeeb and the emirate systems are unaffected by the route. A faster vessel does not clear a certificate mismatch.

**The inland leg.** Jeddah to Riyadh is still about 950 km. Dammam to Riyadh is still about 400 km. Khor Fakkan to Dubai is still roughly two hours. The route change does not move the consignee.

**The port choice.** Jebel Ali is still the region's transhipment hub. Khalifa is still Abu Dhabi's gateway. The relative economics of the ports do not change because a vessel takes a different sea route.

## The trap: assuming normalisation is linear

The most common mistake is to assume that a partial return will continue in a straight line. It may not. Red Sea risk is a security and insurance question, not a schedule question, and it can change faster than a carrier can re-plan a network.

A shipper who signs a 12-month contract on the assumption of a full return is taking a position on a security outcome. A shipper who keeps a second routing option is buying an option, not a forecast.

## What to do now

1. **Check the proforma, not the narrative.** Ask for the transit time on the exact service and the routing.
2. **Keep two routings.** Direct and Jebel Ali transhipment, or Jebel Ali and Khor Fakkan, depending on the destination.
3. **Price the war-risk line separately.** It is the fastest-moving part of the quote, and it should be visible.
4. **Do not build a contract on a single assumption.** Include a rate-review and a force-majeure clause that covers routing changes.
5. **Watch the equipment, not just the rate.** A softer rate with no equipment is not a better market.

## The Gulf-specific view

For a Gulf shipper, the Red Sea is a second-order variable. The first-order variables are Hormuz, port congestion, customs and the inland leg. The Red Sea matters because it changes the network that feeds the Gulf, not because it is on the Gulf's doorstep.

That is why the Gulf market can be soft while the Red Sea is still disrupted, and tight while the Red Sea is calm. The correlation is real but loose.

## Our read

A partial Suez return is good news, but it is not a return to normal. The Gulf remains a Hormuz market with a customs problem and a long inland leg. The shippers who benefit most are the ones who use the improvement to renegotiate rates and restore schedule reliability, without assuming that the risk has disappeared.

The ones who lose are the ones who sign a long contract at a spot-rate assumption and discover that the next disruption is priced into someone else's margin.

## A worked example: two routings

Consider a 40-foot container from Ningbo to Riyadh, booked in two ways.

**Routing A: Suez-linked service to Jeddah, then road to Riyadh.** The vessel transits Suez, calls Jeddah, and the container is cleared at the Red Sea port. The road leg is about 950 km. If the Suez service is running on a 30–35 day proforma, the container arrives at Jeddah in about five weeks; clearance and road add another week. The cost includes the ocean freight, Jeddah terminal charges, Saudi duty and VAT, the broker fee and the truck.

**Routing B: direct to Dammam, then rail to Riyadh Dry Port.** The vessel calls Dammam, the container is railed to Riyadh and cleared inland. The ocean proforma is similar, but the land leg is about 400 km and the rail slot is the constraint. The cost includes Dammam terminal charges, the rail leg, the dry-port charges, the broker fee and a short truck.

If Suez normalises, Routing A becomes more reliable and possibly cheaper on the ocean leg. Routing B remains competitive because the inland leg is shorter and the rail slot, when available, is efficient. The Red Sea change affects the ocean proforma; it does not change the 950 km versus 400 km difference.

Now add a disruption. If the Red Sea risk rises, Routing A's war-risk surcharge and transit time increase, and Routing B becomes relatively more attractive. If Dammam is congested, the reverse happens. The two routings are a hedge against each other, which is why keeping both is useful even when one looks cheaper.

The same logic applies to Jebel Ali versus a direct call, or to Khor Fakkan versus Jebel Ali. The route is a portfolio, not a single bet.

The portfolio view also explains why a shipper can be right about the Red Sea and wrong about the rate. A return to Suez may lower the ocean rate on one lane while a port congestion event raises the inland cost on the same lane. The two effects can cancel, and the shipper who only watches the Red Sea misses the port.
