## The thesis

DDP is sold as certainty: one price, one invoice, no customs surprises. In the GCC, it often delivers the opposite. The price is fixed, but the structure behind it is fragile — an importer of record who may not be the buyer, a VAT charge that may not be recoverable, and a product certificate that may not cover the shipment.

The risk does not disappear because the price is fixed. It moves to a party that did not negotiate it.

## What DDP promises

Under Incoterms 2020, DDP means the seller delivers the goods cleared for import, ready for unloading, with duty and taxes paid. The buyer's only obligation is to take delivery and pay.

That is a coherent contract in a market where the seller can be the importer: the EU, the UK, the US. It is a much less coherent contract in the GCC, where the importer of record must normally be a licensed local entity with a commercial registration and a customs account.

A Chinese seller cannot simply declare itself the Saudi importer. It needs one of three structures:

1. The buyer is the importer, and the seller reimburses the duty and VAT.
2. The forwarder's local entity is the importer and resells to the buyer.
3. A nominated local importer is used.

Each structure is legal if it is documented. Each is fragile if it is not.

## The importer-of-record illusion

The most common DDP structure is the first: the buyer is the importer of record, and the seller pays the duty and VAT through the forwarder. The buyer's name is on the declaration, the buyer's VAT registration is used, and the buyer's customs account is debited.

The buyer thinks it bought a delivered product. The customs record says it imported the goods. If the declaration is wrong — a misclassified HS code, an understated value, a missing certificate — the authority penalises the importer, which is the buyer.

The seller, meanwhile, has priced the risk into the DDP margin. If the risk materialises, the buyer may be the one who pays the penalty, while the seller's exposure is limited to a commercial dispute.

That is not a theoretical problem. It is the structure behind a large share of the "all-in" quotes in the market.

## The VAT trap

VAT is the second hidden risk. The GCC rates are 15% in Saudi Arabia, 5% in the UAE, 5% in Oman, 10% in Bahrain and 0% in Qatar and Kuwait.

A DDP price that includes VAT is only correct if the seller knows who can recover it. If the buyer is VAT-registered and is the importer, the buyer can recover the VAT, but the seller has already included it in the price. The buyer gets a windfall and the seller loses the margin — unless the contract says otherwise.

If the seller is the importer through a local entity, the seller may be able to recover the VAT, but only if it has a VAT registration and a valid tax invoice. Many DDP structures do not.

The clean approach is to state, in the contract:

- Who is the importer of record.
- Who pays duty and VAT at the border.
- Whether VAT is included in the price and on what base.
- Who receives the customs declaration and the tax invoice.
- What happens if the rate changes.

Without those five lines, the DDP price is an estimate dressed as a fixed price.

## The certificate mismatch

The third risk is certification. A SABER PCoC in Saudi Arabia, an ECAS certificate in the UAE or a KUCAS certificate in Kuwait is issued to a specific product, manufacturer and often importer. A DDP structure that uses a different importer can invalidate the certificate for the shipment.

The symptom is familiar: the cargo arrives, the certificate exists, but the name on the certificate is not the name on the declaration. The SCoC cannot be issued, the container sits, and the seller and the buyer argue about who should have checked.

The certificate is not a document that can be transferred at the last minute. It is part of the product file, and it must be aligned with the importer before the shipment.

## Why the market keeps selling DDP

DDP sells because it solves a real problem: many buyers do not have an import function, and many sellers want a simple price for a small order. For samples, ecommerce and low-value goods, DDP is a genuine convenience.

It also sells because the risk is invisible at the point of sale. The buyer sees a price, not a customs declaration. The seller sees a margin, not a liability. The forwarder sees a transaction, not a compliance file.

The risk becomes visible only when something goes wrong: a valuation query, a certificate mismatch, a VAT assessment. At that point, the contract is already signed and the cargo is already at the port.

## When DDP is fine

DDP works when the amounts are small and the compliance burden is low:

- Samples and trade-show cargo.
- Ecommerce parcels with a local fulfilment partner.
- Low-value consumables.
- Buyers without an import licence, for a first order.
- Sellers with a local entity that can be the importer.

In those cases, the convenience is worth the margin, and the risk is proportionate.

## When to use DAP instead

DAP is the better contract for most established GCC importers. The seller delivers to the named place, and the buyer clears and pays duty. The buyer, who has the licence and the VAT registration, controls the declaration and recovers the VAT.

DAP does not eliminate risk. It allocates it to the party best placed to manage it. That is what a good Incoterm should do.

## Our read

DDP is not dishonest, but it is often mis-sold. The price looks like certainty, and the structure behind it is a set of assumptions about the importer, the certificate and the VAT that may not hold.

The test is simple: ask who is the importer of record, and ask to see the customs declaration and the tax invoice. If the answer is vague, the DDP price is not a price; it is a hope.

For most GCC importers, DAP plus a good local broker is the better deal. For small buyers and ecommerce, DDP is the right customer experience — provided the structure is documented.

## A worked example

An importer buys a container of electrical goods from a Chinese supplier on DDP terms. The price includes duty and VAT. The supplier's forwarder uses the importer's customs profile to clear the goods, then invoices the supplier for the duty and VAT.

The importer later discovers three things:

1. The HS code used at the border was for a lower-duty category than the product actually is. The authority reassesses the duty and applies a penalty to the importer of record.
2. The VAT was paid through the forwarder, but the tax invoice is in the forwarder's name. The importer cannot recover it.
3. The SABER certificate is registered to a different importer, so the shipment cannot be released without a new SCoC.

The supplier's DDP price covered none of those outcomes. The contract said "DDP", but it did not say who was the importer, whose certificate it was or whose name was on the tax invoice. The importer bore the legal consequences; the supplier bore a commercial argument.

That is the risk DDP hides: not the freight, but the identity of the party the customs system holds responsible.
