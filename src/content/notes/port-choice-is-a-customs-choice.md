## The thesis

Ask a shipper why it chose Jebel Ali over Khalifa, or Jeddah over Dammam, and the answer is usually a rate, a sailing or a transit time. Those are the visible variables. The variable that actually decides the landed cost is the customs regime behind the gate: which authority files the declaration, which importer is on the record, whether the goods are in a free zone or the mainland, and where the duty and VAT are paid.

Ports within the same country can have different customs authorities. Ports in different countries can have radically different importer rules. A rate comparison that ignores those differences is not a comparison; it is a quote for two different products.

## The UAE: one country, several customs regimes

The UAE is the clearest example. Jebel Ali is administered by Dubai Customs. Khalifa is administered by Abu Dhabi Customs. Sharjah's ports are administered by Sharjah Customs. A Dubai trade licence does not automatically cover an Abu Dhabi import, and a Dubai broker who files at Jebel Ali weekly may file at Khalifa monthly.

That produces a strange result: the ocean rate to Khalifa can be 5% lower than to Jebel Ali, and the total cost can be 10% higher for a Dubai consignee, because the truck crosses an emirate border and the empty container returns to a different depot. The reverse is true for an Abu Dhabi consignee.

The free-zone layer adds another dimension. Jafza and KEZAD are not just industrial parks; they are customs territories with their own entry and exit procedures. A shipment into a free zone is not the same import as a shipment into the mainland. The duty and VAT treatment differs, and the documentation must show which one you mean.

## Saudi Arabia: the port is also a clearance model

Jeddah and Dammam are both Saudi ports under ZATCA and Fasah, but they are different clearance models.

Jeddah is a gateway port inside a city. The container is discharged, cleared at the port and trucked to the consignee. The landside is the constraint, and the 15-day transit rule for onward cargo adds a hard deadline.

Dammam is an industrial port with a rail link to Riyadh Dry Port. For a Riyadh consignee, the container can be railed inland and cleared at the dry port, which changes both the clearance location and the inland cost. The customs authority is the same, but the procedure is not.

A shipper who compares Jeddah and Dammam on ocean freight alone will miss the rail slot, the dry-port release process and the 950 km versus 400 km land leg. Those are customs-and-inland variables, not shipping variables.

## Bahrain: the causeway is a border

Bahrain's Khalifa Bin Salman Port is a short drive from Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province. That proximity makes it look like a cheaper gateway to the Saudi market. Sometimes it is. Often it is not.

The cargo can enter Bahrain as a Bahrain import, paying 5% duty and 10% VAT, and then be sold into Saudi Arabia, paying Saudi duty and 15% VAT. Or it can move under a transit or free-zone procedure, with Saudi duty and VAT paid at the causeway. The first structure can double the VAT and the compliance cost. The second requires the documentation to be right before arrival.

The port choice is therefore a customs-status choice. A forwarder who quotes "Bahrain" without asking whether the cargo is Bahrain-bound or Saudi-bound has not understood the route.

## Why the industry compares the wrong thing

Freight procurement is organized around rates. A forwarder is measured on the ocean freight it negotiates, and a carrier is measured on the rate per container. The customs regime sits outside that scorecard, even though it can be a larger share of the landed cost than the freight.

There is also a data problem. Ocean rates are published, indexed and compared daily. Customs rules are published but fragmented across authorities, and their operational interpretation varies by port, broker and product. It is easier to compare what is measurable than what is material.

The result is a market where a 5% freight saving gets attention and a 15% VAT double-charge gets discovered after arrival.

## What to compare instead

A useful port comparison has five layers:

1. **The customs authority and the broker.** Who files, and do they file at that port weekly?
2. **The importer of record.** Does the consignee's licence cover the port, the emirate and the product?
3. **The free-zone status.** Is the cargo a mainland import, a free-zone entry or a transit movement?
4. **The inland leg.** Distance, border, empty return and delivery access.
5. **The duty and VAT.** Rate, base, recovery and cash-flow timing.

Only after those five should the ocean rate be compared. The rate is the smallest of the six numbers in most landed-cost models; it is the easiest to see.

## The counter-argument

There is a legitimate case for rate-first procurement. For a large, repeatable shipment of a simple product into a single market, the customs regime is stable and the rate is the main variable. A retailer shipping the same SKU to the same warehouse every month does not need to re-litigate the customs model on every booking.

But that is a mature, narrow case. For a new market, a new product, a new port or a new importer, the customs model is the decision. The rate is a detail that can be optimised later.

## Our read

The Gulf is not a single market with several ports. It is several customs markets with ports attached. Shippers who treat the port as the decision and customs as the paperwork consistently overpay, because they optimise the visible line and ignore the material one.

The practical advice is to choose the customs model first: which importer, which authority, which zone status, which inland clearance point. Then choose the port that fits. Then negotiate the rate.

That sequence is slower at the start and cheaper at the end.

## A worked example

Take the same 40-foot container, the same Chinese supplier and the same product, and send it to two consignees: one in Dubai, one in Abu Dhabi.

To Dubai, the Jebel Ali route is obvious. The port is in the same emirate, the broker files there weekly, the truck is short and the empty return is local. The total cost is the ocean freight plus the standard destination charges plus a short truck.

To Abu Dhabi, the Jebel Ali route adds a cross-emirate truck and a different empty-return depot. The Khalifa route may have a slightly higher ocean freight, but the truck is short and the broker is local. The total can be lower through Khalifa even though the ocean rate is higher.

Now change one variable: the Abu Dhabi consignee is a KEZAD tenant. The cargo enters a free zone, stays duty-suspended and leaves for the mainland later. The customs status, not the port, becomes the main cost driver. The same exercise applies in Saudi Arabia, Bahrain and Oman.

The lesson is that the port is one line in a landed-cost model. The customs status is the model.
