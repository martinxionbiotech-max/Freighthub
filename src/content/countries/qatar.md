## Direct answer

China to Qatar is a one-port market. Almost every container enters through [Hamad Port](/ports/hamad/), south of Doha, and almost every container reaches Hamad through the Strait of Hormuz. That single fact explains more about your transit time and your risk premium than any rate sheet.

Ocean transit from Shanghai, Ningbo or Shekou is commonly 18–25 days on a direct rotation and 24–34 days with transshipment *(industry claim)*. Add two to five days for clearance and inland delivery. Duty is 5% on CIF for most goods. VAT is zero — Qatar still has no VAT — which makes a DDP quote simpler to build than a Saudi or UAE one, but not cheaper. The real gates are service frequency, Hormuz risk and QGOSM conformity, not tax.

## What changed in the last 60 days

Qatar's freight story in late 2026 is about volume recovery and regional risk, not about new infrastructure.

- **7 July 2026** — Mwani Qatar reported 493,105 TEU across Hamad, Old Doha and Al Ruwais for January–June 2026, with 866 vessel calls, 273,325 tonnes of general cargo and 112,492 head of livestock. June alone cleared more than 78,000 TEU, up 13% on May.
- **16 July 2026** — Qatar issued a decree updating its integrated customs tariff. The direction is upward on selected lines, not a general liberalisation. Any 2025 duty assumption needs rechecking.
- **24 July 2026** — Kuwait's four-year fiscal plan dropped VAT, confirming that Qatar and Kuwait remain the only GCC states without it. For Qatar this is continuity, not news, but it keeps the DDP arithmetic distinct from Saudi Arabia and the UAE.
- **21 September 2026** — QatarEnergy said the Hormuz crisis could delay parts of the North Field LNG expansion, with several NFE trains now due in 2027 and NFS later. This is the clearest evidence yet that the strait is a project-cargo variable, not background noise.
- **3 November 2026** — Qatar confirmed a duty increase on selected fresh and chilled chicken from 5% to 18%, effective 15 November 2026. A narrow line, but a reminder that the tariff is being actively edited.
- **October 2026 volumes** — 59,000-plus TEU, up 9% month on month, with 249 vessel calls and general cargo up 64% to more than 66,000 tonnes. The mix is shifting toward project and bulk cargo, which is consistent with the LNG build-out.

The through-line: Qatar is not short of berths. It is short of direct weekly services, and it is exposed to a single maritime chokepoint while its largest industrial programme depends on that chokepoint staying open.

## The capacity paradox: seven and a half million TEU of port, one million TEU of cargo

Hamad Port was designed for a country that might need to replace every other route it had. The 2017–2021 blockade did that to Qatar: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain and Egypt cut land, sea and air links overnight. Hamad Port, which opened in 2016, became the replacement. The design capacity of 7.5 million TEU a year is not a forecast of Qatari import demand. It is a strategic reserve.

The arithmetic is stark. If the H1 2026 run rate holds, Qatar's entire port network will clear roughly one million TEU this year against a design capacity seven times larger. Even allowing for the fact that nameplate capacity is never fully usable, Hamad is structurally underutilised.

For a shipper, that has three consequences, and only one of them is good.

The good one: **berth congestion is rarely your problem.** Unlike Jeddah in 2026, Hamad does not queue ships for days. If your container is on a vessel that calls Hamad, it will usually berth and discharge on schedule.

The bad one: **frequency is your problem.** A port with spare capacity attracts transshipment, not necessarily more direct calls. Qatar's own volumes are too thin to justify the same number of China loops that Jebel Ali or Jeddah support. That is why much China–Qatar cargo still relays through Jebel Ali, Colombo or Singapore, and why the gap between a 19-day and a 30-day door-to-door is often a connection, not a sailing.

The strategic one: **CT2 was built as a transshipment terminal, not an import terminal.** QTerminals describes both CT1 and CT2 with the same 1,200 m berth and 17 m draft, but CT2's commercial logic is relay cargo — boxes moving between third countries, not boxes consumed in Qatar. That puts Hamad in direct competition with Jebel Ali, Khalifa and Sohar for a finite pool of relay volume. Ports win that competition on cost, connectivity and dwell time, not on depth.

This is the right question to ask your forwarder: not "how fast is the vessel" but "is this a direct call or a relay, and what is the cut-off for the connection?"

## Why Qatar has no plan B for Hormuz

Look at the map. Saudi Arabia has Jeddah and the Red Sea. The UAE has Fujairah and Khor Fakkan outside the strait. Oman has Salalah and Sohar, both outside. Qatar is a peninsula inside the Gulf with one maritime door, and Kuwait and Bahrain share the same exposure.

For Qatar, Hormuz is not a risk scenario. It is the only route. There is no Red Sea alternative, no overland corridor of meaningful scale and no second deepwater container port.

The overland option is the Abu Samra crossing into Saudi Arabia. In theory, cargo can discharge at Jeddah or Dammam and move by truck to Doha. In practice, that route adds a border crossing, a Saudi transit document set, a second customs regime and five to nine days — and it depends on Saudi cooperation that was suspended for three and a half years. Since the Al Ula declaration in January 2021 the border has worked, but no Qatari planner treats it as a primary supply route.

The practical implications are specific:

- **War-risk and conflict surcharges hit Qatar-bound cargo as a single event, not a gradual pass-through.** When carriers reprice Gulf risk, they reprice per box. A low-value, high-volume product absorbs that worse than a high-value one.
- **Project cargo is the most exposed category.** The North Field expansion moves turbines, modules and heavy lifts through the same strait. QatarEnergy's September statement that the Hormuz crisis could delay LNG expansion is a signal about schedule, not just price.
- **Inventory policy has to carry the risk.** For importers, the answer is not a cheaper route — there isn't one — but more days of cover on the SKUs that cannot air-freight, and a pre-agreed air fallback for the ones that can.

## What the blockade still teaches about Qatar routing

The 2017–2021 blockade ended more than five years ago, but its freight legacy is still visible in how Qatar buys logistics. First, **Qatar keeps its own shipping capacity.** Milaha, the national shipping and logistics company, is a 49% shareholder in QTerminals and operates its own services and agencies. That is not normal for a country of three million people. It is a hedge against a repeat of 2017, when foreign carriers stopped calling.

Second, **food security became a logistics programme.** The blockade turned Qatar from a country that imported most of its fresh food through Saudi Arabia into one that built dairy, poultry and greenhouse capacity and diversified its import origins. For Chinese exporters of food-processing equipment and cold-chain kit, that programme is still running.

The lesson is unglamorous: Qatar rewards relationships. A forwarder with a nominated allocation into Hamad will outperform a spot booking every time the region tightens.

## How to act: five decisions before you book

**1. Choose direct or relay deliberately.** Ask for the vessel name, the rotation and the transshipment port. If the answer is "via Jebel Ali", price the connection risk: a missed feeder can add five to seven days, and the feeder space is not protected by your ocean B/L.

**2. Confirm the HS line and the duty before you quote DDP.** Qatar's tariff was amended in July 2026 and again in November for poultry. Most lines remain at 5%, but "most" is not a quote. Get the HS heading, the current rate and the valuation basis in writing.

**3. Decide who holds the QGOSM certificate.** For regulated products, the CoC is issued before shipment by an approved body. If your factory has never done one, budget two to three weeks and a sample test. Do not discover this at the destination.

**4. Separate food from non-food.** Food needs its own CoC through the Ministry of Public Health, plus Arabic labelling and shelf-life requirements. A general QGOSM certificate will not clear a food consignment.

**5. Build the inland leg into the price.** Hamad is outside Doha. Delivery to the city, the industrial area or the free zones is a truck leg with its own gate cut-off and its own demurrage clock. A "port-to-port" quote that stops at Hamad is not a delivered price.

## Cost and transit drivers: where the money actually goes

Ocean freight is visible; it is rarely the largest line. A typical China→Qatar FCL shipment splits roughly like this *(editorial judgment based on quote structures, not a rate survey)*:

| Cost bucket | Share of landed cost | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean freight and BAF | 25–40% | Hormuz risk premium, season, carrier, direct versus relay |
| Origin charges and documentation | 3–6% | THC, VGM, certificate of origin, booking amendments |
| Destination THC and port charges | 5–9% | Container type, terminal, prepaid versus collect |
| Customs duty | 3–8% of CIF | HS classification, valuation, the 2026 tariff amendments |
| Clearance and inspection | 2–5% | Document accuracy, product category, QGOSM gaps |
| Inland delivery | 5–12% | Doha versus industrial areas, gate queues, truck availability |
| Demurrage, storage, detention | 0–18% | Free time, release speed, whether the consignee is ready |

Three structural points explain most of the dispersion.

**The risk premium is per box.** When the Strait of Hormuz tightens, carriers add conflict surcharges in the hundreds to low thousands of US dollars per FEU. That is regressive: it costs the same on a container of telecom equipment and a container of cement. Low-value dense cargo should model a two- to three-week inventory buffer rather than assume the spot rate will normalise.

**Direct calls carry a frequency discount.** A direct China–Hamad rotation is faster on the water but may sail fortnightly. A relay through Jebel Ali sails weekly but adds a connection. The correct comparison is 80th-percentile door-to-door time, not the advertised transit.

**Air is a different product.** China–Doha air freight is typically three to six days door to door and priced per kilo with a volumetric minimum. It works for launch inventory, spare parts and high-value electronics; it does not work for furniture or machinery.

## Ports and routing: one container port, three supporting facilities

**Hamad Port** is the container gateway: two 1,200 m container berths at 17 m draft, a general cargo terminal, a grain terminal and a free zone adjacent to the quay.

**Al Ruwais Port** is the northern gateway, close to the Saudi border and the north-field industrial area. It handles general cargo, livestock and some feeder traffic. For northern destinations it can save a day of trucking; for Doha it is the wrong end of the country.

**Mesaieed Port** is the industrial port: petrochemicals, LNG-adjacent cargo, project heavy lifts and the industrial city south of Doha. It is not a general container gateway, but a large share of the LNG expansion's breakbulk and heavy modules will arrive there.

For routing, the practical rule is simple. If the cargo is containerised and destined for Doha or the free zones, Hamad is the answer. If it is project cargo for the industrial belt, Mesaieed or Hamad depending on the lift. If it is destined for the north, Al Ruwais is worth a quote.

## Customs and duty: 5%, no VAT, and a tariff in motion

Qatar applies the GCC common tariff: 5% ad valorem on the CIF value of general cargo. Exemptions exist for certain foodstuffs, medicines, industrial inputs and goods imported by approved entities. Tobacco, alcohol and pork are at 100% and are effectively prohibited or tightly controlled for local sale.

There is no VAT. That is the structural difference between Qatar and Saudi Arabia (15%), the UAE (5%), Oman (5%) and Bahrain (10%). For a DDP quote, it removes an entire calculation layer and the working-capital problem of funding recoverable VAT. For a local importer, it removes the input-credit machinery — which is why Qatari importers are often less focused on VAT invoices than their Saudi counterparts and more focused on the duty and the release.

The absence of VAT does not make Qatar cheap. Duty is still assessed on CIF, and CIF includes freight. In a market where the Hormuz premium can add four figures per FEU, the dutiable base moves with the risk premium. That is the "zero-VAT trap": a quote that assumes zero tax and a normal freight rate can still land 20% above plan.

Clearance runs through **Al Nadeeb**, Qatar's electronic single window. The declaration, permits, inspection and release are handled in one system, and most routine consignments clear in two to five working days. The delays come from three places: a missing CoC for a regulated product, a valuation query, and an inspection triggered by the HS line. All three are avoidable before the vessel sails.

Two procedural points that catch Chinese exporters:

- **The importer of record must be a Qatari-registered entity** with a valid commercial registration and import licence. DDP does not change that. A seller who "handles everything" still needs a local importer on the declaration.
- **Arabic labelling and country-of-origin marking** are enforced at inspection for food, cosmetics and many consumer products. A label that passes the factory audit can still fail at the port.

## Conformity: QGOSM, the G mark and food

Qatar's conformity regime is narrower than Saudi Arabia's SABER but it is not optional. The Qatar General Organization for Standards and Metrology (QGOSM) runs a pre-shipment verification programme. Regulated products must be accompanied by a certificate of conformity issued by an approved body before shipment.

The regulated list has been built in phases. The first two phases cover vehicle tyres, safety belts, wheel rims, brake pads, exhaust fans, electric irons and hair dryers. Later phases extend into electrical products and building materials. The list is not static, and the direction is expansion.

There is also a Gulf-wide layer: the G mark, the GCC conformity mark, applies to low-voltage electrical equipment and toys across the six states. A product with a valid G mark and test reports is usually well placed for a Qatari CoC, but the CoC itself is still a shipment document.

Food is separate. The Ministry of Public Health runs its own certificate of conformity programme for imported food, with requirements for product registration, Arabic labelling, shelf life and, for some categories, laboratory testing at an approved lab. A food consignment without the MoPH certificate will not clear, regardless of what the QGOSM paperwork says.

The practical advice: **treat conformity as a lead-time item, not a documentation item.** A first-time CoC takes two to three weeks including sampling and testing. If your product is on the regulated list and your factory has no existing certificate, the certificate — not the vessel — sets your launch date.

## Risk and compliance: the failure modes we see

**The relay that was not booked.** A forwarder quotes a 22-day transit via Jebel Ali. The ocean leg arrives on time, the feeder is full, and the box sits eight days. The B/L says "transshipment at carrier's option"; the shipper has no claim. Fix: nominate the connection and get the feeder cut-off in writing.

**The CoC that arrives after the vessel.** A regulated product ships without a CoC because the factory assumed the importer would handle it. The container is held at Hamad, storage accrues, and the only fix is a rushed certificate plus a re-inspection. Fix: confirm the certificate before booking, not before arrival.

**The DDP quote that forgot the importer.** A Chinese seller quotes DDP Doha and discovers that the declaration needs a Qatari importer of record. The workaround — using a local agent's licence — shifts liability and often the duty. Fix: name the importer of record in the contract.

**The project cargo that assumed Hormuz.** A module or turbine is booked on a schedule that assumes normal strait transit. A risk event adds a surcharge and a delay, and the installation window moves. Fix: build the contingency into the vessel booking and the insurance, not just the freight rate.

## Our read: what to do, what to avoid

**Do** treat Hamad as a reliable port with an unreliable service frequency. The port will not be your bottleneck; the rotation will. Book direct where you can, and when you cannot, buy the connection explicitly.

**Do** use Qatar as the simplest DDP market in the GCC. Zero VAT removes a layer of cost and cash-flow complexity that Saudi Arabia and the UAE impose — a genuine advantage for smaller Chinese sellers testing the Gulf.

**Do** budget for conformity early. The QGOSM list is shorter than SABER's, but it is enforced, and food has a separate route.

**Avoid** assuming that a Gulf-wide port story applies to Qatar. Jebel Ali's capacity, Jeddah's congestion and Hamad's underutilisation are three different problems. Qatar's issue is connectivity and chokepoint risk, not berth space.

**Avoid** competing on ocean rate alone. In a market where the risk premium can move by four figures per FEU, a US$150 difference in base rate is noise. Compete on clearance certainty and landed cost.

The contrarian view: Qatar is the GCC market where a small exporter can most easily build a compliant, predictable channel — precisely because the port is not congested and the tax system is simple. The constraint is not the country. It is the number of sailings.

## FAQ

**How long does sea freight from China to Qatar take?**
Typically 18–25 days port to port to Hamad on a direct rotation, and 24–34 days with transshipment. Add two to five days for clearance and inland delivery. Use the 80th percentile for planning, not the advertised transit.

**Is there VAT in Qatar?**
No. Qatar has not implemented VAT. Duty is 5% on CIF for most goods, with higher rates on tobacco, alcohol, pork and selected items. The absence of VAT makes DDP simpler but does not make the landed cost low.

**Do I need a certificate of conformity for Qatar?**
For regulated products, yes. QGOSM requires a CoC issued by an approved body before shipment. The list includes tyres, brake pads, seat belts, wheel rims, exhaust fans, electric irons and hair dryers, and is expanding. Food needs a separate MoPH certificate.

**Can I ship to Qatar through Dubai?**
Yes. Many China–Qatar consignments relay through Jebel Ali, and some go overland via Saudi Arabia. Both add a connection or a border crossing. Price total door-to-door time, not the ocean leg.

**Is DDP legal in Qatar?**
DDP is a commercial arrangement, not a customs status. The importer of record must be a Qatari-registered entity, and duty must be declared correctly. A DDP quote that depends on under-declaring value is a liability waiting to be assigned.

**Which port serves Doha?**
Hamad Port is the container gateway, about 40 km south of the city. Old Doha Port handles cruise and some general cargo; Al Ruwais serves the north and Mesaieed the industrial belt.

**What is the biggest risk for China–Qatar shipments in 2026?**
Hormuz. Qatar has no maritime alternative to the strait, so a risk event hits every container at once. The second risk is service frequency: a relay that misses its feeder can add a week.
