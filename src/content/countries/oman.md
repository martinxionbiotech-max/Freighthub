## Direct answer

Oman is the only GCC state with deepwater container capacity that a ship can reach without transiting the Strait of Hormuz. That single sentence is the commercial case, and 2026 argued it for us. When the strait closed at the end of February, Bahrain's monthly import containers fell by roughly 80%. Sohar's container throughput rose 40% year on year in the first half. Same region, same crisis, opposite result.

For a Chinese exporter, though, Oman is not one market. It is three ports doing three different jobs. **Sohar** is the northern gateway: 2.46 million TEU of container capacity, more than 3,000 vessel calls a year and about 170 km of road to Dubai. **Salalah** is a relay hub where roughly nine containers in ten are transshipment rather than Omani import. **Duqm** is an industrial and project-cargo bet that is only now acquiring a container terminal worth the name.

Transit from Shanghai or Ningbo to Sohar is commonly 18–24 days on a direct rotation, and 14–19 days to Salalah on the Asia–Europe lane *(industry claim)*. Duty is 5% on CIF for most goods. VAT is 5%, charged on the duty-paid value, so the combined effective rate on a CIF invoice is about 5.25%. The gates that actually decide your launch date are Bayan access, the certificate of conformity, and whether your cargo is really coming to Oman or merely passing through it.

## What changed since August 2026

Oman's freight story this autumn is not a new port. It is the repricing of an old one.

- **H1 2026 results, published in July and August** — Sohar handled 52 million tonnes of cargo in six months, up 52% year on year, with container throughput up 40% to 545,000 TEU and breakbulk nearly doubling to 1.24 million tonnes. Sohar's own commentary attributes the container gain primarily to transshipment, which tells you the boxes were diverted, not newly consumed.
- **29 June 2026** — Asyad Group and CMA CGM signed a framework agreement for a US$400 million multipurpose logistics terminal at Sohar. Trade press framed it explicitly as capacity *outside* the Strait of Hormuz. This is the first major private commitment sized for the post-crisis routing pattern rather than the pre-crisis one.
- **August 2026** — Oman confirmed that APM Terminals' concession at Salalah will not be extended beyond 1 November 2028. The government is taking back its largest container asset, five years after a US$300 million upgrade lifted capacity from 4.5 million to 6.5 million TEU.
- **September 2026** — Salalah handled 511,209 TEU in a single month, an all-time record. Full-year 2025 volume had already reached 4 million TEU, itself a record.
- **October–November 2026** — Asyad and JD.com agreed to develop three distribution warehouses in the Muscat International Airport Free Zone. Chinese e-commerce logistics is now building inside Oman's free-zone envelope, not just shipping through it.
- **1 January 2026** — Oman began enforcing digital tax stamps on excise goods. A compliance item for beverage, tobacco and energy-drink importers, and a reminder that Omani border enforcement is tightening rather than loosening.
- **February and April 2026** — Water-dispensing appliances and e-scooters were added to the Omani Conformity Scheme's regulated scope. The list is expanding in small, specific steps, which is exactly how conformity lists become traps for exporters who checked once.

The through-line: Oman spent a decade building capacity that its own population could not fill, and 2026 finally supplied the demand — not Omani demand, but regional demand for a way around the strait.

## Why it matters: the cleanest natural experiment in Gulf logistics

In the first week of March 2026, two GCC countries with similar income levels and similar import dependence had completely different months. Bahrain, inside the Gulf, lost four fifths of its import containers between January and May. Oman, outside the strait, gained 40% more containers at Sohar in half a year.

That is not a marketing story. It is a routing fact with three consequences.

**First, Oman is now the default contingency, not the alternative.** Before 2026, a forwarder quoting Oman was usually solving a specific problem: a project cargo, a Salalah relay, a Sohar free-zone factory. In 2026, Oman became the answer to a general question — "where do we discharge if the Gulf is closed?" That shift in default status is worth more than any tariff differential, because defaults are sticky. Buyers who built a Sohar routing in March will still be using it in 2028.

**Second, the benefit is concentrated in the north.** Sohar sits on the Gulf of Oman, outside the strait, close to the UAE border and within trucking range of Dubai, Abu Dhabi and the Saudi Eastern Province. Salalah sits 1,000 km south of Muscat on the Arabian Sea. Both are outside Hormuz, but only Sohar is inside the commercial gravity of the Gulf. That is why Sohar's container volume moves with regional disruption while Salalah's moves with carrier network design.

**Third, Oman's domestic market cannot absorb the capacity it has built.** The country has about five million people and an import base that supports perhaps a few hundred thousand TEU of genuine gateway cargo. Everything above that is relay, transshipment or trans-border road haulage. A Chinese exporter who mistakes Oman's port statistics for Omani demand will over-invest in a market that is smaller than a single Chinese prefecture.

The practical reading: Oman is a **routing play with a small consumer market attached**, not a consumer market with good ports. Price it that way.

## Three ports, three products

### Sohar: the industrial port that became a container gateway

Sohar was designed as an industrial complex, not a container hub. The port company is a joint venture between the Omani government and the Port of Rotterdam, and the anchor tenants are aluminium, steel, petrochemicals and plastics — industries that need bulk berths and pipeline access, not reefer plugs. Hutchison Ports operates the container terminal alongside that industrial base.

That history explains both Sohar's strength and its limitation. The strength is depth and land: seven container berths, a 2.4 km quay and drafts to 18 m, with a free zone directly behind the gate. The limitation is that container handling is a secondary business in a port built for tonnage. When 2026 pushed an extra 40% of containers through, the terminal could absorb it because it had spare capacity — but spare capacity and service quality are not the same thing, and a terminal can only stretch so far before berth windows and yard density start to bite.

For a Chinese shipper, Sohar is the right answer for: Muscat and the Batinah coast, the Sohar free zone, and road delivery into Dubai and the northern Emirates. It is the wrong answer for Salalah, Dhofar or anything requiring a southern Omani delivery.

### Salalah: a relay hub wearing a country's flag

Salalah is one of the most efficient container terminals in the world — second globally in the World Bank's 2022 Container Port Performance Index — and almost none of that performance is for Oman. The port sits 15 km south of Salalah city with near-zero deviation from the Asia–Europe lane, equidistant from East Africa, the Indian subcontinent and the Gulf. It handles more than 3,200 vessel calls a year and connects to more than 50 direct ports.

The cargo mix tells the story: 90–95% transshipment, 5–10% gateway. Salalah is a network node, and its customers are carriers, not importers. A Chinese seller shipping to Oman who is told "we discharge at Salalah" should immediately ask how the box gets to Muscat. The answer is usually a 1,000 km truck leg or a feeder, and neither is included in the ocean rate.

### Duqm: the long bet

Duqm is the Special Economic Zone on the Arabian Sea coast, with a dry dock, a refinery, a liquid bulk terminal and a new Asyad container terminal of three berths, over 1,000 m of quay, a 26,000 TEU yard and more than 600 reefer plugs. Its design capacity is measured in millions of TEU; its actual container traffic is a fraction of that.

Duqm matters for two reasons. It is the only Omani port with serious industrial land and deep water outside both Hormuz and the Salalah network, which makes it a genuine project-cargo and heavy-lift option. And it is the government's hedge against over-dependence on a single southern hub. It does not matter yet for containerised consumer goods, and any forwarder who proposes Duqm for a standard FCL shipment to Muscat is solving the wrong problem.

## The Salalah cliff: what happens on 1 November 2028

Oman's decision not to extend APM Terminals' concession beyond 2028 is the most under-discussed risk in Gulf container logistics. Salalah is not just a port; it is a node in the Gemini cooperation and in Maersk's and CMA CGM's Indian Ocean networks. The terminal's efficiency, its 6.5 million TEU capacity and its carrier relationships were built over 15 years under a single operator.

A concession transition can go smoothly. It can also do what transitions do: freeze investment in the final two years, unsettle carrier commitments, and produce a period in which the terminal is technically open but commercially uncertain.

For shippers, the implications are specific:

- **Do not build a 2028–2030 supply chain that assumes today's Salalah service pattern.** If your routing depends on a specific Salalah relay, ask the carrier what its post-2028 plan is. If the answer is vague, that is the answer.
- **Watch the capex signal, not the press release.** A terminal that stops investing 18 months before handover is telling you something. Reefer plug additions, yard equipment orders and berth maintenance are the tells.
- **Treat Salalah's record months as a 2026 phenomenon, not a trend line.** 511,209 TEU in September is a network artefact — carriers rerouting around a closed strait — and network artefacts reverse when networks normalise.

Our judgment: the 2028 transition is more likely to be orderly than chaotic, because Oman has a strong interest in keeping the hub and the terminal is too valuable to strand. But "more likely orderly" is not a guarantee, and it is the kind of risk that should appear in a contract rather than a planning assumption.

## How to act: six decisions before you book

**1. Decide which Oman you are buying.** Sohar for northern delivery and UAE road haulage. Salalah only if your forwarder can show you the onward leg and its cost. Duqm for project cargo and heavy lifts. These are not interchangeable, and a quote that names "Oman" without a port is not a quote.

**2. Price the road leg before the ocean leg.** Sohar to Dubai is about 170 km; Sohar to Riyadh is roughly 1,200 km. Both are routine. Neither is free, and both cross a border where your Omani clearance stops being valid.

**3. Get the importer of record and Bayan access right.** Bayan is Oman's single-window customs environment, operated by the Royal Oman Police, authenticated by PKI. It is a company system: the importer needs a commercial registration and an authorised user, and the declaration runs on 12-digit HS codes. A Chinese seller quoting DDP still needs an Omani entity on the declaration.

**4. Start the conformity file before production, not before shipment.** The Omani Conformity Scheme, run by the Directorate General for Standards and Metrology, requires a certificate of conformity or a type examination certificate for regulated products, valid for one year. Low-voltage electrical equipment and appliances are the core of the scope, and the list is growing.

**5. Treat VAT as working capital, not cost.** Five percent VAT is recoverable for a registered importer, but it is paid at the border and reclaimed later. On a 40-foot container of mid-value goods, that is a real cash-flow item, and it is the reason some Omani importers prefer to buy on a landed-price basis rather than clear themselves.

**6. For project cargo, book early and book heavy.** Sohar and Duqm have heavy-lift capacity, but not unlimited windows. The CMA CGM–Asyad terminal will not be operational for years, and in the meantime the good berth slots go to the projects that booked first.

## Cost and transit drivers: where the money actually goes

Ocean freight is the visible line; it is rarely the largest one for Oman. A typical China→Sohar FCL shipment splits roughly like this *(editorial judgment based on quote structures, not a rate survey)*:

| Cost bucket | Share of landed cost | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean freight and BAF | 25–40% | Direct versus relay, carrier, season, whether the call is a mainline or a feeder |
| Origin charges and documentation | 3–6% | THC, VGM, certificate of origin, booking amendments |
| Destination THC and port charges | 5–9% | Container type, terminal, prepaid versus collect |
| Customs duty | 3–8% of CIF | HS classification and valuation; 5% is the default, not the rule |
| Clearance and inspection | 2–5% | Bayan data quality, permit route, conformity gaps |
| Inland delivery | 6–15% | Muscat versus Dubai versus Riyadh; border waiting; truck availability |
| Demurrage, storage, detention | 0–15% | Free time, release speed, consignee readiness |

Four structural points explain most of the dispersion.

**The Hormuz premium is the variable Oman actually avoids.** War-risk insurance for strait transits reached 3.5–7.5% of hull value by mid-2026, according to industry estimates cited by Singapore's transport ministry, and carriers layered surcharges of US$1,500–4,000 per TEU on Gulf-bound boxes. A Sohar routing sidesteps the strait premium on the ocean leg. It does not sidestep congestion, and it does not sidestep the fact that Sohar is a smaller call with fewer sailings and therefore a thinner rate market.

**Salalah looks cheap and often is not.** The ocean leg to Salalah can be the cheapest quote you receive, because Salalah is a mainline port on the way to somewhere else. The onward leg to Muscat — 1,000 km by road, or a feeder with its own cut-off — is where the money reappears. Always price door-to-door.

**The inland leg dominates northern deliveries.** Sohar's geography is its advantage: 170 km to Dubai is a day truck. But a container cleared in Oman and delivered to the UAE is an import into the UAE, with its own declaration, its own duty and its own conformity regime. The GCC customs union has never functioned as a true single market for goods, and 2026 has not changed that.

**Air is a supplement, not an alternative.** Muscat International has belly capacity and some freighter service, but China–Oman air freight is thin compared with Dubai or Doha. For urgent cargo, the realistic routing is often China→Dubai by air, then truck to Oman — which is fast, expensive and adds a border.

## Risk and compliance: the failure modes we see

**The Salalah misroute.** A forwarder quotes a competitive rate "to Oman" and discharges at Salalah. The consignee is in Muscat. The onward truck is 1,000 km, the feeder is fortnightly, and the free time expires before either is arranged. Fix: the port name belongs in the contract, not just the country.

**The GCC customs union assumption.** A shipper clears in Sohar and assumes the box can move freely to Dubai. It cannot. UAE import clearance, UAE duty and UAE conformity apply. Fix: treat each GCC state as a separate customs territory with a common tariff, because that is what it is in practice.

**The conformity certificate that arrives after the vessel.** A regulated electrical product ships without a certificate because the factory assumed the importer would arrange it. The box is held, storage accrues, and the fix is a rushed type examination. Fix: confirm the certificate before booking; the one-year validity means the renewal date matters too.

**The Bayan access gap.** A Chinese seller signs a DDP contract and discovers that the Omani declaration requires a registered importer with PKI access and a licensed broker. The workaround — using a local agent's licence — moves liability and often the duty. Fix: name the importer of record and the broker in the contract.

**The labour-cost surprise.** Omanisation requirements apply across logistics and are enforced at licence renewal. A warehouse or trucking operation that budgets on expatriate labour rates will find its staffing plan is a compliance document as much as a cost document. This is not unique to Oman, but it is more binding than in the UAE.

## Our read: what to do, what to avoid

**Do** treat Oman as the GCC's structural hedge. If your Gulf volume is large enough to justify a second routing, Sohar is the most credible non-Hormuz option with real container capacity, and 2026 proved it can absorb diverted volume.

**Do** use Oman for what it is good at: northern gateway cargo, UAE road haulage, relay through Salalah, and project cargo through Duqm and Sohar. The country's ports are better than its market, and pretending otherwise leads to bad inventory decisions.

**Do** build the compliance file early. Five percent duty and five percent VAT are simple; the certificate of conformity and the Bayan registration are not. The simple part is not where launches fail.

**Avoid** assuming that Oman's port growth equals Omani demand. Sohar's 40% container increase was overwhelmingly transshipment. If you are selling consumer goods, your addressable market is five million people, not a 6.5 million TEU port.

**Avoid** routing to Salalah for Omani delivery without pricing the onward leg. It is the most common expensive mistake in this market.

**Avoid** planning a 2029 supply chain around Salalah's current service pattern until the post-2028 operator is named and its carrier commitments are visible.

The contrarian view: Oman is the GCC market where a Chinese exporter can most easily build a *resilient* channel, precisely because the country has over-built its ports and under-built its domestic demand. The constraint is not access. It is knowing which of the three Omans you are actually selling into.

## FAQ

**How long does sea freight from China to Oman take?**
Typically 18–24 days port to port to Sohar on a direct rotation, and 14–19 days to Salalah on the Asia–Europe lane. Add two to five days for clearance and inland delivery. For planning, use the 80th percentile, not the advertised transit.

**Is Oman outside the Strait of Hormuz?**
Yes. Sohar, Muscat, Duqm and Salalah are all outside the strait. That is why Omani container volumes rose in the first half of 2026 while Bahrain's imports collapsed. It is the country's single most important freight characteristic.

**What is the difference between Sohar and Salalah?**
Sohar is a northern industrial and gateway port with 2.46 million TEU of container capacity and road access to the UAE. Salalah is a southern transshipment hub where 90–95% of containers are relay cargo. They are not substitutes.

**What are the import duties and taxes in Oman?**
Duty is 5% on CIF for most goods under the GCC common tariff, with excise of 50–100% on tobacco, alcohol, energy drinks, carbonated drinks and pork. VAT is 5% on the duty-paid value, giving an effective combined rate of about 5.25% on CIF.

**Do I need a certificate of conformity for Oman?**
For regulated products, yes. The Omani Conformity Scheme requires a certificate of conformity or a type examination certificate issued before shipment, valid for one year. Low-voltage electrical equipment and appliances are the core scope, and the list is expanding.

**Can I use Oman as a gateway to Saudi Arabia or the UAE?**
Physically yes, commercially with care. Sohar to Dubai is about 170 km and Sohar to Riyadh roughly 1,200 km, but each border is a separate customs and conformity regime. Clearing in Oman does not clear you in the UAE or Saudi Arabia.

**What is the biggest risk for China–Oman shipments in 2026–2028?**
The Salalah concession transition in November 2028, followed by Sohar congestion if diverted volumes persist. Neither is a 2026 problem, and both are easier to price now than to fix later.
