## Direct answer

China-to-UAE freight in November 2026 does not default to Jebel Ali. With the Strait of Hormuz disrupted, most container cargo discharges at Khor Fakkan, Fujairah or Sohar and moves by truck. Budget 30–50 days door-to-door, 5% duty on CIF plus 5% VAT, and put the land leg in the contract before you book. *(Editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

The UAE freight market is no longer adjusting to a single event. It is adjusting to a permanent detour, and the detour is now the product.

**25 September 2026.** A seven-day proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz was put on the table between Washington and Tehran. It did not become a functioning corridor. The headline matters less than the pattern: reopening has been discussed repeatedly since the spring, and each proposal has run into the same sequencing problem — who guarantees passage, who pays for it, and who accepts the verification regime. *(Reported fact, then editorial judgment)*

**5 October 2026.** Oil traffic through Hormuz rebounded, and tanker rates eased from their peak. This is the contradiction that confuses importers. Tankers are moving again under convoy and naval cover; container lines are not. The reason is commercial, not military. A tanker voyage is a single asset with a single cargo. A container service is a weekly network with fixed port rotations, slot commitments and empty-container flows. Once a carrier has rebuilt its rotation around Khor Fakkan, Salalah or Jeddah, reversing that decision costs money and takes months. *(Reported fact; interpretation is editorial)*

**13–19 October 2026.** The Gemini alliance relaunched its Asia–Middle East loop, but swapped Jebel Ali for Khor Fakkan. This is the clearest signal of the quarter. The carrier did not cancel the service; it moved the call. Jebel Ali remains a destination for cargo, but increasingly as a trucked destination rather than a vessel call. *(Reported fact)*

**16 October 2026.** Semafor reported that the UAE's "Zero Hormuz" strategy now runs through Fujairah. The strategy is not a slogan. It is a reorientation of infrastructure spending toward the east coast, outside the strait, with Fujairah as the pressure-release valve for both energy and containerised cargo. *(Reported fact)*

**30 October 2026.** Kuehne+Nagel's operational data showed average vessel waiting times of 7.25 days at Khor Fakkan and 8.86 days at Fujairah over the preceding week. Sharjah was around three days. Maersk imposed temporary booking restrictions on Khor Fakkan, Fujairah and selected Upper Gulf destinations. The same week, DP World's CEO warned that rerouting costs would feed through to consumer prices. *(Reported fact)*

**3 November 2026.** The Port of Fujairah resumed oil loadings after an attack, and Linerlytica declared Middle East container congestion a record. The resumption is good news for energy flows. It is not evidence that the container system has normalised. *(Reported fact)*

The net effect: the UAE has stopped being a single-gateway market. It is now a multi-gateway market with a broken centre. Jebel Ali still matters — it is the largest man-made harbour in the world, the base for JAFZA, and the address most UAE importers put on their customs paperwork — but it is no longer the place where a China-origin container reliably arrives by sea. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Why it matters for your landed cost

Three cost lines have changed at once, and they do not move in the same direction.

**Ocean freight is higher, but not uniformly.** Freightos reported that Shanghai-to-Jebel Ali container rates quadrupled from under USD 2,000 to above USD 8,000 per container after the closure began at the end of February 2026. That is a reported fact from May, not a current quotation. By the fourth quarter the spread had widened: the same 40-foot box can carry a several-thousand-dollar difference depending on whether it is discharged at Khor Fakkan, Fujairah, Salalah or Jeddah and whether trucking is included. *(Reported fact + industry claim)*

**The inland leg is now a first-order cost, not a rounding error.** A container discharged at Khor Fakkan and trucked to Jebel Ali covers roughly 120 kilometres. From Fujairah the road leg is roughly 150 kilometres. From Salalah it is over 1,200 kilometres; from Jeddah, roughly 1,800 kilometres through Saudi Arabia. The first two are manageable daily trucking moves. The second two are multi-day domestic transfers with their own customs and insurance complications. Importers who compare only the ocean quote will systematically choose the wrong port. *(Editorial judgment based on geography)*

**Time has become the expensive variable.** Pre-war, a Shanghai-to-Jebel Ali FCL booking was commonly quoted at 18–24 days port-to-port, with Shenzhen at 14–20 days. Those numbers are still repeated on forwarder websites, which is one reason quote comparisons are so misleading. In the current routing, the ocean leg to an east-coast port plus the 12–15 days now needed to transfer a container from Khor Fakkan or Fujairah to Jebel Ali, Sharjah or Abu Dhabi produces a door-to-door cycle of roughly 30–50 days for FCL. LCL adds another 7–10 days because of consolidation and deconsolidation. *(Industry claim; the 12–15 day transfer figure is reported by Kuehne+Nagel via Splash247)*

For a distributor carrying six weeks of inventory, that difference is not a freight problem. It is a working-capital problem. For an e-commerce seller, it is a stock-out problem. For a project cargo buyer, it is a demurrage and storage problem. *(Editorial judgment)*

## How to act: a routing decision in five steps

**1. Choose the discharge port by consignee location, not by habit.** Dubai and JAFZA cargo should default to Khor Fakkan plus truck. Abu Dhabi cargo should test Khalifa Port first, then Khor Fakkan. Northern Emirates cargo should test Sharjah's Khalid Port, accepting that it sits inside Hormuz and carries more schedule risk. Saudi-bound cargo should not automatically transload in the UAE; Jeddah or Dammam direct is often cheaper once the border and inland leg are counted. *(Editorial judgment)*

**2. Insist on a through bill of lading with a named land leg.** The most common failure in this market is a carrier B/L to Khor Fakkan with no contractual owner of the trucking leg. The container arrives, the consignee discovers that the forwarder's quote ended at the port gate, and the box sits in a yard at USD 100–300 per day while three parties argue. A through B/L, or a written door-delivery addendum naming the trucker, the transit time and the free time, is the minimum. *(Industry claim + editorial judgment)*

**3. Pre-clear before the vessel arrives.** Clearance in Dubai is fast when the file is clean — often two to five working days — and slow when it is not. The difference is almost always documentation: a missing certificate of origin, an HS code that does not match the product, a consignee whose trade licence does not cover the activity, or a regulated product without ECAS. Start the file when the container is loaded, not when it arrives. *(Industry claim)*

**4. Model duty and VAT on CIF, not on the supplier invoice.** UAE customs duty is generally 5% of CIF. Import VAT is 5% of the customs value plus duty plus any excise. That means the effective cash outlay at clearance is roughly 10.25% of CIF for a standard 5%-duty, 5%-VAT product, before any anti-dumping duty. If the product is a car battery, ceramic tile or hydraulic cement, anti-dumping duty can reach 67.5% of CIF. If it is tobacco or an energy drink, excise is 100%. *(Reported fact)*

**5. Build a buffer and a fallback into the contract.** For FCL, plan 45–55 days from factory gate to warehouse; for LCL, 55–70 days. Keep a second routing option open — Sohar for northern UAE, Khalifa for Abu Dhabi, Jeddah for Saudi-bound — and agree in advance who pays if the carrier switches discharge port. Do not sign a fixed-delivery penalty clause without a force-majeure and routing-change carve-out. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Transit benchmarks.** Pre-war port-to-port times from Chinese load ports were commonly quoted as Shanghai 18–24 days, Ningbo 18–24, Shenzhen 14–20, Guangzhou 16–22, Qingdao 22–28. Treat those as historical baselines. In the current market, add the east-coast discharge, the 12–15 day port-to-door transfer reported at Khor Fakkan and Fujairah, and the variability of truck availability. A realistic FCL door-to-door range is 30–50 days; a realistic LCL range is 55–70 days. Air freight is 4–7 days door-to-door for priority cargo, with capacity tight and rates well above 2025 levels. *(Industry claim)*

## Ports and routing: the new UAE gateway map

**Khor Fakkan (Sharjah, east coast).** The main container valve. It sits on the Gulf of Oman, outside the Strait of Hormuz, and is operated by DP World. It is currently handling ships up to 24,000 TEU on MSC's expanded Far East–Middle East service, with average waits of 7.25 days. The terminal is doubling capacity to catch diverted trade, but the expansion will not be finished before the current congestion peak. Use it for Dubai, JAFZA, Sharjah and Abu Dhabi cargo when the trucking leg is priced and owned. Expect 12–15 days from discharge to Jebel Ali, Sharjah or Abu Dhabi — three times the pre-war five-day transfer. *(Reported fact)*

**Fujairah (east coast).** The pressure-release valve for energy and a growing container option. It is outside Hormuz, but its container infrastructure is thinner than Khor Fakkan's, and waits reached 8.86 days in late October. Fujairah is the centre of the UAE's "Zero Hormuz" strategy, which is why investment is flowing there. For containerised cargo, it is a secondary choice behind Khor Fakkan unless your consignee is in the northern emirates or the cargo is project or breakbulk. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

**Sharjah (Khalid Port and Hamriyah).** Inside Hormuz, close to Sharjah's industrial zones, with about three-day waits in late October. It is a useful fallback for northern-emirates cargo and light industrial goods, but it carries more schedule risk than the east-coast ports because vessels still transit the strait. *(Reported fact)*

**Jebel Ali (Dubai).** The largest man-made harbour in the world, with 67 berths and a 134.68 square kilometre port area. In normal times it handles 80+ weekly services to 150+ ports. In November 2026 it is largely restricted to ad hoc vessel calls. Cargo still reaches Jebel Ali, but increasingly by landbridge from Khor Fakkan or Fujairah, or by feeder from Salalah. JAFZA remains open and busy; the port is not the same thing as the free zone. *(Reported fact)*

**Khalifa Port (Abu Dhabi).** The deep-water alternative for Abu Dhabi and western UAE cargo, with rail and road connections. It is inside Hormuz, so it shares the strait risk, but it is less congested than the east-coast container terminals and is the natural first test for Abu Dhabi-bound industrial and project cargo. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Oman: Sohar and Salalah.** Sohar is the most practical overland alternative for northern UAE, with a road leg of roughly 180–220 kilometres to Dubai and waits of two to three days in late October. Salalah is a deep-water transshipment hub with 84% yard density and berthing delays of up to 36 hours; its overland leg to the UAE is over 1,200 kilometres, so it is a container transshipment option rather than a trucking option. *(Reported fact)*

**Jeddah (Saudi Arabia, Red Sea).** A viable entry point for Saudi-bound cargo and, in extremis, for UAE-bound cargo trucked across Saudi Arabia. Jeddah's container yards were at 76% density in late October. The overland leg to Dubai is roughly 1,800 kilometres and involves Saudi transit customs. It is not a casual substitute for a Gulf port. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

## Free zone versus mainland: where duty and VAT actually land

The UAE's free zones are not tax-free shops. They are duty-deferral and VAT-deferral machines, and the deferral ends the moment goods enter the mainland.

**JAFZA** is the largest customs bonded zone in the Middle East, with 11,000+ businesses from 150+ countries and 23.9% of Dubai's foreign direct investment. Goods entering JAFZA from abroad are not subject to UAE customs duty or import VAT at entry, provided they stay within the zone and are not released into the mainland. That is why JAFZA works for re-export, regional distribution and e-commerce fulfilment. *(Reported fact)*

**The mainland transfer is the taxable event.** When goods leave JAFZA for a UAE mainland customer, an import declaration is filed, 5% duty is payable on CIF, and 5% VAT is payable on the customs value plus duty. If the goods are re-exported outside the UAE, duty and VAT are generally not triggered — but the destination country's rules apply, and Saudi Arabia, for example, requires SABER compliance regardless of the UAE origin. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

**DMCC, DAFZA, SAIF Zone and RAKEZ** operate similar bonded regimes with different sector specialisations. DAFZA is the natural choice for air-sea e-commerce; DMCC for commodities and trading; SAIF Zone and RAKEZ for light manufacturing and cost-sensitive warehousing. The customs logic is broadly the same: duty suspension inside, declaration and payment on release to the mainland. *(Industry claim)*

**Corporate tax is a separate question.** The UAE's headline corporate tax rate is 9%, with 0% on the first AED 375,000 of taxable income. Qualifying free-zone income can be taxed at 0%, but only if the entity meets the substance and qualifying-income conditions. A free-zone licence is not, by itself, a corporate tax exemption. Sellers who set up a JAFZA company to hold inventory should get the tax position confirmed before they sign the lease, not after. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

## Customs and duty: the 5% + 5% stack

The UAE is part of the GCC Customs Union. For most goods of non-GCC origin, the customs duty is 5% of the CIF value — cost, insurance and freight. Some goods are duty-free; alcohol and tobacco carry higher rates; and anti-dumping duties apply to specific products, including car batteries, ceramic and porcelain tiles, and hydraulic cement, with rates that can reach 67.5% of CIF. *(Reported fact)*

Import VAT is 5%. The base is not the supplier invoice. It is the customs value plus customs duty plus any excise. For a standard product with 5% duty and 5% VAT, the cash payable at clearance is about 10.25% of CIF. For a product with 100% excise — tobacco, energy drinks, electronic smoking devices — the VAT base rises accordingly. *(Reported fact)*

The documents that decide whether clearance takes two days or two weeks:

- Commercial invoice with correct HS code, unit value, currency and Incoterm.
- Packing list with weights, dimensions and carton counts.
- Bill of lading or sea waybill, with the consignee matching the trade licence.
- Certificate of origin, especially where a preferential rate is claimed.
- UAE trade licence and customs client code for the importer of record.
- Product certificates: ECAS for regulated electrical goods, toys, cosmetics and construction materials; TDRA approval for telecom devices; Dubai Municipality food registration for food; MOHAP registration for medical devices and pharmaceuticals.
- MSDS and UN 38.3 test reports for batteries and dangerous goods.

*(Industry claim, compiled from UAE import practice)*

**The importer of record is the choke point.** A UAE trade licence is required, and the licence activity must cover the goods. Sellers who use a forwarder's "DDP" service without a named importer are relying on the forwarder's licence, which is legal only if the forwarder is genuinely acting as importer and is registered for VAT. If the arrangement is a workaround, the risk lands on the seller when customs audits the valuation or the consignee. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Valuation is the second choke point.** UAE customs can reject a declared value that looks below market, and the penalty regime includes fines and delays. Related-party transactions, sample shipments and "gift" declarations are common triggers. Keep the commercial invoice, payment proof and a price benchmark for the HS code. *(Industry claim)*

## Product certification: what stops clearance

The UAE does not have a single door for product compliance. It has several, and the wrong one produces a detention notice.

**ECAS** covers regulated electrical equipment, electronics, toys, cosmetics, personal care, construction materials and food-contact materials. The certificate is issued through an accredited conformity body, and the product needs the correct test reports. **TDRA** covers telecom and radio devices, including phones, routers and Bluetooth products. **Dubai Municipality** regulates food imports through its food import system and cosmetics through the Montaji programme. **MOHAP** regulates medical devices and pharmaceuticals.

Batteries are the category most likely to be underestimated. Lithium cells and battery packs need UN 38.3 transport test reports, and products containing them need the correct dangerous-goods declaration. A container of power banks with a missing UN 38.3 report is not a customs delay; it is a refusal to load or a seizure at destination. *(Industry claim)*

The practical rule: certify before you book, not after. A certificate that arrives three days after the vessel sails is a certificate that arrives two weeks too late. *(Editorial judgment)*

## E-commerce: Amazon FBA and noon in a disrupted market

The UAE is still one of the easiest GCC markets for cross-border e-commerce, but 2026 has made "easy" conditional on inventory planning.

Amazon.ae FBA and noon both require a UAE importer of record, a trade licence that covers the product category, and compliant product labelling. Sellers typically ship to a third-party warehouse in JAFZA or a Dubai industrial zone, then replenish FBA/noon fulfilment centres in smaller batches. The FBA inbound appointment, carton labelling and FNSKU requirements are strict, and a rejected inbound shipment can sit in a 3PL for weeks.

The routing change matters more than the platform change. With Jebel Ali restricted to ad hoc calls and Khor Fakkan congested, a replenishment cycle that was 30 days is now 45–55 days. Sellers should:

- Hold 8–10 weeks of cover on top-selling SKUs, not 4–6.
- Split inventory between a JAFZA 3PL and the marketplace fulfilment centre.
- Use air freight only for launches and stock-out rescue, not routine replenishment.
- Confirm the importer-of-record arrangement in writing, including who pays VAT and duty at clearance.

*(Editorial judgment, based on reported port conditions)*

## Risk and compliance

**War-risk insurance.** Standard marine cargo policies exclude war, strikes and terrorism unless endorsed. In the current Gulf market, war-risk cover is available but priced separately, and some underwriters impose voyage-specific conditions. Check that the policy covers the actual discharge port and the overland leg, not just the ocean segment. A policy that ends at Khor Fakkan does not cover a truck to Jebel Ali. *(Industry claim)*

**Sanctions and re-export controls.** The UAE suspended trade with Iran in August 2026 following an antiship missile strike. Goods with Iranian origin, Iranian ownership or an Iranian end-use should not be routed through the UAE without legal review. Re-export from JAFZA to Iran is a compliance red line, not a commercial grey area. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

**Force majeure and contract terms.** Carriers have invoked force majeure, changed discharge ports and imposed congestion surcharges. Your sales contract should say which Incoterm applies, which port is the agreed discharge point, who bears the cost of a carrier-initiated diversion, and what happens if the cargo cannot be delivered within a stated period. "CIF Jebel Ali" is ambiguous when the carrier discharges at Khor Fakkan. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Demurrage, detention and abandonment.** A box stuck at Khor Fakkan accrues demurrage; a box stuck at a 3PL accrues storage; a box that nobody clears can be abandoned and sold by the port authority. The three failure modes are usually caused by the same thing: a missing document that nobody checked before sailing. *(Industry claim)*

**Payment and currency.** The dirham is pegged to the US dollar, so USD invoicing is standard. The risk is not FX; it is counterparty risk. A new UAE buyer with a free-zone licence and a PO box is not automatically a creditworthy customer. Use a letter of credit or a staged payment for first orders. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Our read

**Do not treat Jebel Ali as the default.** In November 2026, a direct Jebel Ali B/L is a promise the carrier may not keep. Book to Khor Fakkan or Sohar, price the truck, and treat Jebel Ali as a destination rather than a discharge port. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Do use JAFZA for inventory, but not for a long lease.** JAFZA's duty and VAT deferral is genuinely valuable, and its 3PL ecosystem is deep. But a five-year lease signed on the assumption that Jebel Ali direct calls return in 2027 is a bet on a ceasefire and a carrier rotation schedule. Start with flexible 3PL space and a one-year licence. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Do not ship low-margin bulky goods by air.** Air freight is for high-value, time-critical or launch inventory. Using it to patch a routine replenishment gap destroys the unit economics of furniture, building materials, bulky consumer goods and low-value electronics. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Do consider Saudi direct if your end market is Saudi.** The UAE's value as a transshipment hub is real, but a Jebel Ali-to-Riyadh truck is roughly 1,000 kilometres plus border formalities. Dammam or Jeddah direct is often cheaper and simpler, especially for project cargo and heavy goods. *(Editorial judgment)*

**What would change our mind.** Two conditions: sustained weekly liner calls at Jebel Ali for at least eight consecutive weeks, and Khor Fakkan average waits below three days. Until both are true, the multi-gateway routing is the safer operating model. *(Editorial judgment)*

## FAQ

**Is Jebel Ali Port open?**
Yes, but not as a normal container gateway. It is handling ad hoc vessel calls, and cargo is reaching Jebel Ali by landbridge from Khor Fakkan and Fujairah. Direct liner services have largely moved to Khor Fakkan. Check with the carrier for the specific service, not with the port's general status page. *(Reported fact)*

**How long does China-to-UAE shipping take in November 2026?**
Plan 30–50 days door-to-door for FCL and 55–70 days for LCL. The ocean leg itself is not the bottleneck; the discharge port, the 12–15 day port-to-door transfer and truck availability are. Air freight is 4–7 days door-to-door for priority cargo. *(Industry claim)*

**How much is UAE customs duty?**
Generally 5% of CIF for most goods. Some goods are duty-free, alcohol and tobacco are higher, and anti-dumping duties can reach 67.5% on specific products such as car batteries, ceramic tiles and hydraulic cement. Check the HS code before you price the landed cost. *(Reported fact)*

**Can I import into a UAE free zone and avoid duty and VAT?**
You can defer them. Goods entering JAFZA or another bonded zone are not subject to duty or import VAT while they remain in the zone. When they are released to the mainland, duty and VAT become payable. Re-export outside the UAE generally avoids UAE duty and VAT, but the destination country's rules still apply. *(Reported fact)*

**Do I need a UAE company to import?**
Yes, for a normal commercial import. You need a UAE trade licence and a customs client code, and the licence activity must cover the goods. Some forwarders offer an importer-of-record service; if you use one, confirm in writing who is the legal importer, who pays duty and VAT, and who is liable if customs challenges the valuation. *(Industry claim)*

**Is air freight a viable alternative?**
For high-value, urgent or launch cargo, yes. For routine replenishment of bulky or low-margin goods, no. Air capacity into Dubai is tight, rates are elevated, and the door-to-door advantage shrinks once customs and last-mile delivery are included. *(Editorial judgment)*
