## Direct answer

The UAE is the GCC's easiest market to enter and its easiest market to mis-price. Jebel Ali handles most China-origin containers, Khalifa serves Abu Dhabi, and Sharjah's Khor Fakkan offers a route outside Hormuz. Plan 16–30 days port to port. Budget 5% duty on CIF and 5% VAT, unless the goods stay in a free zone. The decisive question is not the ocean rate; it is whether your cargo is a mainland import or a free-zone movement.

| Item | Position in late 2026 | Why it matters |

## One country, seven customs authorities

The UAE is a federation, and customs is administered at emirate level under a federal framework. That produces a fact that surprises new importers: **a licence and a customs account in one emirate do not automatically work in another**.

In practice:

- **Dubai Customs** administers Jebel Ali, Port Rashid, Al Maktoum Airport and the Dubai free zones. Its electronic declaration system is the one most forwarders know.
- **Abu Dhabi Customs** administers Khalifa Port, Zayed Port and the Abu Dhabi free zones.
- **Sharjah Customs** administers Port Khalid, Hamriyah and Khor Fakkan, plus the Sharjah free zones.
- The northern emirates have their own arrangements, and Fujairah is a bunkering and bulk port rather than a container gateway.

For a shipper, this means the broker and the importer licence must match the port. A Dubai broker can file in Sharjah or Abu Dhabi if licensed and registered, but many do not file there weekly. The practical test is simple: ask how many declarations the broker filed at that port in the last month.

## The ports and what each is for

**Jebel Ali** is the region's transhipment and distribution machine. It has the widest feeder network, the deepest forwarder pool and the most frequent China calls. It is the default for Dubai, the northern Emirates and re-export. Its weakness is congestion and cost: the port is busy, the free zone is expensive, and the landside can queue.

**Khalifa Port** at Taweelah is Abu Dhabi's deep-water gateway. It suits Abu Dhabi, Al Ain and KEZAD industrial cargo, plus project and heavy lift. Its weakness is a thinner service board and a smaller broker pool. See the [Khalifa port profile](/ports/khalifa/).

**Sharjah's ports** divide by function: Port Khalid for city and project cargo, Hamriyah for free-zone industry, and Khor Fakkan for containers outside Hormuz. Khor Fakkan is the strategically interesting one; the land bridge to Dubai is the constraint. See the [Sharjah port profile](/ports/sharjah/).

**Fujairah** matters for bunkering and bulk, not for container imports. Do not route a container there to save a strait transit unless the consignee is on the east coast.

The port decision is a distribution decision. A Dubai consignee that imports through Khalifa pays a cross-emirate truck; an Abu Dhabi consignee that imports through Jebel Ali pays the same in reverse. The ocean rate is usually close. The land leg and the empty return are not.

## Transit: what the schedule really says

China–UAE is one of the densest container corridors in the world. Direct calls from Yantian, Shekou, Nansha, Ningbo and Shanghai to Jebel Ali are frequent, and the proforma range is roughly 16–26 days. Add 3–10 days for clearance and delivery.

The range widens for four reasons:

- **Origin port.** South China (Yantian, Shekou, Nansha) is faster than North China (Qingdao, Tianjin, Dalian) because more strings start there.
- **Service string.** An express string with fewer calls is faster than a loop that collects cargo across Asia.
- **Transhipment.** A feeder connection through Colombo, Singapore or Salalah adds time and a second handling risk.
- **Red Sea routing.** UAE ports are inside the Gulf, so they depend on Hormuz rather than Suez for Far East cargo. The Red Sea disruption affects Suez-bound services and war-risk pricing more than the direct Asia–Gulf run, but it changes carrier networks and equipment availability.

Air freight from China to Dubai is typically 3–7 days door to door, with a wide range depending on security screening, airline capacity and destination clearance. Dubai's air cargo hub is the region's strongest; that is both an advantage and a congestion risk.

## Duty, VAT and the free-zone question

For a mainland import, the arithmetic is straightforward:

- **Customs duty:** 5% of the CIF value on most goods. Some goods are 0%; a few are higher.
- **VAT:** 5% of the CIF value plus duty.
- **Other charges:** terminal handling, documentation, delivery order, inspection, brokerage.

For goods entering a free zone, the treatment changes. Goods are generally not subject to duty or VAT while they remain in the zone and are not released into the mainland. When they leave the zone for the UAE mainland, they become domestic imports and duty and VAT apply. When they are re-exported, the zone treatment can be preserved if the documentation and the physical movement support it.

The free-zone decision is therefore a business-model decision, not a tax trick:

- **Trading into the UAE mainland:** a mainland licence and a mainland import are usually simpler. The 5% duty and 5% VAT are part of the cost.
- **Re-export to the region:** a free zone can defer duty and VAT and simplify the re-export flow.
- **Ecommerce fulfilment:** a free zone can work for cross-border models, but the last-mile and returns economics often favour a mainland 3PL.
- **Manufacturing:** a free zone can work if the activity is licensed and the origin rules are understood.

The most common error is assuming that a free-zone company can sell into the mainland without triggering duty and VAT. It cannot. The second most common is assuming that a mainland licence covers a free-zone activity. It does not.

## Conformity: ESMA, ECAS and the emirate layer

The UAE regulates product conformity through federal standards and emirate-level enforcement. The key bodies and schemes:

- **ESMA** — the Emirates Authority for Standardization and Metrology — sets the federal standards framework.
- **ECAS** — the Emirates Conformity Assessment Scheme — covers regulated products such as electrical equipment, toys, cosmetics and some building materials.
- **EQM** — the Emirates Quality Mark — is a voluntary mark for products that meet the relevant standard.

For regulated products, the exporter needs a certificate of conformity and, in many cases, a registered label. The certificate must match the product, the model and the manufacturer. A CE or CB report is supporting evidence, not a substitute for the UAE certificate.

Three failure modes:

1. **Certificate for a different model.** The shipment contains a variant that the certificate does not cover.
2. **Wrong standard version.** The regulation has been updated and the certificate references the old version.
3. **Label not applied.** The certificate is valid, but the product or packaging lacks the required Arabic/English label.

Food and cosmetics add another layer. Dubai Municipality registers food products and premises; cosmetics and personal care products need registration through the relevant emirate system. A food shipment without registration can be held even with a perfect customs declaration.

Telecoms and radio equipment need type approval from the Telecommunications and Digital Government Regulatory Authority. Phones, routers, smart watches and anything with a radio module are in scope.

## Documents and the importer of record

A clean UAE import file usually contains:

- Commercial invoice
- Packing list
- Bill of lading or airway bill
- Certificate of origin
- Certificate of conformity, where applicable
- Insurance certificate
- Arabic labelling artwork, where required
- Trade licence and customs registration of the importer

The importer of record must be a UAE entity with a valid trade licence and customs registration. A free-zone licence is not a mainland import licence; a mainland licence does not cover free-zone activity. The licence activity must cover the product.

This is the point where many DDP quotes fail. The forwarder quotes an all-in price, then uses the consignee's licence, then discovers the licence does not cover the product or the consignee cannot recover the VAT. The fix is to agree the importer, the licence activity and the VAT treatment before booking.

## Cost structure and the drivers that matter

A China–UAE landed cost has six layers:

| Layer | Typical share | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean or air freight | 30–50% | Service, season, fuel, capacity |
| Origin charges | 5–10% | THC, documentation, export clearance |
| Destination charges | 15–25% | Terminal handling, delivery order, inspection |
| Duty | 5% of CIF | HS line and origin |
| VAT | 5% of CIF+duty | Recoverable, but a cash-flow cost |
| Inland delivery | 10–25% | Distance, congestion, empty return |

The drivers that change the total most:

- **Port and emirate.** Jebel Ali versus Khalifa versus Khor Fakkan changes the land leg and the broker cost.
- **Free-zone status.** A zone movement can defer duty and VAT, but adds documentation and a zone operator fee.
- **Weight and volume.** UAE road pricing is weight- and volume-sensitive.
- **Season.** Q4, Ramadan and Chinese New Year move both rates and clearance times.
- **Cargo type.** DG, reefer and project cargo need pre-approval and carry surcharges.
- **Delivery window.** Dubai has truck restrictions and peak-hour congestion; a night delivery may cost more but save a day.

As a planning range, a 40-foot container from South China to Jebel Ali typically prices 10–25% below the same box to Saudi Arabia on the ocean leg, before duty and inland. The gap narrows once the Saudi inland leg is included. Do not compare ocean rates across destinations; compare landed cost.

## Ecommerce and the last mile

The UAE is the GCC's most developed ecommerce market, and that changes freight demand. Amazon FBA, noon and independent stores create a flow of small parcels, returns and replenishment that does not fit a pure FCL model.

The practical options:

- **Air express** for launch stock and fast-moving SKUs.
- **LCL or consolidation** for replenishment that is not time-critical.
- **FCL to a 3PL** for mature catalogues with predictable demand.
- **Free-zone fulfilment** for cross-border models, with the mainland duty and VAT triggered on delivery.

The hidden cost is returns. A returns rate of 5–15% is normal in ecommerce, and the reverse logistics leg is often priced worse than the outbound leg. Ask the 3PL for the returns process and the disposal/re-export options before you choose a model.

## Risks and failure modes

**Free-zone versus mainland confusion.** The most common structural error. The licence, the declaration and the duty treatment must match the actual destination.

**Certificate mismatch.** A regulated product arrives without a valid ECAS certificate or with a certificate for a different model. The cargo sits.

**Under-valuation.** A low invoice triggers a valuation review and a penalty risk. The UAE compares declared values with regional and historical data.

**Wrong importer licence activity.** A trading licence cannot import regulated food or medical devices. The activity must be on the licence.

**Jebel Ali congestion.** The port is efficient but busy. Peak season, a crane failure or a customs IT outage can add days.

**Khor Fakkan land bridge.** A cheap ocean rate can be erased by a two-hour truck leg and a limited empty-return network.

**Delivery without a slot.** Dubai buildings and malls often require a delivery booking. A truck that arrives without one waits, and waiting is charged.

## How to act: a five-step sequence

1. **Decide mainland or free zone.** This determines the licence, the duty treatment and the declaration.
2. **Check the product regulation.** ESMA/ECAS, food registration, telecom type approval. Do this before you book.
3. **Choose the port by consignee.** Jebel Ali for Dubai and re-export, Khalifa for Abu Dhabi, Sharjah for the industrial belt or the east coast.
4. **Confirm the importer licence and VAT registration.** The licence activity must cover the product.
5. **Quote the total.** Freight, origin, destination, duty, VAT, inland and a clearance buffer.

## Our read

The UAE is the right first GCC market for most Chinese exporters. It has the deepest logistics pool, the most frequent services and the most predictable clearance — provided the licence and the free-zone question are settled.

The mistake to avoid is treating the UAE as a single market. Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Sharjah have different ports, different brokers and different cost structures. A quote that says "UAE" without a port and a licence is not a quote.

For ecommerce, the UAE is also the region's test bed: if a product cannot work in Dubai's last-mile economics, it will struggle in Riyadh and Kuwait.

## FAQ

**How long does sea freight take from China to the UAE?**
Plan 16–26 days port to port to Jebel Ali, plus 3–7 days for clearance and delivery. North China origins and transhipment add time.

**How much is duty and VAT in the UAE?**
Most goods attract 5% duty on CIF and 5% VAT on CIF plus duty. Some goods are duty-free. Free-zone goods are generally exempt while they remain in the zone.

**Can I import into a UAE free zone and sell locally?**
Yes, but the goods become a mainland import when they leave the zone. Duty and VAT apply at that point.

**Do I need a UAE licence to import?**
Yes. The importer must be a UAE entity with a trade licence and customs registration, and the licence activity must cover the product.

**Is Jebel Ali always the best port?**
No. It is best for Dubai and re-export. Khalifa is better for Abu Dhabi, and Sharjah's Khor Fakkan can be better for the east coast or when Hormuz risk rises.

**What is ECAS?**
The Emirates Conformity Assessment Scheme for regulated products. It is separate from a CE mark and must be obtained for the UAE market.

**Can I use a Dubai broker for an Abu Dhabi shipment?**
Only if the broker is licensed and registered there. Many Dubai brokers do not file regularly in Abu Dhabi, which shows up as delays.
