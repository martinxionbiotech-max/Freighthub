## Direct answer

GCC certification is product-and-shipment specific, not a single sticker. Saudi Arabia requires SABER PCoC and SCoC for regulated products; the UAE requires ESMA/ECAS certification; Kuwait uses KUCAS; Qatar uses QGOSM; Oman and Bahrain apply GSO standards with national additions. Start testing before production, register the certificate to the correct importer, and treat the shipment certificate as a separate task from the product certificate.

## Why certification is a product problem, not a customs problem

Customs brokers clear declarations. They do not certify products. A shipment that arrives without the right certificate cannot be cleared by a better broker, a higher payment or a phone call. The only fixes are to obtain the certificate or to re-export the cargo.

That is why certification belongs in product development, not in the shipping department. The questions to answer before the first order:

- Is the product in a regulated category?
- Which standard applies, and which version?
- Which laboratory can test to that standard?
- Which entity will hold the certificate?
- Does the certificate cover the exact model, rating and manufacturer?
- What label is required, and where must it be applied?

If any answer is unclear, the shipment is not ready to book.

## Saudi Arabia: SABER and SASO

Saudi Arabia has the most structured conformity system in the GCC. SASO sets technical regulations; SABER is the online platform that registers certificates.

Two documents are required for regulated products:

**PCoC — Product Certificate of Conformity.** Issued for a product and manufacturer, based on testing and factory inspection. It has a validity period and covers specific models.

**SCoC — Shipment Certificate of Conformity.** Issued for a specific shipment, referencing the invoice and the PCoC. It is the document customs sees at the port.

The sequence is: test the product, obtain the PCoC, register it on SABER, then obtain the SCoC for each shipment. A PCoC without an SCoC is not enough; an SCoC without a valid PCoC cannot be issued.

SASO technical regulations cover a wide range of products, including:

- Low-voltage electrical equipment and appliances.
- Toys.
- Cosmetics and personal care.
- Food contact materials.
- Building materials — steel, cement, cables, insulation.
- Motor vehicles and tyres.
- Chemicals and detergents.
- Textiles and footwear.
- Machinery and equipment.

Sector regulators add requirements: SFDA for food, drugs, cosmetics and medical devices; CITC for telecoms and radio equipment; the Saudi Energy Efficiency Center for appliance labelling.

The most common Saudi failure is a **model mismatch**: the PCoC covers model A, the shipment contains model B, and the SCoC cannot be issued. The second is an **importer mismatch**: the PCoC is registered to a different importer than the shipment consignee.

## UAE: ESMA, ECAS and the emirate layer

The UAE's federal standards body is ESMA. The Emirates Conformity Assessment Scheme (ECAS) covers regulated products, and the Emirates Quality Mark (EQM) is a voluntary mark for products that meet the relevant standard.

The regulated categories include:

- Electrical and electronic equipment.
- Toys.
- Cosmetics and personal care.
- Building materials.
- Food contact materials.
- Motor vehicle parts and tyres.
- Detergents and chemicals.

The certificate is issued through an accredited conformity assessment body and registered with ESMA. For some categories, the product must also carry the ECAS mark or a label.

The UAE adds an emirate layer. Dubai Municipality registers food and cosmetic premises and products; Abu Dhabi and Sharjah have their own inspection regimes. A federal certificate does not automatically satisfy a municipal food registration.

The most common UAE failure is a **standard version mismatch**: the certificate references an older version of the standard, and the inspector queries it. The second is a **label gap**: the certificate is valid, but the product or packaging lacks the required Arabic/English label.

## Kuwait: KUCAS

Kuwait's conformity scheme is KUCAS, administered by the Public Authority for Industry. It requires a certificate of conformity for regulated products, issued through an accredited body.

The categories are similar to the rest of the GCC: electrical equipment, toys, building materials, chemicals, food contact materials and some consumer goods. The certificate must match the product, the model and the manufacturer.

Kuwait's additional twist is the **commercial agency law**. For some products, the importer must be a registered agent, and the certificate may need to be issued in the agent's name. That makes the agency decision a certification decision as well.

## Qatar: QGOSM

Qatar's General Organization for Standards and Metrology administers standards and conformity. Regulated products need a certificate of conformity, and food, cosmetics and telecoms have separate regulators.

Qatar's market is small, so the certificate cost per unit can be high for a first shipment. That is a reason to combine product families under one certificate where the standard allows it, rather than certifying each SKU separately.

## Oman and Bahrain

Oman's Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion administers standards, with sector regulators for food, health and telecoms. Bahrain's Standards and Metrology Directorate sits within the Ministry of Industry and Commerce. Both use GSO standards as the baseline and add national requirements.

For a shipper, the practical approach is to treat Oman and Bahrain as **GSO-plus** markets: a certificate that meets the GSO standard is a good starting point, but confirm the national additions, the label language and the importer registration before shipping.

## The G-Mark and GSO standards

The GCC Standardization Organization publishes standards that member states adopt. The G-Mark is a GCC conformity mark used for low-voltage electrical equipment and toys, indicating conformity with the relevant GSO standard.

The G-Mark is not a substitute for a national certificate. A product may need both the G-Mark and a Saudi PCoC, a UAE ECAS certificate or a Kuwaiti KUCAS certificate. The marks serve different legal purposes.

## The certification process, step by step

1. **Identify the regulation.** Use the HS code and the product category. Check the national regulator's list, not the supplier's assumption.
2. **Identify the standard and version.** Standards are revised; a certificate to the old version may be rejected.
3. **Test the product.** Use a laboratory accredited for the standard. Test reports are the basis for the certificate.
4. **Inspect the factory if required.** Some schemes require a factory audit, especially for toys, electricals and building materials.
5. **Issue the product certificate.** The certificate names the manufacturer, the product, the models and the standard.
6. **Register the certificate.** In Saudi Arabia, register the PCoC on SABER. In the UAE, register with ESMA through the conformity body.
7. **Apply the label.** The label must be on the product or packaging as the regulation requires, in the required language.
8. **Issue the shipment certificate.** For Saudi Arabia, the SCoC; for other markets, the per-shipment certificate or release.
9. **Keep the file.** The certificate, test report, label artwork and invoice should be retrievable for the customs broker and the importer.

The timeline is weeks, not days. Testing alone can take two to six weeks, and a factory audit adds more. A certificate obtained after the vessel sails is a certificate obtained after the cargo has already missed its window.

## Cost: what drives it

Certification cost is driven by:

- **Number of models.** Each model or variant may need separate testing.
- **Number of standards.** A product can fall under several regulations.
- **Testing complexity.** EMC, safety, chemical and performance tests have different costs.
- **Factory audit.** Travel and audit time for the auditor.
- **Certificate validity.** A multi-year certificate costs more up front but less per shipment.
- **Country coverage.** Six national certificates cost more than one.

As a planning range, a single-product certificate for one GCC country can cost from a few hundred to several thousand dollars, depending on the category. A multi-country, multi-model programme can run into five figures. The cost per shipment falls sharply after the first certificate.

## The mismatches that stop cargo

**Model mismatch.** The certificate covers a product family, but the shipment contains a variant outside the family.
**Manufacturer mismatch.** The certificate names the factory, but the goods were made in a different plant.
**Importer mismatch.** The certificate is registered to a different importer than the consignee.
**Standard version mismatch.** The certificate references an old version of the standard.
**Label mismatch.** The label is in the wrong language, the wrong format or missing.
**Certificate expiry.** The certificate expired between production and shipment.
**Scope mismatch.** The product is in a regulated category that the exporter did not identify.

Each mismatch has the same consequence: the shipment cannot be cleared until it is resolved, and the resolution can take weeks.

## How to act: a pre-shipment checklist

- Confirm the HS code and the regulated category for each destination country.
- Confirm the standard and version for each country.
- Confirm the certificate holder and the importer of record.
- Confirm the certificate covers the exact model, rating and factory.
- Confirm the label artwork and language.
- Confirm the shipment certificate can be issued before arrival.
- Confirm the certificate's expiry date is after the planned shipment date.
- Keep a digital copy of the full file with the broker.

## Our read

Certification is the most underestimated cost in GCC market entry. Exporters budget for freight and duty, then discover that the certificate, the testing and the label cost more than the first shipment's margin.

The shippers who succeed treat certification as a one-time capital investment. They certify the product family, register it correctly, and then reuse the certificate across shipments. The ones who fail treat it as a per-shipment formality and re-learn the same lesson every time.

Saudi Arabia is the strictest and the most digitised. If a product can pass SABER, it is usually close to passing the other GCC schemes, with national additions. That makes Saudi certification a useful benchmark even for exporters whose first market is the UAE.

## FAQ

**Do I need SABER for every product?**
Only for products covered by SASO technical regulations. Most consumer and electrical goods are covered; some industrial inputs are not. Check the HS code and the regulation.

**What is the difference between PCoC and SCoC?**
PCoC is the product certificate, tied to the product and manufacturer. SCoC is the shipment certificate, tied to a specific invoice and shipment.

**Can I use a CE certificate in the GCC?**
As supporting evidence, yes. As a substitute for the national certificate, no. The GCC schemes require their own certificate and, in some cases, their own testing.

**How long does certification take?**
Testing and certification typically take weeks, not days. A factory audit adds time. Plan the certificate before production, not before shipment.

**Who should hold the certificate?**
The importer of record or the manufacturer, depending on the scheme and the country. In Saudi Arabia, the PCoC is registered to an importer, and the SCoC references the shipment.

**Is the G-Mark enough?**
No. The G-Mark is a GCC mark for certain product categories, but national certificates may still be required.

**What happens if the certificate is wrong?**
The shipment is held at the port until the certificate is corrected or re-issued. Storage and demurrage accrue, and the delivery date slips.
