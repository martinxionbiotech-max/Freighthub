## Direct answer

Oman is a two-coast market. Sohar serves the north, Muscat and the industrial belt; Salalah serves the south and the Indian Ocean transhipment trade. Both sit outside the Strait of Hormuz, which makes Oman a strategic hedge when the strait is disrupted. Plan 16–28 days port to port, then 3–7 days for clearance. VAT is 5%, duty is usually 5%, and the land border to the UAE is a real alternative to sea for some cargo.

## Two coasts, two strategies

Oman's geography explains its port system. The country sits on the south-eastern corner of the Arabian Peninsula, with the Gulf of Oman to the north-east and the Arabian Sea to the south. The Strait of Hormuz is to the north, between Oman's Musandam exclave and Iran.

That gives Oman two commercial strategies.

**Sohar** is the northern strategy. It sits on the Gulf of Oman, outside the strait, about 200 km north-west of Muscat and close to the UAE border. It serves the Sohar industrial port, the free zone, the steel and aluminium plants, and the northern population. It is the natural port for Muscat, the Batinah coast and cargo moving to or from the UAE.

**Salalah** is the southern strategy. It sits on the Arabian Sea, close to the Indian Ocean shipping lanes, and is a genuine transhipment hub. Salalah handles containers for Oman's southern region, but its larger role is transhipment between deep-sea services and feeders for the Indian Ocean, East Africa and the Gulf.

**Duqm** is the long-term industrial strategy. It is a special economic zone with a port, dry dock, refinery and land for heavy industry. It is not a general container gateway today, but it matters for project cargo and energy investment.

The practical rule: **Sohar for the north and Muscat; Salalah for the south and transhipment; Duqm for projects.** Muscat itself has no commercial container port — Port Sultan Qaboos was converted to cruise and tourism, and commercial traffic moved to Sohar.

## Transit and the Hormuz hedge

China–Oman transit depends heavily on the port.

- **South China to Salalah:** roughly 16–26 days, with direct calls on Indian Ocean strings.
- **South China to Sohar:** roughly 18–28 days, often direct or via a Gulf hub.
- **North China:** add 3–5 days.
- **Clearance and delivery:** 3–7 days with a complete file.
- **Air freight:** 3–7 days to Muscat, with limited direct freighter capacity.

The strategic value of Oman is the Hormuz hedge. Sohar and Salalah are outside the strait, so cargo can be discharged and moved by road or feeder without a strait transit. When Hormuz risk rises, that option becomes valuable — not just for Oman-bound cargo, but for UAE-bound cargo that can enter through Sohar and cross the land border.

The hedge has a cost. The road from Sohar to Dubai is roughly 200 km, and the border crossing at Al Buraimi/Hatta adds time. The road from Salalah to the UAE is much longer and not practical for most container cargo. So the hedge is real for the north, limited for the south.

## Customs, duty and VAT

Oman Customs administers clearance through its electronic system. The file mechanics are familiar: a licensed broker files the declaration, attaches the invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin and conformity documents, and the shipment is routed for inspection or release.

The cost structure:

- **Customs duty:** 5% of CIF on most goods, 0% on some essentials, higher on selected items.
- **VAT:** 5% since April 2021, charged on CIF plus duty.
- **Other charges:** terminal handling, documentation, delivery order, inspection, brokerage.

Oman-specific points:

**The importer must be an Omani entity.** A foreign seller cannot simply declare itself the importer. The commercial registration and the customs account must match the importer.

**Valuation is CIF-based.** Oman Customs compares declared values with regional data. A low invoice triggers a review, and the review consumes free time.

**Inspection is risk-based.** A clean file reduces the probability of a physical inspection but does not eliminate it.

**Free-zone goods are treated separately.** Goods entering Sohar Freezone, Salalah Free Zone or Duqm SEZ are generally not subject to duty or VAT while they remain in the zone. When they leave for the Omani mainland, duty and VAT apply.

## Conformity and food

Oman's standards system is administered by the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion, with sector regulators for food, health, telecoms and building materials. The GCC Standardization Organization's standards are the baseline, and Oman adds national requirements.

The categories that matter most for China-origin cargo:

- **Electrical and electronic goods.** Safety, plug and voltage requirements.
- **Food and beverages.** Registration, Arabic labelling, shelf-life and halal requirements.
- **Cosmetics.** Registration and labelling.
- **Building materials.** Standards for steel, cement, cables and insulation.
- **Telecoms.** Type approval for radio equipment.

The failure mode is the Gulf standard: a certificate for a different model, a certificate issued to a trading company rather than the manufacturer, or a label that does not meet the Arabic requirement. A customs broker cannot fix a product that is not registered.

## Importer, free zones and the land border

The importer of record must be an Omani entity with a commercial registration and a customs account. For free-zone tenants, the zone operator adds a layer of procedure.

The free zones:

- **Sohar Freezone** — industrial and logistics, adjacent to the port. Good for manufacturing, processing and distribution.
- **Salalah Free Zone** — logistics and light manufacturing, adjacent to the transhipment port.
- **Duqm SEZ** — heavy industry, energy and project cargo.
- **Al Mazunah Free Zone** — on the Yemen border, a smaller trade zone.

The land border matters more in Oman than in any other GCC market. The UAE border at Al Buraimi is a short drive from Sohar, and goods can move by truck between the two countries. The Saudi border via the Empty Quarter road is long and lightly used for container cargo, but it exists.

For a UAE-bound consignee, the choice is:

1. **Direct sea to Jebel Ali**, then truck to the destination.
2. **Sea to Sohar**, then truck across the border.

Option 2 can be faster when Jebel Ali is congested or when Hormuz risk is high. It can also be cheaper on the ocean leg. The border clearance, the truck and the extra documentation usually erase the saving for normal cargo; for time-sensitive or strait-averse cargo, it can win.

## Cost structure and the drivers

A China–Oman landed cost has the same six layers as any GCC import:

| Layer | Typical share | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean or air freight | 35–55% | Service, transhipment, season |
| Origin charges | 5–10% | THC, documentation, export clearance |
| Destination charges | 10–20% | Port, inspection, broker |
| Duty | 5% of CIF | HS line |
| VAT | 5% of CIF+duty | Recoverable, but a cash-flow cost |
| Inland delivery | 5–15% | Muscat, Sohar, Salalah distances |

Oman is often 5–15% cheaper per container than Saudi Arabia on the ocean leg and similar to the UAE. The land leg is shorter than Saudi Arabia's for Muscat and Sohar, but longer for Salalah and the south.

Other drivers:

- **Transhipment at Salalah.** A connection through Salalah can be fast or slow depending on the feeder schedule.
- **Project cargo.** Duqm and the energy sector create demand for heavy lift and flat racks.
- **Ramadan and Eid.** Food and consumer goods peak; working hours shorten.
- **Summer.** June–September heat and humidity affect reefer and outdoor work.
- **Border delays.** The UAE border can queue at peak times; a truck booked for a morning crossing may not clear until the afternoon.

## Muscat, the Batinah and the free-zone map

Oman's population and industry are concentrated in the north, along the Batinah coast between Sohar and Muscat. That concentration explains why Sohar matters more than its size suggests.

Muscat is the capital and the largest consumer market, but it has no commercial container port. Port Sultan Qaboos, the historic harbour in the city, was converted to cruise and tourism, and the commercial traffic moved to Sohar. The result is a 200 km truck leg between the port and the capital, which is short by Saudi standards but still a cost that a Dubai-based forwarder may not price correctly.

The Batinah coast between Sohar and Muscat is where much of Oman's light industry, food processing and logistics warehousing sits. Sohar Industrial Port, the free zone and the aluminium and steel plants anchor the northern economy. For a supplier of machinery, building materials or industrial inputs, Sohar is the natural entry point.

The free-zone map is more diverse than most shippers expect:

- **Sohar Freezone** — industrial and logistics, adjacent to the port, focused on manufacturing, processing and distribution.
- **Salalah Free Zone** — logistics and light manufacturing around the transhipment port.
- **Duqm SEZ** — a very large special economic zone with a port, dry dock, refinery and land for heavy industry.
- **Al Mazunah Free Zone** — a smaller trade zone on the Yemen border, oriented to cross-border trade.

Each zone has its own incentives, its own customs arrangements and its own tenant mix. The incentives usually include duty suspension, simplified licensing and, in some cases, tax holidays. They do not include an exemption from Omani product standards, and they do not make a foreign seller the importer of record.

For a distributor, the practical choice is between a mainland warehouse in Muscat or Sohar and a free-zone warehouse in Sohar or Salalah. The mainland option is simpler for domestic sales; the free-zone option is better for re-export and for holding stock without paying duty up front. The decision should be made with the tax adviser, not the freight forwarder.

## Risk and compliance

**Port choice confusion.** Sohar and Salalah serve different markets. A quote that says "Oman" without a port is incomplete.

**Hormuz assumption.** Oman is outside the strait, but not every Omani port is equally suited to every cargo. Salalah is a transhipment hub, not a Dubai substitute.

**Conformity gap.** A regulated product without the right certificate is held.

**Food shelf life.** A long transit leg can make a compliant shipment non-compliant on arrival.

**Border bottleneck.** The UAE border at Al Buraimi can queue, and the documentation for a cross-border move is separate from the Omani import file.

**Free-zone boundary.** Goods leaving a free zone for the Omani mainland trigger duty and VAT. The documentation must support the movement.

## How to act: a five-step sequence

1. **Choose the coast.** Sohar for the north and Muscat; Salalah for the south; Duqm for projects.
2. **Confirm the product regulation.** Standards, food, telecoms. Before booking.
3. **Confirm the importer and broker.** Omani licence, customs account, product registration.
4. **Decide free zone or mainland.** The duty and VAT treatment follows.
5. **Quote the total.** Freight, destination, duty, VAT, inland and a border buffer if the cargo is UAE-bound.

## Salalah's transhipment role

Salalah is not only an Omani import port. It is a transhipment hub, where deep-sea services exchange cargo with feeders for the Indian Ocean, East Africa and the Gulf. For a shipper whose cargo is transhipped there, the port is a connection point rather than a destination. That changes the risk profile: a missed feeder is a delay, and the port's performance matters more than the Omani inland leg.

## Our read

Oman is the GCC's most underrated logistics market. It has deep water, two coasts, free zones, a stable currency and a government that wants industrial investment. It is also small, with a limited forwarder pool and a service board that rewards planning.

The strategic case is the Hormuz hedge. If the strait is disrupted, Sohar becomes a UAE back door and Salalah becomes an Indian Ocean relay. That case is worth building for a shipper with regional exposure, even if the day-to-day rate is not the lowest.

The tactical case is simpler: for Muscat and the north, Sohar is a good port with a short land leg; for the south, Salalah is the only sensible option.

## FAQ

**How long does sea freight take from China to Oman?**
Plan 16–26 days to Salalah and 18–28 days to Sohar from South China, plus 3–7 days for clearance. North China adds 3–5 days.

**Does Oman have VAT?**
Yes, 5% since April 2021, charged on CIF plus duty.

**Which Omani port serves Muscat?**
Sohar. Port Sultan Qaboos in Muscat is now cruise and tourism, not a commercial container gateway.

**Is Oman outside the Strait of Hormuz?**
Sohar and Salalah are outside the strait. Musandam, the northern exclave, controls the strait's southern side.

**Can I use Oman as a back door to the UAE?**
Yes, via Sohar and the Al Buraimi border. It can be faster or cheaper when Jebel Ali is congested, but the border and truck costs usually erase the saving for normal cargo.

**Do I need a certificate of conformity?**
For regulated products, yes. The Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion and sector regulators administer the requirements.

**What is Duqm?**
A special economic zone and port on Oman's central coast, focused on heavy industry, energy and project cargo. It is not a general container gateway.
