## Direct answer

DDP means the seller delivers the goods cleared for import and pays duty and taxes. In the GCC, that is harder than it sounds: the importer of record must normally be a licensed local entity, so most "DDP" shipments use the buyer or a broker as the importer and the seller reimburses the cost. DDP works for samples, ecommerce and low-value goods. It fails on high-value, regulated or VAT-recoverable cargo unless the importer-of-record question is settled in writing.

## Key facts

| Item | Position in the GCC | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Incoterm | DDP — Delivered Duty Paid (Incoterms 2020) | The only Incoterm where the seller clears for import |
| Importer of record | Usually a licensed local entity | A foreign seller cannot simply declare itself the importer |
| Duty | GCC common tariff, often 5% | The seller bears it under DDP |
| VAT | 15% Saudi, 5% UAE, 5% Oman, 10% Bahrain, 0% Qatar and Kuwait | VAT may be recoverable by the importer, not the seller |
| Certification | SABER, ESMA/ECAS, KUCAS, QGOSM | The seller bears the cost of a certificate gap |
| Best fit | Samples, ecommerce, low-value, unlicensed buyers | The compliance burden is proportionate |
| Worst fit | High-value, regulated, VAT-recoverable cargo | The hidden costs and the liability are disproportionate |
| Alternative | DAP, CIF, FOB, or a landed-cost contract | Clearer allocation of risk |

## What DDP actually obliges the seller to do

Under Incoterms 2020, DDP requires the seller to deliver the goods to the named place, cleared for import, ready for unloading. The seller bears all costs and risks to that point, including:

- Export clearance and any export duties.
- Main carriage.
- Import clearance.
- Customs duty, VAT and any other import taxes.
- Delivery to the named place.

The buyer's obligation is to take delivery and pay the price. That is the theory.

In the GCC, the theory meets a structural obstacle. Import clearance requires an importer of record with a local commercial registration, a customs account and, for regulated goods, product registrations in its name. A Chinese seller generally does not have those. So the DDP contract is performed through one of three structures:

1. **The buyer is the importer of record.** The seller pays the duty and VAT, but the declaration is in the buyer's name. The seller reimburses the buyer or the broker.
2. **The forwarder's local entity is the importer.** The forwarder imports in its own name and sells to the buyer. This is a true DDP structure, but it requires the forwarder to have a local licence and to accept the compliance risk.
3. **A nominated importer of record is used.** A local agent or a free-zone entity imports and transfers the goods. The structure must be documented, or it looks like an undeclared sale.

Each structure has a different risk profile. The first is common but depends on the buyer's cooperation. The second is cleaner but rarer and more expensive. The third is the one that most often goes wrong.

## Why "all-in DDP" quotes fail

A DDP quote is a promise about costs the seller does not control. The failures cluster around five issues.

**Duty classification.** The seller quotes on one HS code, the customs authority classifies on another, and the duty difference is the seller's loss. A product described as "machine parts" can be classified as a specific machine, a component or a part, with different rates.

**Valuation.** Customs values the goods on CIF, not on the invoice's FOB price. If the seller's DDP price excludes freight and insurance, the duty base is higher than expected. Related-party transactions and low invoices attract scrutiny.

**VAT recovery.** In Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Oman and Bahrain, VAT is recoverable by a registered importer. If the seller includes VAT in a DDP price, the buyer may recover it and the seller has effectively overpaid. If the seller excludes it, the buyer may be surprised at the border. The contract must say which party accounts for VAT.

**Certification.** A SABER PCoC, an ECAS certificate or a KUCAS certificate is issued to a specific importer and product. If the seller's DDP structure uses a different importer, the certificate may not be valid for the shipment. The seller then bears the cost of re-issuing it, or the cargo sits.

**Hidden destination charges.** Terminal handling, delivery order, inspection, storage, demurrage and inland delivery are often excluded from the ocean quote. A DDP price that covers "freight and duty" but not destination charges is not DDP.

The practical test: ask the forwarder to itemise the DDP price into freight, origin charges, destination charges, duty, VAT and delivery. If any line is "miscellaneous" or "as per actual", the price is not fixed.

## DDP versus DAP versus CIF

The three terms are often confused.

| Term | Seller clears export | Seller pays main carriage | Seller clears import | Seller pays duty/VAT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FOB | Yes | No | No | No |
| CIF | Yes | Yes (to port) | No | No |
| DAP | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| DDP | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

**DAP** is the cleaner choice for most GCC imports. The seller delivers to the named place, the buyer clears and pays duty. The buyer, who has the local licence and the VAT registration, controls the customs file and recovers the VAT. The seller avoids a liability it cannot manage.

**CIF** is a port-to-port term. It is useful for commodity trades and for buyers who have their own broker, but it leaves the buyer with the import clearance and the inland leg.

**DDP** is useful when the buyer has no import capability, when the goods are low-value, or when the seller has a local entity that can act as the importer.

## When DDP is the right contract

DDP works when the compliance burden is small and the buyer values simplicity.

- **Samples and trade-show cargo.** Low value, fast clearance, no VAT recovery interest.
- **Ecommerce and small parcels.** The buyer is a consumer, not an importer. A DDP structure with a local fulfilment partner is standard.
- **Spare parts and urgent service items.** The buyer needs the part, not a customs project.
- **Low-value industrial consumables.** The duty is small and the certificate burden is light.
- **Buyers without an import licence.** A new distributor or a project office may not have the registration yet. A DDP structure bridges the gap.

In these cases, the seller should still confirm the importer of record, the HS code and the certificate position before quoting.

## When DDP is the wrong contract

DDP fails when the seller is bearing risks it cannot price.

- **High-value capital goods.** A duty classification dispute on a seven-figure shipment is not a rounding error.
- **Regulated products.** SABER, SFDA, ECAS, KUCAS and QGOSM certificates are issued against a specific importer and product. A DDP structure that uses a different importer creates a mismatch.
- **VAT-recoverable purchases.** A Saudi or UAE company that can recover VAT does not want the seller to bury VAT in the price. It wants a clean tax invoice and its own declaration.
- **Buyers with their own broker.** The buyer's broker knows the local process and may be cheaper than the seller's.
- **Products with variable duty.** Textiles, food, chemicals and steel can have rates that depend on classification, origin or quota. A fixed DDP price is a bet.
- **Cargo that may be re-exported.** A free-zone or re-export flow needs a different structure; DDP assumes a domestic delivery.

## The VAT arithmetic

VAT is the most misunderstood line in a DDP quote. The basic formula for a GCC import is:

**VAT = rate × (CIF + duty + other dutiable charges)**

The rates in late 2026:

| Country | VAT rate | Recoverable by a registered importer? |
|---|---|---|
| Saudi Arabia | 15% | Yes, subject to the return |
| UAE | 5% | Yes, subject to the return |
| Oman | 5% | Yes, subject to the return |
| Bahrain | 10% | Yes, subject to the return |
| Qatar | 0% | Not applicable |
| Kuwait | 0% | Not applicable |

If the seller includes VAT in the DDP price and the buyer is registered, the buyer may recover the VAT while the seller has already paid it. The result is a windfall for the buyer and a loss for the seller, unless the contract says otherwise. If the seller excludes VAT, the buyer may face a charge at the border that it did not expect.

The clean solution is to state in the contract:

- Who is the importer of record.
- Who pays duty and VAT at the border.
- Whether VAT is included in the price and, if so, at what rate and on what base.
- Who receives the VAT certificate and the customs declaration.
- What happens if the rate changes between quotation and shipment.

## Building a DDP price

A defensible DDP price has eight lines:

1. **Ex-works or FOB cost** of the goods.
2. **Origin charges** — export clearance, THC, documentation.
3. **Main carriage** — ocean or air freight.
4. **Insurance** — at least 110% of CIF, if the contract requires it.
5. **Destination charges** — terminal handling, delivery order, inspection.
6. **Duty** — HS-specific, on CIF.
7. **VAT** — rate-specific, on CIF plus duty, with the recovery assumption stated.
8. **Inland delivery** — to the named place, including unloading if agreed.

Add a contingency for classification, inspection and storage. A DDP quote with no contingency is a quote that assumes everything goes right.

## Compliance and liability

Under DDP, the seller is the importer in commercial terms, even if the declaration is in another entity's name. That creates liability exposure:

- **Customs penalties** for misclassification or undervaluation.
- **Product safety liability** if the goods do not meet the local standard.
- **Storage and demurrage** if the file is incomplete.
- **Reputational risk** with the buyer and the broker.

Insurance should cover the goods, but it does not cover customs penalties. The contract should allocate them explicitly.

## Our read

DDP is a commercial convenience, not a compliance shortcut. In the GCC, the shippers who use it well are the ones who have a local entity or a trusted importer of record, a stable product range and a clear VAT position. The ones who use it badly are the ones who quote an all-in price to win an order and discover the duty classification, the certificate mismatch and the VAT recovery after the vessel sails.

For most industrial shippers, DAP plus a good local broker is the better contract. For ecommerce and samples, DDP is the right customer experience. For high-value or regulated cargo, neither DDP nor DAP substitutes for a proper importer-of-record structure.

## FAQ

**Is DDP legal in Saudi Arabia?**
Yes, but the importer of record must be a Saudi entity with a commercial registration and customs account. A foreign seller cannot declare itself the importer in the ordinary sense.

**Does DDP include VAT?**
Under Incoterms 2020, DDP includes all import taxes, so VAT is included in the seller's obligation. In practice, the contract should state whether VAT is in the price and who recovers it.

**What is the difference between DDP and DAP?**
DAP requires the buyer to clear the goods for import and pay duty and VAT. DDP requires the seller to do so.

**Can I get a VAT refund if I am not the importer?**
Generally no. VAT recovery follows the registered importer and the tax invoice. A seller that pays VAT through a broker may not be able to recover it.

**Why is my DDP quote higher than the FOB price plus freight?**
Because it includes duty, VAT, destination charges, certification and a risk margin. Ask for the itemisation.

**Is DDP cheaper than self-clearance?**
Sometimes, for small or low-value shipments. For large or regulated shipments, a local importer with its own broker is usually cheaper and safer.
