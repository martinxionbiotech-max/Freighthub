## Direct answer

FCL into the GCC is no longer a port-to-port purchase. For [Saudi Arabia](/countries/saudi-arabia/), book [Jeddah](/ports/jeddah/) or [Dammam](/ports/dammam/) direct and budget 25–40 days door to door. For the UAE, assume the box discharges at [Khor Fakkan](/ports/sharjah/) and moves by truck, adding 12–15 days and roughly USD 900–1,800 per FEU. Ocean freight in November 2026 runs about USD 2,200–7,500 per container depending on gateway, equipment and week. The line that decides your margin is not the ocean rate — it is free time, payload compliance and who pays when the box sits. *(Editorial judgment)*

## What changed in the last 60 days

**The Hormuz closure stopped being a shipping story and became a network story.** The Strait has been effectively closed to normal container liner traffic since the end of February 2026. Tanker traffic partially recovered in October, which confused importers reading oil prices as a reopening signal. Container services are weekly networks with fixed rotations and empty flows; once a carrier rebuilds around Khor Fakkan, [Sohar](/ports/sohar/) or Jeddah, reversing the change costs months. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

**13–19 October 2026.** Gemini relaunched its Asia–Middle East loop and replaced the Jebel Ali call with Khor Fakkan. This was not a cancellation; it was a permanent-looking redraw of the rotation. Jebel Ali still receives cargo, increasingly by truck. *(Reported fact)*

**30 October 2026.** Kuehne+Nagel measured average vessel waits of 7.25 days at Khor Fakkan and 8.86 at Fujairah. Container transfers from those ports to Jebel Ali, Sharjah and Abu Dhabi, normally about five days, were taking 12–15. Maersk imposed temporary booking restrictions on Khor Fakkan, Fujairah and selected Upper Gulf destinations. *(Reported fact)*

**Jeddah absorbed the Gulf's problem.** With Hormuz unreliable, Red Sea gateways took volume that would normally have discharged in the Gulf. Jeddah can take the ships but not always the boxes; Journal of Commerce reported carrier surcharges and booking curbs in August, and Hapag-Lloyd's 15-day onward-movement expectation for transit cargo is still the most under-priced clause in China→Saudi contracts. *(Reported fact)*

**Pricing stopped being linear.** Freightos reported Shanghai–Jebel Ali rising from under USD 2,000 to above USD 8,000 per container after the closure, with emergency conflict surcharges of USD 3,000/FEU from CMA CGM and Hapag-Lloyd. Those levels have moved, but the mechanism has not: risk is repriced per box. *(Reported fact)*

Every routing change is logged in our [update feed](/updates/). What did **not** change: the GCC common tariff, Saudi Arabia's 15% VAT, the UAE's 5% VAT, and the rule that a container is released only against payment. Customs did not slow down because shipping got harder; it slowed because more files arrive incomplete. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Why it matters: FCL economics flipped from container to position

In a normal market an FCL quote is a transport price: you buy space on a ship built to fill it. In November 2026 the constraint is not ship space but **position** — a legal discharge point, a truck, a chassis, a free-time clock and an empty return that all have to line up in the same week. *(Editorial judgment)*

A 40HQ from Shanghai to Jeddah is therefore at least four products: ocean space on a rotation that may call direct or feed from Salalah; terminal capacity at a yard being used as a buffer for other people's cargo; inland capacity, meaning a truck, a permit and a gate slot; and time, meaning free days and the option to return the box before the carrier's clock expires.

When all four are scarce at once, the marginal box is priced by desperation. Two quotes for the same route can differ by USD 2,000 and both be honest: one includes a truck from Khor Fakkan and seven free days, the other is port-to-port with three days and a "subject to space" note. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Container choice: four decisions that move more money than the rate

**20GP versus 40HQ.** The 20GP is the heavy box: about 33 cbm and 21–22 t of practical payload. The 40HQ is the volume box: about 76 cbm and 24–26 t. Dense goods — tiles, batteries, machinery parts, bagged chemicals — usually win on the 20GP because they hit the weight limit first. Furniture and packaging win on the 40HQ because they hit the cube limit first. *(Industry claim)*

**40GP versus 40HQ.** The high-cube adds about 6 cbm of height for a small rate premium. It is almost always worth it for light cargo and almost never worth it for cargo that will weigh out at 24 t. The trap is assuming the HQ has a higher payload; it does not. The box is taller, not stronger. *(Industry claim)*

**40NOR and 45HQ.** The NOR box is a high-cube with a standard door height for over-height machinery; it is a specialist product with thin availability in China and almost none in the Gulf. The 45HQ is rarely the right answer in the GCC: the extra five feet is useful in a warehouse, awkward on a road leg, and not universally accepted by truckers. Treat both as terminal-to-terminal options unless your forwarder names the equipment and the permit. *(Industry claim)*

**The pallet question is where cube disappears.** A 40HQ is about 2,350 mm wide inside. A 1,200 × 1,000 mm pallet loaded 1,000 mm across fits two side by side and leaves 350 mm; loaded 1,200 mm across it does not fit twice. Get the orientation wrong and you lose a row and roughly 10–12% of usable volume. This is the most common reason a "76 cbm" container only holds 63. *(Industry claim)*

## The weight trap: VGM says yes, the checkpoint says no

SOLAS VGM is an origin problem: you weigh the box, submit the figure, and the terminal accepts or rejects it. Gulf road limits are a destination problem: a container that is legal at the terminal gate can be over an axle limit on the Riyadh run, and the fine lands on the trucker, who passes it to you. *(Industry claim)*

Three practical rules:

- - **Do not book to the box's payload rating.** A 40HQ rated at 28 t is not a 28 t road proposition in Saudi Arabia or the UAE. Plan 24–26 t, and less for inland destinations.
- **Weigh the packed box, not the cargo.** Pallets, dunnage and wrap add 3–6% on a dense load; a shipment that leaves the factory at 23.5 t can present at 24.4 t.
- **Ask for the truck's axle configuration before you book.** A 3-axle tractor with a tri-axle chassis behaves differently from a 2-axle unit, and the difference is a permit, not a preference.

An overweight box is rejected at the gate, re-worked at your cost, or moved under a permit with a fine attached. In a market where the Jeddah gate queue is measured in days, that can cost a week. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Free time: the only clause that changes your margin

Demurrage is the clock inside the terminal; detention is the clock outside it. Both start after a free period that is negotiated, not given. At Jeddah and Dammam five to seven free days is the normal starting point; at UAE terminals, seven to ten in normal markets — though "normal" is doing a lot of work in November 2026. *(Industry claim)*

The arithmetic is asymmetric. Extending free time at origin costs a fixed fee, often USD 100–300 per container for a few extra days. Missing the free period costs USD 60–180 per 40HQ per day, plus storage and any relocation charge. Three days of demurrage can equal the entire origin handling charge; ten days can exceed the ocean freight. *(Industry claim)*

Three clauses to negotiate before the booking is confirmed:

1. **Named free days at the named terminal.** "Subject to carrier tariff" is not a number, and Jeddah's terminals do not all behave the same way.
2. **A demurrage and detention cap.** Ask for a daily and a total cap. Carriers will often trade a detention cap for a shorter free period; take that trade only if your clearance is genuinely fast.
3. **The relocation clause.** Hapag-Lloyd's Jeddah transit rules make the customer responsible for clearance and onward movement from 1 August 2026 discharge, with costs charged back. If your box is relocated, you pay for the move and the storage at the new location.

The most expensive version of this mistake is a DDP quote that includes "all destination charges" without defining free time. The seller assumes demurrage is the buyer's problem; the carrier bills the merchant holding the B/L, and the forwarder recovers from whoever is weaker in the negotiation. *(Editorial judgment)*

## How to act: the booking sequence that survives this market

**1. Choose the gateway from the consignee's postcode.** Riyadh and the centre lean Jeddah plus the land bridge. Dammam, Jubail and Al Khobar lean Dammam. Dubai and JAFZA should plan on Khor Fakkan plus truck. Abu Dhabi should test [Khalifa](/ports/khalifa/) first. Do not save USD 150 on ocean freight and spend USD 900 on a truck. *(Editorial judgment)*

**2. Confirm the discharge port, vessel and rotation in writing.** "Jebel Ali" on a quotation is a destination, not a discharge port, in this market. If the answer is "Jebel Ali via landbridge", price it as a Khor Fakkan booking with an inland leg and a through B/L. *(Industry claim)*

**3. Lock equipment and payload before the cut-off.** Confirm the container type, the VGM process and who weighs the box. If cargo is within 1 t of the road limit, split it or book a lighter payload.

**4. Buy free time and a D&D cap with the rate.** A booking without a free-time number is an open-ended liability. This is also the moment to decide who is importer of record; the [DDP guide](/guides/ddp/) explains why that changes who funds VAT.

**5. Pre-clear the file before the vessel sails.** In Saudi Arabia the SABER product certificate is the long pole and the shipment certificate is quick once the PC exists; in the UAE, ECAS and TDRA registrations are checked at clearance. A complete file clears in days, an incomplete one waits indefinitely. The [certification guide](/guides/certification/) covers the sequencing.

**6. Book the truck and the empty return before arrival.** Ask where the empty goes, who pays interchange and what happens if no slot is available. A box returnable only at Jebel Ali can accrue detention before it reaches your warehouse. *(Industry claim)*

Two structural notes explain most of the dispersion. First, **the Gulf risk premium is charged per box**, so it falls hardest on dense, low-value cargo: tiles and laptops pay the same surcharge, but only one can absorb it. Second, **space is allocated by contract, not spot price.** During the August squeeze, Chinese trade media reported Middle East rates above USD 10,000 per FEU; if your product cannot absorb a four-figure swing, you need a nominated allocation. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

## SOC containers: the quiet arbitrage in a broken empty flow

A shipper-owned container avoids carrier detention entirely: you own the box, so nobody can charge you for holding it. In a market where Jebel Ali's throughput fell 90.1% in Q2 2026 and empties are in the wrong place, SOC is rational for cargo with a long inland dwell and for project cargo that will sit at a site. It is not free: a used 40HQ costs several thousand dollars, repositioning is priced like a shipment, and a box stranded in a market with no export cargo is a write-down. The break-even is roughly three to four months of detention avoided. Below that, negotiate free time instead. *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

## Local detail: three gateways, three behaviours

**Jeddah.** The default for western and central Saudi and the transshipment door for Upper Gulf cargo. It handles about two-thirds of Saudi seaborne imports, and its terminals are operated separately, which is why two shipments to the same city can have different dwell times. The binding constraint in 2026 is the gate and the yard, not the berth: a box can arrive on schedule and still miss its window by a week. See the [Jeddah port profile](/ports/jeddah/). *(Reported fact + editorial judgment)*

**Dammam.** The Eastern Province door: Jubail, Al Khobar, Dhahran. Less transshipment load, so usually calmer, but fewer direct China rotations. For project and petrochemical cargo it is often correct even at a higher ocean rate. *(Editorial judgment)*

**Khor Fakkan and Jebel Ali.** Khor Fakkan sits outside Hormuz and has become the UAE's main container valve; Jebel Ali sits inside the strait and is largely restricted to ad hoc calls. The landbridge is a trucking product with a port name attached: 120 km of road and 12–15 days of total transfer in late October. For a Dubai consignee that is the last mile; for a Saudi consignee it is a border crossing and a second customs regime. See the [Jebel Ali profile](/ports/jebel-ali/) and the [Jebel Ali versus direct comparison](/compare/jebel-ali-vs-direct/). *(Reported fact)*

**Riyadh Dry Port.** The inland release valve: boxes move by rail or truck from Jeddah or Dammam and clear inland, which can be cheaper and faster if the rail slot and inland bond are arranged. It moves the queue rather than removing it. *(Industry claim)*

## Risk and compliance: the failure modes that cost containers

**Certificate gaps.** A SABER product certificate cannot be created at the port. If the PC does not exist when the vessel arrives, the container waits, and the wait has no defined end. This is the most expensive error in the category because it converts a freight delay into a compliance delay. *(Editorial judgment)*

**HS misclassification.** Choosing a code for a lower rate is a penalty risk, not optimisation. Saudi Customs can reassess duty, apply fines and flag the importer. Classify defensibly and price the real band. The [customs clearance guide](/guides/customs-clearance/) covers the document set.

**Dangerous goods.** Lithium batteries, aerosols, solvents and some machinery parts are regulated. A mis-declared DG box can be rejected at origin, offloaded at a hub, or held at destination. For batteries, UN38.3 and the MSDS are the minimum; the [batteries sector page](/sectors/batteries/) covers the product side.

**Sanctions and re-export.** The UAE suspended trade with Iran in August 2026 after an antiship missile strike. Goods with Iranian origin, ownership or end-use should not transit the UAE without legal review. A JAFZA re-export to Iran is a compliance red line, not a workaround. *(Reported fact)*

**Cargo abandonment and war risk.** If clearance fails and the consignee walks away, the seller faces storage, demurrage and eventual disposal or re-export; re-export from Saudi Arabia is possible but slow, and the costs sit with whoever is named on the B/L. Insurance covers a physical loss, not a commercial abandonment. A contract that fixes an all-in rate without a force-majeure and surcharge clause will be renegotiated by email at the worst moment. *(Editorial judgment)*

## Our read

**Do** book Jeddah or Dammam direct for Saudi cargo whenever a direct rotation exists. The landbridge through the UAE adds a border, a second customs regime and a transit document set that nobody prices properly.

**Do** buy free time at the same time as the rate, and put the D&D cap in the booking confirmation. This is the cheapest insurance in the market.

**Do** use 20GP for dense cargo and 40HQ for light cargo, and check pallet orientation before calculating cube: a 10% cube loss is a 10% cost increase that never appears on a quote.

**Avoid** comparing ocean rates across gateways without the inland leg: a USD 400 saving on the ocean can be a USD 1,200 loss on the truck.

**Avoid** assuming the Red Sea normalisation is complete. Suez container transits passed 50% of pre-disruption levels in October 2026, but attacks around Bab al-Mandeb continued and war-risk pricing stayed alive. Build a route that works at 70% reliability, not one that assumes 95%. *(Reported fact)*

**Avoid** DDP unless the seller can name the importer of record, the duty band and the VAT treatment in one sentence each. If they cannot, buy FOB or CIF and use a broker you have met. The [DDP versus self-clearance comparison](/compare/ddp-vs-self-clearance/) is the right starting point.

## FAQ

**How long does FCL take from China to Saudi Arabia?**
Port to port, 18–26 days to Jeddah on a direct rotation and 22–30 to Dammam, with 25–35 days if transshipment is involved. Add 5–12 days for clearance and inland delivery. For planning, use the 80th percentile, not the advertised transit time. The [China–Saudi route guide](/guides/china-to-saudi-arabia/) has the port-pair detail.

**Can I ship FCL to Riyadh?**
Yes, via Jeddah or Dammam to Riyadh Dry Port, by rail or truck. Clearing inland can be cheaper and faster than clearing at the coast if the rail slot and inland bond are arranged. It is not a shortcut around congestion; it moves the queue.

**Do I need SABER for an FCL shipment?**
Only for regulated products, but the regulated list is broad: electronics, electricals, toys, automotive parts, building materials, cosmetics and food-contact items are all in scope. You need a product certificate per product and a shipment certificate per consignment.
