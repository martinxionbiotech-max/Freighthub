## Direct answer

Saudi Arabia is the GCC's largest import market and its most document-heavy. Most China-origin containers enter through Jeddah on the Red Sea or Dammam on the Gulf, with Riyadh Dry Port as the inland release valve. Plan 18–30 days port to port, then 3–10 days for clearance if the SABER file is complete. Budget 15% VAT on the CIF value plus duty, and do not book until you know who the importer of record is.

## The three gateways and how they divide the country

Saudi Arabia is a large country with two coasts and one inland distribution centre. That geometry produces three distinct import routes.

**Jeddah Islamic Port** is the largest container gateway and the default for western Saudi, the holy cities and much of the consumer market. It also acts as a pressure valve for Upper Gulf cargo when Hormuz is disrupted. Its weakness is the landside: the port sits inside a city, the truck gate is constrained, and transit cargo competes with gateway cargo for the same yard.

**King Abdulaziz Port, Dammam** serves the Eastern Province, Riyadh and the industrial belt around Jubail. It is the better choice for cargo whose final destination is Riyadh or the east, and it avoids the Red Sea risk premium. Its weakness is a narrower service board than Jeddah and a rail link that is capacity-limited.

**Riyadh Dry Port** is not a seaport. It is an inland container terminal connected by rail to Dammam, where containers can be cleared and released closer to the consignee. For a Riyadh consignee, it can remove a 400 km truck leg and a few days of port dwell. It depends on rail slots, terminal release and a customs procedure that must be arranged before arrival.

The routing question is therefore: **where does the container stop being a ship and start being a truck?** Jeddah suits the west and south; Dammam suits the east and centre; Riyadh Dry Port suits the centre when the rail slot is available.

## Transit times and why the range is so wide

A published "18 days" is a proforma, not a promise. The realistic range from South China to Jeddah is 18–30 days, and to Dammam 20–32 days, depending on:

- **Direct call versus transhipment.** A direct Jeddah call saves 3–10 days; a transhipment through Jebel Ali or Salalah adds a second handling risk.
- **Port rotation.** A string that calls Singapore, Colombo and Jeddah in that order is slower than a direct express.
- **Red Sea routing.** Since late 2023, carriers have alternated between Suez and the Cape of Good Hope. The Cape route adds roughly 10–14 days to Asia–Europe and a similar detour to some Red Sea services. When Suez returns, schedules improve before rates do.
- **Congestion.** Jeddah's yard and gate have been the constraint in peak periods, not the berth.
- **Clearance.** Three to ten days is normal with a complete file; two to three weeks is normal with a SABER or valuation query.

For planning, use the upper half of the range. A production line that assumes the lower bound is a production line that stops.

## Customs: duty, VAT and valuation

Saudi customs duty is set by the GCC common tariff. Most industrial and consumer goods fall at 5%, some essentials at 0%, and selected categories — tobacco, some plastics, luxury goods — at higher bands. The HS classification decides the rate. A vague invoice description ("plastic articles", "machine parts") invites a reclassification and a delay.

Valuation is normally the CIF value: goods plus international freight plus insurance to the Saudi port. Related-party transactions and unusually low invoices attract scrutiny. Keep the commercial invoice, the payment evidence and the freight invoice consistent.

VAT is 15% and applies to the customs value plus duty. The formula is simple: **VAT = 15% × (CIF + duty)**. The cash-flow implication is not simple. VAT is usually paid at import and recovered later through the return, so a 15% charge on a large shipment can tie up working capital for a quarter.

Three common errors:

1. **Quoting VAT on the FOB value.** It is charged on CIF plus duty, so the number is higher.
2. **Assuming a free-zone exemption.** Saudi free zones are limited and conditional. Most imports are normal imports.
3. **Forgetting the 15% when pricing a DDP quote.** A DDP price that ignores VAT is not a DDP price.

## SABER and SASO: the certification gate

SABER is the online platform through which Saudi product conformity is registered. SASO, the Saudi Standards, Metrology and Quality Organization, sets the technical regulations. For regulated products, two certificates are needed:

- **PCoC — Product Certificate of Conformity.** Tied to the product and the manufacturer. It is the long-lived certificate.
- **SCoC — Shipment Certificate of Conformity.** Tied to a specific shipment and its invoice. It is issued per shipment.

The failure mode is always the same: the exporter has a PCoC for one model, ships a variant, and the SCoC cannot be issued. Or the certificate is registered by a Saudi importer that is not the importer on the shipment. Or the product is in a category that requires testing, and the test report predates the current regulation.

What to check before booking:

- Is the product in a SASO technical regulation? Check the HS line and the product category, not the marketing name.
- Does the PCoC cover the exact model, rating and manufacturer?
- Is the PCoC still valid, and is it registered to the same importer?
- Can the SCoC be issued before the vessel arrives?
- Is Arabic labelling required, and is it on the product or the packaging?

If the answer to any of these is "the supplier will handle it", get it in writing with a certificate number.

## Food, drugs and telecoms: the specialist regulators

Saudi Arabia routes certain categories through specialist agencies, and a customs broker cannot substitute for them.

**SFDA** — the Saudi Food and Drug Authority — regulates food, cosmetics, drugs, medical devices and feed. Food imports need product registration, Arabic labelling and, for some categories, a health certificate and halal evidence. Shelf life at arrival matters: a product with less than the required remaining shelf life can be rejected even with a valid registration.

**CITC** — the Communications, Information Technology and Communications Commission — type-approves telecom and radio equipment. Phones, routers, smart home devices and anything with a radio module need approval before import. A CE or FCC mark is not a substitute.

**SASO energy efficiency** — appliances, air conditioners, lighting and some motors need energy-efficiency registration and labelling. The label is part of the product, not a sticker added at the port.

The general lesson: **Saudi Arabia regulates products, not just shipments**. A shipment certificate cannot fix a product that is not registered.

## Documents and the importer of record

A clean Saudi import file usually contains:

- Commercial invoice, legalised where required
- Packing list with weights and dimensions
- Bill of lading or airway bill
- Certificate of origin
- SABER PCoC and SCoC, or the applicable SFDA/CITC approval
- Insurance certificate
- Arabic label artwork and, for food, the SFDA registration reference

The importer of record must be a Saudi entity with a commercial registration and customs registration. That is the structural reason DDP in Saudi Arabia is not the same as DDP in the EU: a foreign seller cannot simply declare itself the importer and pay the duty. It needs a Saudi importer, a licensed broker and a customs account.

This is where many "all-in" quotes break. The forwarder quotes DDP, then uses the consignee's importer profile, then discovers the consignee cannot deduct the VAT or does not have the right activity code. The fix is to agree the importer of record, the VAT treatment and the duty payer in writing before the booking.

## Cost structure and the drivers that matter

A China–Saudi landed cost has six layers:

| Layer | Typical share | What moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean or air freight | 35–55% | Service, season, fuel, Red Sea risk |
| Origin charges | 5–10% | THC, documentation, export clearance |
| Destination charges | 10–20% | Terminal handling, delivery order, inspection |
| Duty | 0–15% of CIF | HS line and origin |
| VAT | 15% of CIF+duty | Recoverable, but a cash-flow cost |
| Inland delivery | 10–25% | Distance, weight, empty return, season |

The ocean rate is the most visible and the least decisive. Two quotes that differ by 10% on freight can differ by 40% on the total once the destination charges and the inland leg are included.

Specific drivers to watch:

- **Weight and volume.** Saudi inland trucking is priced on both. A light bulky cargo pays for air, a dense cargo pays for weight.
- **Port choice.** Jeddah versus Dammam changes the inland distance and the risk premium.
- **Free time.** Jeddah's free time is consumed by clearance. A certificate gap turns it into storage.
- **Season.** Ramadan, Eid, National Day and the pre-Ramadan import peak move both rates and clearance times.
- **Cargo type.** DG, reefer and project cargo carry surcharges and need pre-approval.

## Inland distribution: the part that decides the route

Saudi Arabia's inland leg is long. Jeddah to Riyadh is roughly 950 km by road, Dammam to Riyadh roughly 400 km, and Jeddah to Jeddah's own industrial districts can still be an hour in traffic.

That makes the port choice a distribution decision. A Riyadh consignee that imports through Jeddah pays for a long truck or a rail leg; the same consignee importing through Dammam pays for a shorter truck or a rail slot to Riyadh Dry Port. A western consignee has no real alternative to Jeddah.

Rail matters more than most shippers assume. The Riyadh–Dammam corridor is the backbone, and Riyadh Dry Port exists to exploit it. But rail slots are finite, and the terminal's release process is separate from the seaport's. Ask for a rail booking reference, not a promise.

## The calendar: Ramadan, Eid and the import peak

Saudi import demand has a religious calendar. Ramadan moves about 11 days earlier each year; in 2026 it falls in February and March, with Eid al-Fitr following. The practical effects:

- **Pre-Ramadan stocking.** Food, beverages, household goods and gifts peak in the four to six weeks before Ramadan. Rates and clearance times rise.
- **Ramadan working hours.** Customs, ports and government offices run shorter hours. A file submitted in the last week of Ramadan may not clear until after Eid.
- **Eid al-Adha.** Another peak, tied to the Hajj season, with livestock, food and travel effects.
- **National Day and Founding Day.** Port and government closures can add days in September and February.

Book six to eight weeks before a religious peak. The cheapest rate is not the one that arrives after the shelf life has expired.

## Risk and compliance: the failures that cost the most

**Certification gap.** The most common and most expensive. A container arrives without a valid SCoC and sits while the importer tries to obtain one. Storage, demurrage and lost sales usually exceed the certificate cost by an order of magnitude.

**Valuation query.** An invoice that looks low against comparable goods triggers a review. The cargo sits while the importer produces payment evidence. Under-invoicing to reduce duty is not a saving; it is a penalty risk.

**Wrong importer.** A shipment consigned to an entity without the right activity code or VAT registration cannot be cleared normally. Fix the consignee before the booking, not after arrival.

**Restricted goods.** Some products need a prior import permit — chemicals, medical devices, telecoms, some food additives. A permit obtained after arrival is often too late.

**Label and shelf-life rejection.** Food and cosmetics can be rejected at the border for Arabic labelling or remaining shelf life. The rejection cost includes return freight or destruction.

**Red Sea disruption.** Routing changes add transit time and war-risk surcharges. They also change which port is congested. A route that was optimal last quarter may not be optimal this quarter.

## How to act: a five-step sequence

1. **Classify the product.** Get the HS code and check the SASO technical regulation, SFDA status and any CITC requirement. Do this before you ask for a freight rate.
2. **Confirm the importer of record.** A Saudi entity with a commercial registration, customs account and VAT registration. Agree who pays duty and who recovers VAT.
3. **Build the certificate file.** PCoC before production, SCoC before sailing, Arabic labels approved before printing.
4. **Choose the port by destination.** Jeddah for the west, Dammam for the east and centre, Riyadh Dry Port when the rail slot is confirmed.
5. **Quote the total.** Ocean, origin, destination, duty, VAT, inland and a clearance buffer. Compare totals, not freight rates.

## Our read

Saudi Arabia rewards preparation and punishes improvisation. The market is large enough to justify the compliance work, but the compliance work is not optional. The shippers who succeed are the ones who treat SABER, SFDA and the importer-of-record question as part of product development, not as a customs formality.

For a first shipment, use Jeddah if the consignee is in the west, Dammam if the consignee is in the east or Riyadh, and a broker who files in that port weekly. For a repeat programme, invest in the PCoC and the label artwork once, then reuse them. The cost per shipment falls sharply after the first clean file.

The biggest strategic risk is not freight rates. It is the assumption that a Chinese supplier's "SASO certificate" is the same as a Saudi-registered PCoC. It usually is not.

## FAQ

**How long does sea freight take from China to Saudi Arabia?**
Plan 18–30 days port to port to Jeddah, 20–32 days to Dammam, plus 3–10 days for clearance. Transhipment and Red Sea routing can add a week or more.

**How much is customs duty in Saudi Arabia?**
Most goods are at 5% under the GCC common tariff, some at 0%, and selected categories higher. The HS code decides. VAT is 15% on CIF plus duty.

**Do I need SABER for every shipment?**
Only for products covered by SASO technical regulations. For those, you need a PCoC for the product and an SCoC for each shipment.

**Can a Chinese company be the importer of record in Saudi Arabia?**
Not in the ordinary sense. The importer must be a Saudi entity with a commercial registration and customs account. A foreign seller can sell DDP only through a Saudi importer or a licensed structure.

**Is Dammam better than Jeddah?**
For Riyadh and the Eastern Province, often yes. For western Saudi, no. The Red Sea risk premium and the inland distance decide.

**How much VAT will I pay?**
15% of the customs value plus duty. It is usually recoverable, but it is a cash-flow cost at import.

**What is the biggest cause of clearance delay?**
A missing or mismatched conformity certificate. The second is a valuation query. Both are avoidable with a pre-shipment file review.
